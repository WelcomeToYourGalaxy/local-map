# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective effort begins by finding the exact harm, its driver, and — distinctively here — the alternative that could replace it. Vague concern goes nowhere, and prohibition without an alternative fails; an effort aimed at "this beach mined for sand, this reef smothered, this turtle ground lost, driven by this need, which community governance, this alternative livelihood, the living value of the reef, and the international support can turn around" is a real, workable effort — the kind that has already worked in Mohéli. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What is lost?** This is the first question here. What **beach, reef, mangrove, or turtle ground** is being destroyed — the mined beach, the silted reef, the felled mangrove, the lost nesting site.

**2. What drives the harm, and what could replace it?** What **need** the harm serves — the sand for building, the catch, the materials — and what **alternative** could replace that livelihood, since prohibition without an alternative fails.

**3. How can the community govern it?** How the **community, through its village authority and the park model**, can govern the resource itself, as Mohéli did.

**4. What is the reef worth alive?** How the **reef, turtles, and beach are worth more alive** — as fisheries and as the draw for visitors — than destroyed.

**5. What international support can back it?** How the **international conservation and restoration partners** can fund and support the alternative and the conservation.

Some honest truths hold across every effort here. **The community is both the source of the harm and, as Mohéli shows, the source of the cure.** **Alternatives are essential — prohibition without a livelihood fails where people are poor.** **The reefs and turtles are worth more alive, and made to pay, they sustain their own protection.** **But the harm is poverty-driven, the state is fragile, and the pressures are structural.** Hold both truths at once: a real, proven, community-led cure, and the honest weight of poverty and fragility.

Above all, keep the discipline — and the hope — that give these efforts their force. Document the harm and its driver. Build community governance with real alternatives. Make the reefs pay alive. Secure the international support. And offer a better way, not prohibition — because in a poor island archipelago where the harm comes from the community's own need, an effort that turns that need from harm into cure, offering a better livelihood as Mohéli did, is the one that is both effective and just.

The Comoros is a poor and fragile archipelago whose own communities, in their need, mine the sand from their beaches, dynamite their reefs, and fell their mangroves, eroding the very shores their children and their turtles depend on; this guide has hidden none of that, nor pretended that poverty can be wished away, or that a fragile state can enforce what it cannot fund, or that one island's success is yet the whole country's. But the Comoros holds what few countries in this series can offer: a real and hopeful proof that it can be done, for on the island of Mohéli the communities built one of the Indian Ocean's pioneering community-managed marine parks, brought their green turtles back to beaches they once mined, and found in their living reefs a better living than the dead ones gave — and that model is spreading. There is no distant enemy to defeat here, only a community's own need to turn from harm into cure; and the levers to do it — community self-governance, better livelihoods, the living value of the reefs, and the international support behind them — are real, and have already worked. The work is patient and it must always offer a better way, not prohibition alone; this guide has said so throughout. But the beaches and reefs and turtles of the Comoros are worth defending, the cure runs through the community's own hands, and the proof that a poor island community can save its own environment is already written on the shores of Mohéli. So can you write it on yours.

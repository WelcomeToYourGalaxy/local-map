# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN CONFRONT ENVIRONMENTAL HARM AND DESTRUCTIVE PROJECTS IN MARTINIQUE

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Martinique's environmental reality begins with a **poison in the soil**. **Chlordécone** — a banana-plantation pesticide — was used here for **two decades after its dangers were known**, and it has **contaminated the land for generations**, tainted the rivers and the sea, restricted the fisheries, and left its trace in the **blood of almost the entire population**. It is a persistent poison that will outlast everyone now living. Against it, Martinicans have built a long, painful, and still-unfinished struggle for recognition, protection, and accountability — a powerful civic movement, affected families, scientists, and their legal allies pressing through the French and European systems. That struggle has won real things: **health recognitions** (including the acknowledgment of chlordécone-linked illness as an occupational disease), decontamination science, food-safety monitoring, and a national reckoning that a poisoning had occurred. But it has also met the hard wall of a contamination that cannot be removed and a criminal accountability that, after twenty years, the courts **denied**. This is the ground Martinicans stand on — and, this guide will show, it is ground on which real, if partial, victories can be won.

The Martinican movement against the chlordécone legacy — affected families, scientists, and their legal allies — draws on a toolkit strengthened by the French and European frameworks, in a rule-of-law European territory. **Health recognition and compensation** channels exist for those harmed, including the acknowledgment of chlordécone-linked illness as an occupational disease. **Food-safety standards and monitoring** can be invoked to force testing and limits on what reaches the market. The **French and European courts**, the **EU frameworks**, and **human-rights channels** are genuinely powerful. A new project needs an **EIA and public inquiry (enquête publique)**, open to challenge. And a **powerful civic movement**, a free press, and democratic protest carry it all. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The **contamination is effectively permanent** — it will outlast generations, and the poison cannot be removed from the soil. The **criminal accountability has, after twenty years, been denied** in the courts — the case dismissed, acknowledgment without full justice. The **banana monoculture endures** and the economy depends on it. The **sargassum tides** return each year to foul the coasts. And a **colonial history and an economic dependency** run through the whole struggle, shaping who was poisoned, who profited, and whose justice was deferred. So this guide does not pretend the poison can be removed, or that the courts have delivered criminal justice; what it shows is how, through these genuinely powerful channels, Martinicans can confront the legacy, protect health and food, and defend the land and sea — winning the real, partial victories that recognition, protection, and vigilance make possible.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

A permanent poison, and the projects and pressures that compound it, look like harms too vast to fight — but each has a channel that reaches it. The chlordécone legacy is confronted through **health recognition, decontamination science, and the legal and EU channels**; the land and food through **agroecology and food-safety monitoring**; the sea and sargassum through **management and the law**; a new destructive project through the **EIA, the public inquiry, and the courts**. Every one of those is a lever you can pull. **Find the one closest to your hand and pull.**

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO'S CAPTURED

Martinique's chlordécone catastrophe was itself a failure of a state and industry that used a poison after its dangers were known, so this matters.

**How to spot it.** A harm known and permitted anyway; recognition or protection withheld; a new project's EIA written for the proponent; the interests of the monoculture placed above health.

**Who's supposed to guard against it.** French **audit and public-health bodies**, the **French and European courts**, **EU frameworks**, and **human-rights bodies** provide checks. And Martinique's **civic movement, scientists, and press** have forced the reckoning and exposed the failures.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — the harm known and permitted, who profited, what the law required — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect. Route serious evidence to the audit and public-health bodies, the courts, the EU and human-rights channels, and trusted journalists rather than making accusations you can't back. Pair the exposure with the recognition and protection channels.

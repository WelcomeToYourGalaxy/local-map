# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN SAUDI ARABIA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Concern for the environment in Saudi Arabia turns on a distinctive tension: this is a wealthy, closed kingdom, the world's largest oil exporter, undertaking some of the largest development projects the world has ever seen — vast flagship "**giga-projects**" marketed as models of sustainability — even as it pledges to plant ten billion trees and reach net zero, and even as those projects reshape deserts and coasts and, in at least one case, displaced the people who lived there, whose resisters met severe punishment. That tension — between the flagship developments and their green branding, between the oil economy and the green pledges, between the image projected to the world and the reality on the ground — is the distinctive ground of environmental effort here, because it is precisely the state's image-consciousness, its own stated green commitments, and the international attention its projects invite that offer the levers, in a country where open activism is dangerous. Saudi Arabia is a large, arid, and closed absolute monarchy of vast desert and long Red Sea coast, whose economy rests on oil, whose water comes overwhelmingly from the world's largest desalination effort, and whose Vision 2030 is remaking the country through enormous state-flagship projects. The levers here rest on the state's own commitments and its image-consciousness, and, used with care, are real.

In a closed, repressive kingdom where open activism is dangerous, the levers rest on the state's own image, its green commitments, and the international attention its projects invite — used with great care, strictly environmentally, and protecting anyone inside. The distinctive lever is the **giga-projects' green branding held to its own promise**: the flagship developments are marketed to the world as green, and can be scrutinised and held to that claim through the international attention they invite. The **Saudi Green Initiative and the net-zero pledge** set commitments the state can be held to, on its own terms. The **oil-superpower's footprint** invites international climate scrutiny. The **globally significant Red Sea coral** is a treasure the state itself claims to protect, defensible through marine protection and its international significance. The **water-security argument** is powerful: in a nation almost without fresh water, wholly dependent on the world's largest desalination effort — itself a marine harm — a project that strains water is self-defeating. And major projects have a formal **environmental assessment**. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The giga-projects are top-down and state-driven, so they cannot be openly opposed but only scrutinised and held to their own claims. Oil is the economy's foundation, so the footprint is central. Desalination is essential to a nation almost without fresh water. The green initiative is real but set against a vast footprint. And the political space is closed and repressive: those who resisted a flagship project's displacement met severe punishment, so the work must run through international, technical, and protective channels, be kept strictly environmental, and shield anyone inside absolutely — never framed as political. And progress is partial, not total. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a flagship project scrutinised and held to its own green claims; the state held to its net-zero and green pledges; the Red Sea coral and the water defended; the international climate attention engaged — through the reputational, international, scientific, and technical channels, with great care, and protecting everyone.

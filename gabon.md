## The Strategic Framework

Effective efforts follow the same underlying pattern, whether the goal is to defend a forest, a park, a river, or a community's land from logging, plantations, or mining:

```
STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION  (find the project, the harm, and the promises)
        |
STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION          (build the evidence -- harm and broken promise)
        |
STEP 3: LOCAL OPPOSITION        (build the community and its land rights, carefully)
        |
STEP 4: LEGAL, REGULATORY & CLIMATE-FINANCE  (forest law, parks, climate framework)
        |
STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY         (make it a national and global forest cause)
```

These steps operate **simultaneously, not in sequence**. The evidence of harm and broken promise feeds the climate-finance partners, the forest authorities, the certifiers, and the world at once; the community organises around its land rights, with care; a report to a climate-finance donor, a certification challenge, or a land-rights claim is both pressure and a spur to the next. The community that runs all five together, each reinforcing the others, gives itself the best — if still constrained — chance of defending the forest and its people.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective effort begins by finding the exact project and harm — and the leverage the community holds in a paid-to-conserve country. Vague concern goes nowhere, and confrontation a semi-authoritarian state resists; an effort aimed at "the palm plantation clearing this forest and community land, breaching Gabon's green promise and the certification, which the climate-finance donors and the certifiers could press and which the community's land rights could defend" is a real, workable effort — the kind Gabon's green-bargain levers can deliver. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What would be lost?** This is the first question here. What **forest, wildlife, and community land** would the project destroy — the rainforest, the elephants and apes, the villages' and forest peoples' land.

**2. How does it betray the green promise?** How does the project **breach Gabon's green commitments and the climate-finance conditions** the country is paid under — the distinctive reputational and financial lever.

**3. Which company and certification are involved?** Which **company** — logging, palm, mining — and which **certification** it must meet, since a company breaching certification or its commitments can be pressed.

**4. How do the parks, the law, and land rights apply?** How the **national parks, the forest law, and the community's land rights** can defend the forest.

**5. How can the community organise safely?** Given the authoritarian legacy and uncertain transition, **how can the community organise and act safely**, favouring the legal, the certified, the funded, and the land-rights arenas over risky confrontation.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on — and here, where the case must persuade climate-finance donors, certifiers, forest authorities, and the world, sound evidence of both the harm and the broken green promise is decisive. A donor, a certifier, a forest official, and a journalist all move on evidence — and in Gabon, a clear record of the forest and land lost, and of how it betrays the green bargain, is precisely what an effort turns into a real result, so build it well. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Project, the Company, and the Promise

Get the facts the case turns on. Identify the **project, its operator, the concession or permit, and the forest and community land it would take**; and, crucially here, establish how it **breaches Gabon's green commitments, the climate-finance conditions, and the certification** the country and the company are bound by. Establish the deciders, the company, and the broken promise precisely, because in Gabon the distinctive case is not merely that a project does harm, but that it betrays the green bargain a country is paid and praised for.

## Layer 2: The Loss — Forest, Wildlife, and Land

Document what would be lost. Establish the **environmental harm** — the rainforest cleared, the forest elephants and apes and biodiversity lost, the carbon released, the rivers harmed — drawing on **satellite monitoring, conservation studies, and forest data**, much of which exists for a forest as watched and measured as Gabon's. And establish the **community harm** — the land and forest taken from villages and forest peoples, the livelihoods and tenure lost. Photographs, satellite imagery, and field documentation make the loss undeniable — and the breach of the green promise makes it a scandal.

## Layer 3: The Loss, the Broken Promise, the Land Rights, and the Channel

Finally, document the case. Does the **environmental and community harm** show a real loss? How does it **breach the green commitments, the climate-finance conditions, and the certification**? Each is a separate element, and together they make a case that can hold a paid-to-conserve state to its bargain and secure the community's land. This is the layer that most often decides these efforts, because a documented loss, a broken green promise, a land-rights claim, and an open channel are exactly what let a Gabonese community defend the forest and its people. Build it well, and the climate finance, the green commitments, the certification, and land rights become the community's genuine strength. One practical note on building this layer: the broken green promise is the element that most distinguishes a Gabonese case and most repays careful work, because it is what turns an ordinary forest loss into a scandal the world will notice. A cleared forest in a country nobody watches is a tragedy easily ignored; the same clearing in a country the world pays and praises as a green conservation leader is a betrayal of a public bargain, and that betrayal is precisely what gives a community leverage over actors — donors, certifiers, the global public — far more powerful than itself. So the file should establish, as precisely as possible, exactly what green commitments Gabon has made, exactly what conditions attach to the climate money it receives, and exactly how the project in question breaches them. It is this documented gap between the celebrated promise and the destructive reality, more than the raw fact of the harm, that engages the world's attention and the paying partners' obligations, and it is worth the effort to establish it as rigorously as the evidence allows.

Some honest truths hold across every effort here. **Community land rights are the just and durable foundation, the surest defence of a forest by the people who live in it.** **The climate finance and green commitments are the distinctive levers, holding a paid-and-praised state to its bargain.** **The certification has halted clearing before, and the parks and law can defend.** **But the state is authoritarian in legacy, the transition uncertain, and the money must be made to reach the people.** Hold both truths at once: real, distinctive, world-spanning levers, and the honest weight of an authoritarian legacy, an uncertain transition, and misdirected money.

Above all, keep the discipline — and the care — that give these efforts their force. Document the harm and the broken green promise. Invoke the climate finance, the green commitments, the parks, and the law. Press the companies and certifiers. Secure community land rights and redirect the money toward them. And act with care where the legacy is authoritarian and the transition uncertain — because in a country paid and praised for its forest, an effort that holds it to that promise while securing the rights of the forest's own people is the one that is both effective and just.

Gabon still holds one of the last great rainforests on earth — nearly nine-tenths of the country under Congo Basin forest, absorbing more carbon than the nation emits, home to forest elephants and the peoples who have always lived among the trees — and the world pays and praises Gabon for keeping it; this guide has hidden neither that hopeful fact nor the harder ones, that illegal logging is rising, that palm and mining and oil press against the forest, that the money too rarely reaches the people, and that the politics are uncertain, and there is no landmark community victory here to promise you. But the very thing that makes Gabon celebrated — that it is paid to keep its forest standing and stakes its name on being green — is the lever: the world that pays can be asked to condition its money, the promise that is praised can be held, the certifiers can halt the clearing as they have before, and the people who live in the forest can secure the land rights that are its surest defence. There is no easy victory, and no landmark precedent to inherit; this guide has said so throughout, and counselled care. But your forest, your wildlife, your rivers, and your land are worth defending, the green bargain the world rewards is a real lever, and the patient work of holding that bargain, pressing the companies, and securing your land is one your community can genuinely do — and defend both the forest and the people who are its truest guardians. So can you.

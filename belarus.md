# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN BELARUS

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Belarus is a closed and severely repressive state — one where civil society was crushed after 2020, hundreds of thousands were forced into exile, and thousands were imprisoned, and where open environmental organising is dangerous and public oversight extremely limited. It is a heavily forested Eastern European nation carrying two heavy burdens and holding one great treasure. It bore the brunt of the 1986 **Chernobyl** disaster more than any other country: most of the fallout fell here, a tenth of the land and of the people remain in affected territories, and a vast contaminated zone in the south, emptied of its people, has become a living laboratory of radiation. It carries a heavy **state-run logging industry**, extensive drained **peatlands** that smoulder and emit carbon, and a new **Russian-built nuclear plant** raised, against all irony, in the very country Chernobyl harmed most. And it holds, in **Belovezhskaya Pushcha**, the last great primeval forest of the European plain — a shared treasure straddling the border with Poland, where the European bison, once hunted to extinction in the wild, has been painstakingly brought back. No one inside can safely resist. But the world has a genuine stake in these things, and the tools to defend them, from outside, are real.

Those who would defend Belarus's environment cannot do so safely inside, but the tools from outside are real. Belovezhskaya Pushcha is a **World Heritage Site** shared with Poland, defensible through **UNESCO and transboundary conservation**. The state logging industry's timber can be scrutinised and contested under **international timber-legality and sustainability standards** — the certification and import rules that markets and buyers abroad enforce. The Russian-built nuclear plant touches **international and regional nuclear-safety frameworks** — the IAEA, the stress tests, the concern of neighbouring states. The drained, smouldering peatlands can be pressed for protection through the **international wetland and climate frameworks** — Ramsar and the climate conventions. The world's **scientific attention** to the Chernobyl legacy and the forest grounds it all, and **rigorous documentation** — satellite imagery, published science — needs no one inside. And the **exile and diaspora networks** carry the case where those within cannot speak. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Belarus is closed and severely repressive: civil society was crushed, hundreds of thousands driven into exile, thousands imprisoned, and open environmental work inside is dangerous. The Chernobyl contamination is vast and largely permanent — managed and studied, not reversed. The primeval forest is pressed by logging and a militarised border, and cooperation across that border is strained. And, above all, no one inside can be exposed. So the work must be external, scientific, transboundary, and diaspora-borne, strictly environmental, protecting anyone inside absolutely — and the honest prize is the international defence of a world-treasured forest, the scrutiny of the logging and the nuclear, and the pressing of the peatlands' protection, not a project stopped from within.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot safely resist inside a state that jails and exiles those who dissent, and you must never ask anyone to. What can be done is to defend Belarus's environment from outside: document the harm, invoke the World Heritage and transboundary protection of the forest, scrutinise the logging against international standards and the nuclear against the safety frameworks, press the peatlands' protection through the wetland and climate conventions, and let exile and international voices and the world's science carry it — while protecting everyone inside absolutely.

## Be Honest About Which Fight You're In

Belarus is one of the most closed and dangerous settings in this guide, and honesty about that is the first and gravest discipline. This is not a fight you win by domestic organising — that has been crushed. # QUICK REFERENCE: SUCCESS RATES

No Belarusian effort yields a domestic "win," and honesty requires saying so — civil society is crushed and the harms are state-driven. # STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Ask five questions. **What is the harm?** — the logging of the primeval forest, the peatland drainage and smouldering, the nuclear-safety risk, the Chernobyl legacy. **What international status or standard does it touch?** — World Heritage, timber legality, nuclear safety, wetland or climate convention. **Who outside shares the interest?** — Poland and the neighbouring states, the conservation and scientific bodies, the timber markets, the diaspora. **Which external channel reaches it?** — UNESCO, a certifier, a safety framework, a convention. **How is everyone inside kept safe?** — the overriding question, answered by keeping all work external and exposing no one. Pick the harm where the international handle is clearest and safest.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Build the case in layers, all from open, external sources. **Layer one: the harm** — the logging documented by satellite, the peatland drainage and fires, the nuclear-safety concerns, the forest's condition. **Layer two: the status and the standard** — the World Heritage designation, the timber-legality and sustainability rules, the nuclear-safety frameworks, the wetland and climate conventions the harm offends. **Layer three: the channel and the protection** — which external body to reach, and the absolute rule that no person inside is named, exposed, or endangered. A case built on satellite imagery and published science — needing no one inside — is one no closed state can suppress.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

Gather the **harm record**: satellite imagery of the logging and peatlands, the published science on the forest and the Chernobyl zone, the nuclear-safety analyses. Gather the **status-and-standard record**: the World Heritage designation, the timber-legality and certification rules, the IAEA and regional safety frameworks, the Ramsar and climate conventions. Gather the **channel record**: the conservation and scientific bodies, the timber markets and certifiers, the neighbouring states, the diaspora. Gather the **safety discipline**: confirm every source is external and open, and that nothing traces to a person inside.

# STEP 3: BUILDING THE INTERNATIONAL AND DIASPORA COALITION

The coalition here is external, because it must be. Build it from the **exile and diaspora networks**, who can speak where those inside cannot; the **international conservation and scientific bodies** engaged with the forest and Chernobyl; the **neighbouring states — Poland above all — and their conservation and nuclear-safety concerns**; and the **timber-market, certification, and climate campaigners** who can reach the standards. Build carefully, protect every source, and never in a way that draws in or exposes anyone inside the country.

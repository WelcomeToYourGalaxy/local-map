# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN OMAN

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community effort in Oman has a distinctive character, because Oman is a stable, consultative Sultanate where the ultimate power rests with the Sultan and the Royal Court, and there is a sobering precedent this guide places at its center. In 2007, Oman's **Arabian Oryx Sanctuary** — a vast desert reserve that had been one of the world's great conservation successes, home to the first free-ranging herd of Arabian oryx anywhere on earth since the species was hunted to extinction in the wild in 1972 — became the first site ever to be removed from the UNESCO World Heritage List, after the Sultanate reduced the protected area by a full ninety percent, by royal decree, to allow oil and gas exploration; the oryx population fell from four hundred and fifty in 1996 to just sixty-five. This is the honest starting point for any effort in Oman: the state, when it chooses, can and will override even its own celebrated conservation and its international heritage status for oil, and it does so from the top, with little domestic voice. This guide will not pretend that a community can easily overturn such a decision; it cannot. But the levers, though limited, are real.

A community in Oman works not through protest or confrontation, which have little place here, but respectfully within the system, appealing to its better commitments — and the tools, though limited, are real. The **consultative councils** — the elected Majlis al-Shura and the municipal councils — are real, if advisory, channels to raise concerns and influence policy. The **traditional governance** — the wali system and the tribal sheikhs — are respected intermediaries with standing to carry a community's concerns upward. Major projects must reckon with **environmental impact assessment and Oman's environmental law**, a formal gate. The **heritage-and-values appeal** — the Sultanate's own celebrated commitment to conservation, heritage, and its national vision — is a lever a community can invoke, though, as the oryx shows, with honest limits. And the **international and UNESCO attention** the image-conscious Sultanate cares about is a real reputational channel. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The state decides from the top: the oryx sanctuary was cut by royal decree despite Oman's conservation reputation and its World Heritage status, because oil was found beneath it — a plain demonstration that the appeal to the state's own commitments, real as they are, can be overridden when the stakes are high enough. Oman has no political parties, little independent civil society, and no culture of public protest; the consultative councils advise but do not command; and the ultimate power over every such decision is the Sultan's alone. So the work must be respectful, within the system, consultative, and appealing to the state's own values and image — never confrontational — and the honest prize is a harmful project raised through the councils and the traditional channels, assessed, reconsidered, or improved, and the state's own heritage and image invoked, not a decision overturned from below. In Oman, one works respectfully within the system and appeals to its better commitments.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot overturn a decision from below in a Sultanate where the ultimate power is the Sultan's, and you do not confront, for confrontation has no place here. What you can do is work respectfully within the system and appeal to its better commitments: raise the concern through the consultative councils and the traditional wali and tribal channels; hold the project to its environmental assessment; invoke the Sultanate's own celebrated commitment to conservation and heritage; and engage the international and UNESCO attention an image-conscious state cares about — all respectfully, and with honest expectations.

## Be Honest About Which Fight You're In

Oman is a stable, top-down Sultanate, and honesty about that is the first discipline — the oryx sanctuary makes it plain. The state decides from the top and will override even its own conservation for oil; there are no political parties, little civil society, and no protest culture; the councils advise but do not command; and the ultimate power is the Sultan's. So the realistic aim is not to overturn a decision from below but to raise a harmful project respectfully through the councils and traditional channels, to have it assessed, reconsidered, or improved, and to invoke the state's own heritage and image — working within the system, and with honest expectations.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

Ultimate power in Oman rests with the **Sultan and the Royal Court**, who decide the great questions from the top. Below them, the **ministries and the environment authority** administer the assessments, permits, and environmental law. The **Majlis al-Shura and the municipal councils** advise. The **walis and tribal sheikhs** carry community concerns upward through the traditional channels. And the **UNESCO and international bodies** hold the reputational attention the Sultanate values. Map who decides, and through which respectful channel a concern can reach them, before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A major project moves through the ministries and the environmental assessment, and — for the great decisions — the Royal Court from the top. You enter respectfully: through the consultative councils and the traditional wali and tribal channels, which can carry a concern upward; through the environmental assessment; through the appeal to the Sultanate's own heritage and values; and through the international and UNESCO attention. Enter within the system, respectfully, and with honest expectations of what the councils can and cannot command.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the decision: whether a project is a ministry matter or a great question decided from the top, as the oryx sanctuary was. Follow the assessment: whether the environmental study and law were properly met. Follow the values: which of the Sultanate's own conservation and heritage commitments the project strains. Follow the image: whether the international and UNESCO attention the Sultanate cares about is engaged. The leverage lies in the consultative and traditional channels, the assessment, the heritage appeal, and the international attention.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

The watchers here are the ministries and environment authority, the advisory Majlis al-Shura and municipal councils, the traditional walis and tribal sheikhs, and — internationally — UNESCO and the bodies whose attention the Sultanate values. The honest caution, written by the oryx, is that all of these can be overridden from the top; so a case engages them respectfully, appealing to the state's better commitments and its image.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Ask five questions. **What is the harm?** — the project threatening the desert, coast, water, or a protected place. **Who decides it?** — a ministry, or the top, as with the oryx. **What does it need?** — an environmental assessment under Oman's law. **Which value does it strain?** — the Sultanate's own conservation, heritage, or national vision. **Which respectful channel reaches it?** — the councils, the wali, the tribal sheikhs, the international attention. Pick the fight where the consultative and traditional channels and the heritage appeal give the clearest, most respectful handle.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

Build the case in layers, respectfully. **Layer one: the harm** — the desert, coast, water, or protected place at stake, documented with the science. **Layer two: the assessment and the value** — the environmental assessment and law, and the Sultanate's own conservation, heritage, and national-vision commitments the project strains. **Layer three: the channel and the image** — the consultative councils, walis, and tribal sheikhs to engage, and the international and UNESCO attention to invoke. A case grounded in the assessment and the state's own values, raised respectfully, is one the system can receive — with the honest limit that the top may still decide otherwise.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

Gather the **harm record**: the project's plans and footprint, the desert, coast, water, or protected place at stake, the science. Gather the **assessment record**: the environmental assessment and Oman's environmental law. Gather the **values record**: the Sultanate's own conservation, heritage, and national-vision commitments. Gather the **channel record**: the Majlis al-Shura and municipal councils, the walis and tribal sheikhs, the international and UNESCO bodies. Much of the values and heritage record is public and can be respectfully invoked.

# STEP 3: BUILDING SUPPORT THROUGH THE CONSULTATIVE AND TRADITIONAL CHANNELS

In a Sultanate with no protest culture, the effort is consultative and traditional, not confrontational. Build with the **affected community**, whose concern must be carried respectfully; the **Majlis al-Shura and municipal council members** who can raise it; the **walis and tribal sheikhs**, respected intermediaries with real standing; and, where the state's image is at stake, the **international and UNESCO bodies**. Frame everything respectfully, within the system, around the shared environment and the Sultanate's own values — never as opposition or confrontation, which have no place and would only fail.

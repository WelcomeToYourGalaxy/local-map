# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN NAURU

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

The story of Nauru carries a warning unlike any other, because this tiny island has already been devoured once by mining, and now stands at the centre of a global fight over whether to do the same thing to the deep sea. Nauru is one of the smallest nations on earth, a single raised coral island in the vast western Pacific, and for the better part of a century it was strip-mined for phosphate — the fossilised residue of countless ancient seabirds — until roughly four-fifths of the whole island, the interior known as **Topside**, was reduced to a barren moonscape of jagged limestone pinnacles, wholly uninhabitable, with the island's remaining people crowded onto the thin coastal ring that is the only living land left to them. The phosphate made Nauru, for a brief and giddy moment, one of the richest places per person on earth; then it ran out, the accumulated wealth was squandered and lost, and the nation was left poor, deeply scarred, and searching desperately for revenue. Now, its old income dwindling, Nauru has become the sponsoring state for a company seeking to mine the deep-sea floor of the Pacific — and has, through a legal manoeuvre, forced the world's seabed authority toward opening that frontier before the rules or the science are anywhere near ready, in a move many see as the island preparing to repeat underwater the very catastrophe that ruined its land. The levers here are real.

The fight over Nauru's deep-sea mining is, distinctively, a global one — for the seabed lies in international waters, and Nauru's own government is driving the project — and the tools are largely international, grounded in a lived warning. The most powerful lever is the **memory of phosphate**: Nauru's own ruin by extraction is the strongest argument against repeating it on the sea, a lived, moral warning the world can see. The **world's seabed governance** — the international seabed authority and the "common heritage of mankind" principle — governs the deep-sea frontier, and the premature push can be resisted there; protest, petition, and advocacy are lawful. A **growing moratorium coalition of nations and the precautionary principle** press for a pause until the science and the rules are ready. The **market refusal** — the buyers, from carmakers to tech companies, who pledge not to purchase seabed metals — undercuts the project's economics. And the **deep-sea science** documenting the harm grounds it all. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Nauru is small and poor and genuinely needs revenue, so the resistance must not merely refuse but help find a better path — a poor nation searching for income cannot simply be told no. Nauru's own government is driving the project, so the domestic democratic levers are limited. The seabed lies in international waters, beyond any one community. These levers all bear on the one great fight the nation's own government has chosen; and though the seabed is the defining struggle, a community facing any other harm at home retains the ordinary tools of a small Pacific democracy — environmental scrutiny, the elected government, and the courts. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — the premature push resisted at the seabed authority, a precautionary pause won through the coalition, the market kept from the metal, the world warned by Nauru's own ruin — and offer the better path: healing Nauru's ruined land and finding revenue that does not repeat the catastrophe.

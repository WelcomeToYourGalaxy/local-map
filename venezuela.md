# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN VENEZUELA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Across Venezuela, and above all south of the Orinoco, communities discover that the forest they live in, the river they drink from, and the land their people have held for generations has been opened to mining — by decree, by an armed group, or by both at once: a gold or coltan pit, a dredge tearing up a riverbed, a militarised "mining zone," an airstrip and a camp where a forest used to be. The first sign is often the mercury in the fish, the malaria, the outsiders with weapons, or the discovery that your territory sits inside a "strategic development zone" no one ever asked you about. The hardest truths must come first. Venezuela's institutions have collapsed as protectors of the environment and of rights: the courts are not independent, the ministries promote extraction, and domestic remedies, real on paper, rarely function against a project the state and its allied armed groups want. Much of the destruction is driven not by an ordinary company but by armed actors — Venezuelan *sindicatos*, Colombian guerrilla groups, and their entanglement with parts of the military — so confronting them directly is not opposition but mortal danger. And the danger to those who document and speak is severe. And yet there are truths on the other side: the Orinoco Mining Arc was created **without the environmental-impact study the Constitution requires (Article 129)** and **without the free, prior, and informed consultation of Indigenous peoples it requires (Article 120)**, and the National Assembly formally rejected it — so by Venezuela's own supreme law, the project is illegal. That illegality, unenforceable at home, is a powerful instrument abroad. This guide does not promise victory. But the levers, mostly international, are real.

A community in Venezuela works, of necessity, mostly from outside and with the greatest care, for much of the harm is enforced by armed men — and the tools, though narrow, are real.

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Around the turn of the millennium, developers proposed to bulldoze the coast at **Clifton** — a strip of scrub forest, wetlands, reef, and cliffs on the western tip of New Providence, near Nassau — for a gated complex of some six hundred homes and an eighteen-hole golf course. But Clifton was not empty land. It held the only place in the Bahamas where the memory of all three of the islands' peoples survives on a single site: the village of the **Lucayans**, the first Bahamians, who met Columbus and were gone within a generation; the ruins of an eighteenth-century **plantation**; and the quarters of the **enslaved Africans** who worked it. Bahamians from every walk of life rose to defend it. Environmentalists, historians, artists, and ordinary citizens joined a grassroots **Fight to Save Clifton** — and they won: in 2004 an **act of Parliament** set the land aside as the **Clifton Heritage National Park**, and the bulldozers never came. A coast of ancestors was saved from becoming a private enclave, and made permanent by law.

The Fight to Save Clifton drew on tools available to communities across the Bahamas. A major project needs an **EIA and environmental authorisation**. **Heritage and national-park designation**, backed by **protective legislation**, can lock a place away permanently — as an act of Parliament did for Clifton. The **courts** are open; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. And the most powerful lever here was the **coast as a commons** — the claim that a shore belongs to the nation, not a private enclave — carried by an appeal to the country's shared inheritance, natural, historical, and ancestral, that united people who agreed on little else. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The Bahamas lives on **tourism and coastal development**, so the pressure to build resorts, marinas, and gated enclaves on every beautiful shore is **structural and relentless**, and Clifton was one battle in a long war — later fights, on other coasts and cays, have been harder and some have been **lost or ground down**. The Clifton win required not just protest but the **political will** to set the land aside, which had to be won. And it was achieved in a **functioning democracy** with a free press and open civic space. So draw courage from the victory without mistaking it for a guarantee: find the shared heritage, make the coast the nation's own, and secure it in law — and know that not every fine coast can be saved.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

A big project looks unstoppable, but it's a chain of approvals, and a chain breaks at its weakest link. A Bahamian coastal project needs its **EIA and authorisation**; it can be challenged in the **courts**; and, above all, a place of **shared heritage** can be turned into a national cause and locked in by **protective legislation**. Every one of those is a link you can grab — and the shared heritage, secured in law, is the one that made Clifton permanent. **Find the one closest to your hand and pull.**

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

To break the chain you have to see it — who decides, how a project moves, and who's watching.

## Who Actually Decides

A major project needs an **EIA and environmental authorisation** from the environment authority, and planning and Crown-land approvals from the government. **Heritage and national-park designation**, and **protective legislation**, can permanently set land aside. The **courts** review the legality of decisions. And in a small democracy, the **government and Parliament** are sensitive to the nation's attention and to a unifying heritage cause. Know which decision — or which protective law — you can reach, and aim there.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

**The EIA and authorisation.** The project is assessed and authorised. *Your opening:* take part, put your objections on the record, and attack the assessment's flaws.

**The heritage and the coast.** *Your opening — the distinctive one:* establish the site's **shared heritage** and its status as a **public coast**, and make it the nation's cause.

**The protective law.** *Your opening — the durable one:* press for **heritage/national-park designation and protective legislation** to lock the place in permanently, as Clifton was.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Dig up three things. **Who's behind it and who owns them** — the developer, and any foreign capital, whose reputation you can reach. **What heritage and coast it threatens** — because shared heritage and a public shore are your unifying levers. **What it still needs** — the authorisation, the Crown-land grant, the political go-ahead: whatever's missing is the soft spot.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

Each is an ally or an arena. The **environment authority** runs the EIA and authorisation. **Parliament** can pass protective legislation and set land aside. The **courts** review decisions. And the Bahamas' **environmental, heritage, and cultural organisations, historians, artists, and a free press** are the forces that reach furthest — the diverse coalition that saved Clifton.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "the project" is weak. Being specific — this heritage, this public coast, this defective authorisation — is strong. Answer five questions in writing.

**1. What is it, and who's behind it?** Name the project, the developer, and any foreign capital.

**2. Where is it, and what does it touch?** Map it over the **coast, reef, mangrove, wetlands — and the natural, historical, and ancestral heritage** of the site. **Shared heritage and a public coast are your sharpest, most unifying levers** — Clifton held the memory of all three of the nation's peoples.

**3. What stage is it at, and what's the next deadline?** Is the EIA in process? Has the authorisation issued (opening the courts)? Is there a window to press for protective legislation? These openings are time-bound.

**4. What does it still need that it lacks?** The authorisation, the Crown-land grant, the political go-ahead. The missing piece is the soft spot.

**5. Can you unite a diverse nation?** Environmentalists, historians, artists, cultural and church groups, ordinary citizens. Clifton's power was a coalition that agreed on little else but this.

**What this looks like in real life.** A gated resort or marina threatens a public coast that holds natural and ancestral heritage. The five questions yield: a developer and its capital; a heritage coast and a public shore at stake (the unifying levers); an EIA or authorisation in process; a project needing the go-ahead; and a diverse nation to unite. That's the Clifton pattern — establish the shared heritage, make the coast the nation's own, and press for protective law.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

The difference between a community that can fight and one that can't is proof — and at Clifton the decisive proof was the heritage. Build it in three layers.

**Layer one: the heritage.** Establish and document the site's **shared heritage** — the natural (reef, mangrove, wetlands, wildlife), the historical (ruins, sites), and the ancestral (Indigenous, colonial, enslaved-African). At Clifton, archaeologists showed what the ground held; get the science and the history that make the case irrefutable.

**Layer two: the paper trail and the harm.** Get the EIA, the authorisation, the Crown-land and planning records, and the developer's plans, and document the harm — the privatization of the coast, the destruction of the heritage and the reef. Read the EIA for flaws.

**Layer three: the map of heritage and harm.** Lay the project over the heritage sites, the reef and mangrove, and the public shore, so the loss — natural, historical, and ancestral — is undeniable. Walking into the authority, the courts, Parliament, or the nation with the heritage documented and that map is what makes them act.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

- **The heritage evidence** — natural, historical (archaeology), and ancestral — the irrefutable core.
- **The EIA, the authorisation, the Crown-land and planning records, the developer's plans** — from the environment authority and the government.
- **Ownership and financing** — the developer and any foreign capital.
- **The heritage-and-harm map** — the heritage sites, the reef and mangrove, and the public shore, laid over the project.
- **Named testimony** — of those who value the coast and its heritage.

**Build to last, and aim for protective law.** Bahamian fights run against a relentless tide. Build clear roles, a secure archive, a way to decide together, a plan for the toll — and, above all, aim for the durable win: **heritage/national-park designation and protective legislation**, which locks the place in as Clifton was.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY

In the Bahamas, the nation's attention is a decisive lever — a small country can be united around its heritage, as Clifton united it. A free press and heritage and cultural organisations make the media front central.

**Lead with the ancestors and the coast, not procedure.** Not "the EIA was deficient" but "the one place where our ancestors — the first Bahamians and the enslaved Africans — are remembered, bulldozed for a private enclave." Use your strongest material: the heritage and archaeology, dated photographs, the map, and the voices of the nation.

**Work several audiences at once.** Local and national press unite the nation; cultural, historical, and diaspora networks amplify the ancestral heritage; the commons claim resonates widely. Make it easy: captioned photos, accurate summaries, a map, strong interviews.

**Two hard rules.** Accuracy above all — one exaggeration can discredit everything. And let the diverse coalition — environmental, historical, cultural — shape the story together, keeping the frame on the shared heritage.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Make them your own; keep them short, exact and calm; keep a copy of everything.

**8A — Information request (to the environment authority).**
*Subject: Request for the EIA and authorisation — [project].*
"We, citizens of [place], request the EIA, the environmental authorisation, and the Crown-land and planning records for [project] at [location]. [Contact.]"

**8B — Objection in the EIA.**
*Subject: Objection — [project].*
"We, citizens of [place], object to [project]: [it privatizes a public coast; it destroys natural, historical, and ancestral heritage; the EIA understates the harm]. We ask that it be refused. [Attach evidence.]"

**8C — Establishing the heritage (to the authorities / public).**
*Subject: The shared heritage at [place].*
"We record that [place] holds [natural / historical / ancestral] heritage: [specifics — the archaeology, the ancestors]. This coast belongs to the nation's memory and must be protected. [Attach evidence.]"

**8D — Pressing for protective legislation (to Parliament / ministers).**
*Subject: Protect [place] permanently, as at Clifton.*
"We, citizens of [place], call on Parliament to set [place] aside as a [heritage/national park] by law, protecting its shared heritage and public coast permanently, as was done at Clifton. [Attach evidence.]"

**8E — To a lawyer (court challenge).**
*Subject: Possible challenge — [project] authorisation.*
"Our community faces [project] destroying [heritage/coast] on [a defective authorisation]. We've gathered [the EIA, the heritage evidence, photographs]. We ask your advice on challenging it. [Contact and evidence summary.]"

**8F — To the developer / foreign capital.**
*Subject: Heritage and reputational risk in [project].*
"We are the community defending [place] from [project]. It bulldozes shared heritage and privatizes a public coast, and faces a united nation and legal challenge. As at Clifton, this carries real risk. [Contact and evidence summary.]"

**8G — Letter to the press.**
*Subject: [Community] moves to protect [place] from [project].*
"[Place] holds [our reef / our ancestors' memory]. Now [project] threatens it — a public coast to be sold as a private enclave. We can provide the heritage evidence, photographs, a map and interviews. [Contact.]"

**8H — Uniting the nation.**

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO'S CAPTURED

The Bahamas has real concerns about the closeness of developers and foreign capital to Crown-land and coastal decisions, so this matters.

**How to spot it.** Public coast and Crown land handed to developers on opaque terms; authorisations against the evidence; EIAs written for the proponent; benefits to the connected while the nation loses its coast and heritage.

**Who's supposed to guard against it.** The **Auditor General** examines public money. Anti-corruption and integrity bodies handle conduct. The **courts** can quash a tainted authorisation. And the Bahamas' **press, heritage, and civil-society organisations** have exposed captured deals.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — how the coast was handed over, who benefits, what the law required — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect. Route serious evidence to the Auditor General, the integrity bodies, the courts, and trusted journalists rather than making accusations you can't back. Pair the exposure with the heritage cause and the push for protective law.

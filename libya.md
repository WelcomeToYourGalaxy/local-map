# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN LIBYA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Concern for the environment in Libya turns above all on water drawn from the deep past — for this is a vast desert nation on the Mediterranean, divided since its revolution between rival governments, whose defining environmental reality is its dependence on fossil water: the **Great Man-Made River**, the largest such project on earth, which mines ancient, non-renewable groundwater from beneath the Sahara and pipes it hundreds of kilometres to the coastal cities, supplying more than nine-tenths of the country's water from a reserve that will not refill. Libya is one of the driest nations on earth and yet the holder of one of the largest freshwater treasures ever found beneath the Sahara — a fractured state with real technical institutions but no unified government, where the deadly failure of the neglected dams at **Derna** showed in a single night what neglect and division can cost. The oil is both the economy and the prize of the division, and the desert presses on a water-scarce land. Parts of the country are dangerous. But Libya's is a crisis of management as much as scarcity, and that gives real ground.

In a divided and often insecure nation, those who would defend Libya's water and land work through what still functions, from outside, and only where it is safe — and the tools are real. The distinctive lever is the **finite-water argument**: the fossil aquifer beneath the desert is non-renewable and depleting, so a project that mines, wastes, or gambles it is self-defeating in one of the driest nations on earth — and, because the aquifer is shared, the **transboundary water frameworks** with Libya's neighbours are a real channel. The deadly failure at Derna demands **accountability and documentation** for the neglect of ageing infrastructure — a concrete standard no future project or dam can be allowed to ignore. The oil's harms can be confronted through the **pollution and revenue-transparency frameworks**. The **technical institutions that still function** — the water and infrastructure bodies — remain real avenues where it is safe. And the **international and UN frameworks and bodies**, which a divided state still engages, carry safeguards, accountability, and support. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The country is divided between rival governments, and parts of it are insecure and dangerous, so the work must run through what functions and from outside, protecting everyone. The fossil aquifer is finite and depleting — a problem no amount of pumping can solve, only careful use can slow. The infrastructure is ageing and neglected, as Derna proved in a single deadly night. The oil is entangled in the division and the conflict. And progress is slow and partial. So the work must centre on what is safe and functioning — the water and infrastructure institutions that still work, the international and UN bodies, the communities where it is safe — never exposing or endangering anyone. And the honest prize is the finite water safeguarded and its mining scrutinised, the infrastructure held to account as Derna demands, the oil's harms confronted, on the safe and functioning ground — not a swift stop in a fractured state. Above all, protect the people.

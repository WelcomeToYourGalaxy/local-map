# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN HUNGARY

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In 1984, when open political dissent was dangerous under communism, a biologist named János Vargha founded the **Danube Circle** — Duna Kör — to oppose the **Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros** dam, a Soviet-era mega-project that would have reworked some two hundred kilometres of the Danube, flooded dozens of islands and over a hundred square kilometres of forest and field, drowned the Danube Bend, and threatened Budapest's drinking water. The government backed it; a 1977 treaty bound it. But Hungarians would not lose their river. The Danube Circle began with **samizdat** — underground pamphlets — and the authority of the **Hungarian Academy of Sciences**, grew through petitions and an international conference, and by 1988 had put a hundred and fifty thousand signatures and tens of thousands of demonstrators onto the streets of Budapest. In May 1989 the government gave way and halted the Nagymaros dam; its democratic successors abandoned it. A movement that began as a handful of scientists with a duplicating machine had stopped a mega-project the whole communist state wanted — and helped open the door to democracy itself. That arc, from pamphlet to petition to a state's retreat, is the Hungarian lesson: a concrete environmental threat can be the ground on which a public rediscovers that it can act together and win.

The heirs of the Danube Circle work today in a democracy and an EU member, but one whose civic space has narrowed under a government that has centralised power — so honesty about both the tools and the tightening is essential. Major projects must pass through **environmental impact assessment and the public-participation and information rights** guaranteed by the Aarhus Convention — a real gate and a real say. The distinctive modern lever is **European Union environmental law**: the EIA, Habitats, Birds, and Water Framework Directives and the Aarhus Convention are enforceable against a domestic government through the **European Commission and the Court of Justice of the EU**, a power reaching over the national government's head. The **authority of independent science** — the Hungarian Academy of Sciences and independent experts — carries a credibility hard for a government to dismiss, as it anchored the Danube fight; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. **Protected-area and Natura 2000 designations** shield valued landscapes, and the **courts, domestic and European,** enforce these, while the **shared resources of national identity**, like the Danube and its water, are a powerful rallying argument. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The civic space has narrowed under a government that has centralised power and weakened checks, so the room for advocacy is tighter than in an open democracy. The government prioritises certain projects firmly. And the mass mobilisation that stopped the Nagymaros dam faces a warier state today. Yet the Danube Circle's deepest lesson endures with new urgency: where politics is constrained, a concrete environmental threat — to a river, a water supply, a loved landscape — can be the ground on which a public rediscovers that it can act together and win. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a project assessed and constrained, the EU law brought to bear over the government's head, a landscape protected, a public rallied around a shared resource — through the assessment, the EU law, the science, and the courts.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You win here as the Danube Circle did — by combining independent science, a public rallied around a shared resource, and the law — and today with a lever they lacked: European Union environmental law, enforceable against a domestic government over its head. Hold a project to its environmental assessment and the Aarhus participation rights; bring the EU directives and the European Commission and Court to bear; anchor the case in the Academy's independent science; invoke the protected-area and Natura 2000 designations; and rally the public around a river, a water supply, a loved landscape.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

A Hungarian project runs through the national government and — crucially — the European Union above it. The **government, its ministries, and the planning and environmental authorities** decide the assessments, permits, and priorities. The **courts, domestic and the Court of Justice of the EU**, review the legality. And the **European Commission** can enforce EU environmental law against the national government. The **Hungarian Academy of Sciences and independent experts**, and the **environmental civil society**, carry the science and the advocacy. Map who decides — and where the EU law reaches over the government's head — before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A major project needs environmental-impact assessment and permits, with the public-participation and information rights the Aarhus Convention guarantees. That assessment and participation process is your first entry point: the moment to demand information, document the harm, and invoke the EU directives. Beyond it lie the domestic courts, the European Commission's enforcement, and the Court of Justice of the EU. Enter early, at the assessment, and escalate to the EU law where the national government resists.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the permission: whether a project has a proper assessment and honoured the Aarhus participation rights. Follow the EU law: which directives — EIA, Habitats, Birds, Water Framework — the project engages, and whether it breaches them. Follow the designations: which protected area or Natura 2000 site it touches. The leverage lies in the assessment and participation rights, the EU directives and the Commission and Court, and the science.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

The watchers are the domestic environmental and planning authorities, the courts, the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, the environmental civil society, and — above them all — the European Commission and the Court of Justice of the EU, which can hold the national government to EU environmental law. These are the institutions a case engages, escalating to the European level where the domestic ones fall short.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Ask five questions. **What is the harm?** — the river, water supply, forest, or landscape the project threatens. **What does it need?** — an assessment, permits, and the Aarhus participation rights. **Which EU law does it engage?** — the EIA, Habitats, Birds, or Water Framework Directive, or a Natura 2000 site. **What does the science show?** — the evidence the Academy and independent experts can supply. **What shared resource does it touch?** — the river, the water, the loved landscape that can rally a public. Pick the fight where the assessment, the EU law, or a designation bites hardest and the shared resource is clearest.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

Build the case in layers. **Layer one: the harm** — the river, water, forest, or landscape at stake, documented with the independent science. **Layer two: the law and the standard** — the assessment and Aarhus participation rights, the EU directives and Natura 2000 designations the project breaches or strains. **Layer three: the coalition and the escalation** — the Academy and experts, the civil society, and the path from the domestic authorities to the European Commission and Court. A case anchored in independent science and framed in EU law — the science the Danube Circle used, plus the European lever they lacked — is one that reaches over a resistant government's head.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

Gather the **harm record**: the project's assessment and plans, the river, water, forest, or landscape at stake, the independent scientific evidence. Gather the **law record**: the assessment and Aarhus participation requirements, the EU directives, the Natura 2000 and protected-area designations. Gather the **coalition record**: the Academy of Sciences and independent experts, the environmental civil society. Gather the **escalation record**: the domestic authorities, the courts, the European Commission's complaint procedures. Hungary's EU membership and the Aarhus information rights make much of this genuinely obtainable.

# STEP 4: THE ASSESSMENT, EU LAW & THE SCIENCE — WINNING OVER THE GOVERNMENT'S HEAD

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Keep every message factual, science-anchored, and framed in the law.

**8A — To the environmental authority**: identify the project and the deficient assessment or denied Aarhus participation right, and request proper process.

**8B — To the European Commission**: set out the national government's breach of the EU environmental directives, with the evidence.

**8C — On the EU law (through counsel)**: prepare the challenge under the EIA, Habitats, Birds, or Water Framework Directive.

**8D — To the Hungarian Academy of Sciences or an expert**: request the independent scientific assessment of the harm.

**8E — On a Natura 2000 or protected area**: invoke the designation the project breaches.

**8F — To the domestic courts (through counsel)**: establish the assessment or designation breach.

**8G — To the environmental civil society and community**: rally the public around the shared river, water, or landscape.

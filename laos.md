# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

## Why This Matters

Across Laos, communities learn that the land they farm, the river they fish, the forest they gather from, or the valley their village sits in has been promised to a project. A dam that will flood the valley or dry the river below it. A mine that will poison the stream. A rubber, banana, or eucalyptus plantation carved from land families have worked for generations. A special economic zone, a railway, a power line, a road. Often the first sign is a survey team, a boundary marker, a notice at the district office, or the arrival of machinery — and the people who will lose the most are the last to be asked.

This guide is a practical manual for those people. It explains, step by step, how the approval system for large projects is supposed to work in Laos, where the genuine openings are, what documentation actually shifts a decision, how to organise safely, and how to reach the few real levers that exist.

You must hear the hardest truth first, because everything else depends on you understanding it. Laos is a one-party state that does not tolerate open dissent, and where independent organising against a government-backed project can bring serious consequences. The disappearance of a respected civil-society leader more than a decade ago, never resolved, still shapes what people feel able to do — and it should inform your caution. This does not mean nothing can be done. It means the methods that work here are different from those that work in an open society, and using the wrong method can bring harm without protecting the land. The whole of this guide is built around that reality.

The projects that damage communities in Laos are not random. They follow a national development model built on turning land and rivers into revenue — above all hydropower, promoted for decades under the ambition of becoming the "battery" of the region, but also mining, plantations, and the infrastructure and zones that serve regional trade and foreign investment.

## The Strategic Framework

The core idea of this guide is simple: **a destructive project is not a single event but a chain of decisions, and a chain can be broken at its weakest link.** Your task is to find that link and concentrate your limited strength there, rather than spreading yourself thin against the whole project at once.

Every large project depends on a sequence. Someone proposes it. A ministry screens and classifies it. An environmental and social assessment is supposed to be produced and consulted on. Permits and a compliance certificate are issued. Land is leased, conceded, or acquired, and people are compensated and resettled. Financing is arranged. Construction begins. At each stage there are documents that must exist and procedures that must be followed. Every requirement is a possible point of failure for the project — and therefore a possible point of leverage for you.

In a country with a tilted system, the domestic links are usually the strong ones you cannot move: the ministry will classify the project as the developer wishes, the assessment will be approved, the courts will not intervene against a state-backed venture. But the chain does not stop at the border. Many projects in Laos depend on things the government cannot fully control: international financing and its safeguard rules; foreign companies that build, own, or operate the project and answer to their own regulators and lenders; the neighbouring states and regional bodies that buy the electricity or share the river; and the documented truth reaching the outside world. Those are the links where a careful community has actually changed outcomes.

This guide teaches you to map that chain for your specific project, identify which link is genuinely weakest given who is behind the project and who funds and buys from it, and put your strength there.

## Critical Caveat: A Tilted System Can Make Opposition Harder (But Doesn't Make It Impossible)

Throughout this guide you will find honest assessments of what is realistic. This is deliberate. A guide that told you any project can be stopped by filing the right objection would be lying, and in Laos that lie could get people hurt. Here is the honest frame:

**When a project is private, mid-sized, and dependent on outside money, foreign operators, or export markets, it is genuinely vulnerable.** A plantation that needs a bank loan, a mine that sells into an international supply chain, a dam built and financed by foreign companies that answer to their own lenders and buyers — these have exposed flanks. Documented illegality, environmental harm, or unlawful dispossession can cost them financing, contracts, and licences in ways domestic pressure alone never could.

**When a project is a state priority — a strategic dam, a flagship of national energy policy, a venture tied to powerful interests — stopping it outright is far less likely.** For these, the realistic goal is usually different: delay, force mitigation, win better compensation and lawful resettlement, shrink the footprint, protect the most vital areas, or build the documented record that constrains the project later or supports accountability in another forum. These are real outcomes. They save homes, livelihoods, and lives, and they are worth fighting for even when total victory is not on the table.

Knowing which situation you are in — before you act — is the single most important judgment you will make.

## How to use this guide

Read the whole thing once before doing anything. Resist the urge to jump to writing letters or calling a meeting. The sequence matters: identify the target and its weak link, then document, then organise, then escalate through legal, lender, and media channels.

If you have very little time, read the Introduction, the section on how the system works, and the Rapid-Fire Version, then return for the detail. If safety is your overriding concern — and in Laos it should be — read "When the System Is Tilted" early, because it governs how everything else should be done.

Two cautions run through every page. First, **safety before tactics**: no document, meeting, or gesture is worth a person's freedom or life, and where the guide describes an action it also weighs its risk. Second, **legality as framing**: wherever possible, frame your opposition as insisting that existing law and procedure be followed — not as political opposition to the government. This is both safer and, with the actors who matter most here, more persuasive.
# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

You cannot break a chain you cannot see. This section maps the bodies that decide, the journey a project takes, the rules that determine the outcome, the money that biases it, and the overseers who are supposed to watch — so you can find where your project is genuinely vulnerable.

## The Bodies That Decide — And What Each One Controls

**The Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment (MONRE) and its Department of Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (DESIA).** On paper this is the central environmental gate. MONRE is the lead agency for environmental management, and DESIA reviews and approves the environmental and social impact assessment for larger projects and issues the Environmental Compliance Certificate. For smaller projects, the provincial environment office (PONRE) handles the lighter Initial Environmental Examination. Understand this as a gate that exists in law but is frequently weak in practice: assessments are often thin, consultation shallow, and — as researchers have documented — developers sometimes begin work while the assessment is still unfinished.

**The Ministry of Energy and Mines (MEM).** For the projects that dominate destructive development in Laos — hydropower and mining — MEM is the sponsoring and overseeing ministry. It champions these sectors as national priorities.

**The investment and planning authorities.** The Ministry of Planning and Investment and the provincial and central investment-promotion bodies handle the concessions and investment licences that larger and foreign projects require.

**The land administration.** Under Lao law, the State ultimately owns the land, and communities and companies hold use rights through certificates, leases, and concessions. MONRE and its land offices, together with district and provincial authorities, administer these rights. Concessions — long leases of state land for plantations, mines, and dams — are the mechanism through which vast areas change hands, and they have historically targeted the lands of ethnic-minority communities, who have less capacity to resist.

**Provincial and district authorities, and the village administration.** Much of what actually happens to a community is implemented at province, district, and village level — the surveys, the boundary-setting, the compensation registers, the resettlement. These local authorities are where many decisions are made real and much documentation is generated.

**The Party and the one-party structure.** Above and through all of this runs a single-party state in which the Party, the government, and major economic interests are closely bound. There is no independent legislature, no free press, and no independent judiciary to appeal to against a state priority.

## How a Decision Is Actually Made — The Journey of a Project

Knowing the sequence tells you when to act, because each stage has a window and a document.

**Stage 1 — Proposal and screening.** A developer submits an investment application, and the project is screened and categorised — under the current rules — as requiring either a preliminary (lighter) or a comprehensive (full) assessment, depending on the severity of expected impacts. This categorisation decides how much scrutiny and consultation the project must undergo.

**Stage 2 — Assessment and consultation.** For a comprehensive assessment, the developer must produce the environmental and social impact study, a management and monitoring plan, and — under the guidelines — carry out consultation with local authorities and affected people. This is the stage where the public has an actual, if limited, role, and it is the stage most often done badly: consultations that are brief, poorly announced, dominated by officials, or held after decisions are effectively made.

**Stage 3 — Review and the compliance certificate.** DESIA reviews the assessment and, if satisfied, issues the Environmental Compliance Certificate with conditions and an approved management and monitoring plan.

**Stage 4 — Land, compensation, and resettlement.** Concessions and leases are granted, land-use rights are affected, and compensation and resettlement are supposed to follow the resettlement decree.

**Stage 5 — Financing and construction.** The project arranges money and begins. Where financing comes from an international lender, or where the project is built and operated by foreign companies, that involvement carries safeguard rules and reputational exposure that domestic approvals do not — the most important external link in the chain.

## The Rulebook — The Provisions That Decide the Outcome

You do not need to be a lawyer, but you must know the handful of instruments that govern your fight:

- **The Environmental Protection Law (No. 29/NA of 2012)** — the backbone requiring environmental management and enabling the assessment system.
- **The Decree on Environmental Impact Assessment (No. 389 of 2022)** — the current EIA rulebook: the categorisation into preliminary and comprehensive assessments, the assessment and consultation process, and the Environmental Compliance Certificate. This is the single most important instrument for procedural challenges. It replaced the earlier 2013 ministerial instruction and the 2010 decree.
- **The Policy on Sustainable Hydropower Development (Decree No. 02/GoL of 2015)** — the government's own commitments on how hydropower should be assessed and managed, including cumulative and basin-wide impacts.
- **The Land Law (2019)** — the framework under which the State owns land and grants use rights, leases, and concessions.
- **The Compensation and Resettlement Decree (No. 192 of 2005, and its revisions)** — governing what is owed to people displaced or affected by a project. Inadequate compensation and resettlement are among the most common and documentable violations.

The pattern to hold in mind: the environmental rules give you procedure and delay; the land, compensation, and resettlement rules are where dispossession happens and where the clearest wrongs often lie; and the external rules — lender safeguards, foreign-operator obligations, and shared-river commitments — are where a tilted domestic system can be bypassed.

## Follow the Money — Why the System Often Leans Toward "Yes"

Understand the incentives and you understand why the gate is so often open. The Lao development model turns land and rivers into revenue, and hydropower in particular has been a central national strategy, generating electricity for export and income for the state and its partners. Concessions convert land into capital. The ministries that sponsor these projects are measured by investment and output, not by the villages protected, and powerful interests are frequently entwined with the ventures. Your leverage therefore rarely comes from persuading the domestic decision-maker on the merits — it comes from raising the cost, legal, financial, and reputational, to the actors who can still be moved.

## The Overseers — Who Watches the Decision-Makers

In an open system, the overseers are courts, an ombudsman, an auditor, a free press, and elected representatives. In Laos most of these are absent or cannot be used against a state priority. There is no independent judiciary to enjoin a state-backed project, no free domestic press, and civil-society organising on sensitive issues carries real risk.

But some overseers do function, and they sit outside the government's control:

- **International financial institutions' accountability mechanisms** — the independent complaint bodies attached to development banks, which can investigate whether a financed project broke the bank's own environmental and social rules.
- **Foreign operators', owners', and buyers' obligations** — the safeguard commitments of the companies and lenders that build, own, operate, or off-take from a project, increasingly backed by law and by reputational exposure in their home markets.
- **The Mekong River Commission and transboundary processes** — the regional body and the downstream states that share the river, which provide a forum, however imperfect, for raising cross-border and cumulative impacts that the national process ignores.
- **International documentation and human-rights bodies** — the UN mechanisms, land-rights and environmental organisations, and researchers whose records reach governments, lenders, and investors.

These are the overseers your strategy should be built to reach.

## Step Importance Ranking (When All Combined)

When these actions are combined into a single campaign, their importance is not equal. Ranked by how much each contributes to a realistic result:

1. **Accurate target and leverage identification.** Choosing the right weak link — usually a compensation/resettlement failure or an external financier, operator, or buyer — determines everything. A campaign aimed at the wrong link fails no matter how hard it works.
2. **Compensation and resettlement documentation.** In Laos this is both the most common site of harm and one of the most defensible claims.
3. **The external channel** — lender complaint, foreign-operator obligation, or transboundary process — matched to who actually funds, builds, and buys from the project.
4. **A unified, well-built community record.** This is the raw material every external channel needs, and the thing that holds a community together under pressure.
5. **Procedural (assessment/consultation) documentation.** Valuable as delay and as evidence, weaker as a standalone.
6. **Media and documentation strategy**, sequenced carefully and safely, to amplify the record to the actors who can move.
7. **Domestic administrative filings**, used chiefly to build the record and force responses, not relied on to win.

## What Laotian Campaigns Actually Show

Several hard lessons recur across documented Lao experiences, and they should shape your expectations.

**Hydropower is national policy, and that sets the ceiling.** Dams are central to the country's development strategy, which means a flagship dam is among the hardest things to stop outright. But even here, external attention, lender safeguards, and transboundary concern have forced design changes, resettlement improvements, and scrutiny — and cross-border and downstream pressure on shared-river impacts is a real and growing lever.

**Compensation and resettlement are where most is actually contested.** Displacement by dams, mines, and concessions is widespread, and the compensation and resettlement that follow are frequently inadequate or delayed. Because this harm is concrete, measurable, and governed by the state's own decree, it is often the best-documented and most defensible claim a community has — and the one most likely to move a lender or foreign operator.

**Concessions have targeted the most vulnerable land.** Plantation and resource concessions have historically fallen on ethnic-minority and upland communities with the least capacity to resist. This is a documented pattern, and precisely because such dispossession is often carried out without the process or consent the law nominally requires, it is frequently a strong documented claim — though it must be handled with care for the safety of those involved.

**The external levers work when they exist and fail when they don't.** Complaints to lender mechanisms, pressure on foreign operators and buyers, and transboundary channels have forced reviews, delays, and improvements — but only for projects actually connected to such money, companies, or rivers. For a purely domestic, state-financed project with no international involvement, these levers may simply not be available, and pretending otherwise wastes effort.

**Safety shapes everything.** In a one-party state intolerant of dissent, where a prominent civil-society leader was disappeared and villagers have been imprisoned for protesting a concession, open opposition carries grave personal risk. Campaigns that achieve something here are careful, documentation-led, and oriented toward external forums rather than domestic confrontation.

**Delay and mitigation are real victories.** A dam delayed may lose its financing or be reshaped. A resettlement made lawful and adequate, a footprint reduced, a vital fishery or forest protected, a downstream flow safeguarded — these save real homes and livelihoods.
# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Before you write a single letter or call a single meeting, you must answer a set of questions about the project you face. The answers determine which link in the chain is weakest, which lever is actually open to you, and — critically in Laos — how much personal risk any given action carries. Getting this stage right is worth more than any amount of energy spent later.

## Core Questions You Must Answer

### Question 1: Who is really behind this project, and how exposed are they?

Everything flows from this. Find out who the developer actually is — not just the name on the notice, but the beneficial owner, the parent company, the foreign partners, and any state or connected interest behind it. A private company dependent on outside money is exposed. A dam built and operated by foreign firms answering to their own lenders and buyers is exposed through them. A flagship state energy project serving national policy is far less so.

Ask: Is this a domestic private firm, a foreign investor, a joint venture, a state enterprise, or a mix? Does it need international financing? Is it built, owned, or operated by foreign companies? Does it sell its output — electricity, a mineral, a crop — across a border or into an international supply chain? The more the project depends on money, companies, and markets outside the government's control, the more leverage you have.

### Question 2: What stage is the project at, and which window is still open?

A project you can still affect is one where a required step has not been completed. Has it been screened and categorised yet? Has the assessment been produced, and has the consultation stage happened or is it coming? Has the Environmental Compliance Certificate been issued? Have the concession and land arrangements been finalised, or are compensation and resettlement still to come? Has financing closed?

Each unfinished step is a window. The earlier you engage, the more windows remain and the more options you have. A project still at screening or consultation is far more vulnerable than one already built.

### Question 3: What is the land and resettlement situation, and what is owed?

This is often the decisive question in Laos. Whose land is it, and under what use rights? Is it held under a land-use certificate, worked under customary practice, or already inside a granted concession? Are people to be displaced, and if so, what compensation and resettlement does the decree require, and is what is being offered adequate and lawful? For a river project, what happens to the fisheries, the flood-recession gardens, the downstream flow that livelihoods depend on?

Because compensation and resettlement failures are so common — and because concessions have so often fallen on communities with the least protection — the land-and-resettlement question is frequently where the clearest, most defensible wrong lies.

### Question 4: What external channels does this project touch?

List every connection the project has to the world outside the domestic system. Is there an international lender — a development bank, a multilateral, an export credit agency — anywhere in the financing? Are foreign companies building, owning, or operating it? Does the output cross a border — electricity sold to a neighbouring state, a mineral or crop entering an international supply chain? Does the project affect a shared river, engaging the Mekong River Commission and downstream states? Is any international body already documenting the area or the company?

Each such connection is a potential external lever — a forum outside the government's control where documented harm can raise the project's cost.

### Question 5: What is the safety environment, and what can be done without unacceptable risk?

This question governs all the others in Laos. What has happened to people who objected to projects, concessions, or authority in this area? What forms of activity are being punished, and how severely? Which actions can be taken quietly — documentation, private complaints, reaching trusted outside contacts — and which cannot be taken safely at all, such as open protest or public confrontation against a state project?

You must answer this honestly and let it shape every choice. The disappearance of a prominent civil-society leader and the imprisonment of villagers for protesting a concession are not distant history; they define the space you operate in.

## Worked example: which lever is actually open

Consider a village facing a proposed rubber plantation on land the community has farmed for generations but which the state has designated for concession. A survey team has appeared; families are told they must move or accept a small payment; no proper resettlement has been discussed.

Working the five questions: **Who is behind it?** A company that needs a bank facility to develop the concession and intends to sell rubber into a regional and international supply chain — exposed on two fronts. **What stage?** The concession is being processed but land has not been fully cleared and compensation is not settled — several windows still open. **Land and resettlement?** The land is under long-standing customary use; the compensation offered falls short of what the resettlement decree requires, and the process is being rushed — the core wrong and the strongest documented claim. **External channels?** The bank financing and the international buyers are the two levers outside the government's reach. **Safety?** Open protest against the company or authorities is dangerous; quiet documentation and outside contact through trusted intermediaries are feasible.

The analysis points clearly: this is not a fight to be waged by public confrontation, but by documenting the customary use, the inadequate compensation, and the flawed resettlement, building a unified and careful record of who farms what and what is owed, and carrying that record to the company's financier and its buyers through their grievance and due-diligence channels — while using any procedural defect in the concession or assessment to add delay.
# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

Documentation is the single most important thing a Lao community can produce, because it is the raw material every effective lever depends on. A lender's accountability mechanism, a foreign operator's compliance office, a transboundary body, an international investigator — none of them can act on your word alone. They act on evidence: dated, specific, verifiable records of what exists now, what the project is, and what harm is being done.

Documentation is also, in present conditions, comparatively safe. Gathering evidence quietly exposes people far less than public protest, and it builds the foundation for every other action.

## The Three Documentation Layers

Effective documentation works in three layers: the baseline conditions before the project, the project and the process itself, and the human and institutional impacts. Each answers a different question and serves a different lever. Build all three.

## LAYER 1: BASELINE CONDITIONS DOCUMENTATION

This layer records what exists now, before the project changes or destroys it.

Document the land and its use: who farms which plots, for how long, under what customary or certified arrangement; the boundaries as the community understands them; the paddy, the swidden fields, the gardens, the grazing, the forest gathering, and — for a river community — the fisheries, the riverbank gardens, and the seasonal flows that livelihoods depend on. Photograph and map it, with dates. Record the state of the environment: the river's flow and clarity, the fish catch, the forest cover, the soil, the wells and streams people drink from. Record the community itself: how many households, how many people, the spirit forests and sacred sites, the graves, the school and health post — the things that make this a place and not just a plot.

Wherever possible, tie your baseline to something external and dated — a dated photograph, a satellite image, an older map or record, a land-use certificate, an account signed and dated by village elders.

## LAYER 2: PROJECT AND PROCESS ANALYSIS

This layer records the project itself and how it was approved — the procedural spine of your case. Obtain and preserve every document the project generates: the investment application or concession agreement, any screening or categorisation decision, the environmental and social impact assessment if one exists, the Environmental Compliance Certificate and its conditions, the management and monitoring plan, the compensation and resettlement plan, and any public announcements.

Then compare what happened against what the rules require. Was the project categorised correctly, or was a large dam or mine treated as a minor-impact project to avoid a comprehensive assessment? Was the required consultation held — and if so, was it announced in time, genuinely open, and conducted before decisions were effectively made, or was it a formality? Does the assessment actually assess the impacts — including the cumulative and downstream effects the hydropower policy requires — or does it read like a document written to secure approval? Were the compliance certificate's conditions met? Was the compensation and resettlement plan consistent with the decree, and was it actually delivered? Did work begin before the assessment was complete?

Every gap between what the rules require and what actually happened is a documented procedural failure. Record each one specifically: the rule, the requirement, what happened instead, and the evidence. This is what turns a grievance into a case.

## LAYER 3: HUMAN AND INSTITUTIONAL IMPACT DOCUMENTATION

This layer records what the project does to people and how the institutions responded. Document the impacts on livelihoods: land lost, paddy and gardens submerged, fisheries destroyed, forest access cut off, income foregone, the cost of a resettlement borne by those least able to pay. Document the health and environmental harms with as much specificity as you can: what was contaminated, what the water looks like now, what the resettlement site lacks. Document the human cost of the process: pressure, threats, or coercion used to secure agreement or silence objection — recorded carefully and, where naming individuals could endanger them, with appropriate protection of identities.

Also document the institutional response — or its absence. When the community raised concerns, what did the authorities do? Were complaints acknowledged, addressed, ignored, or met with pressure?

## How to Structure Your Documentation

Keep your evidence organised so it can be handed to someone else and understood without you. Maintain a simple, dated log of events — what happened, when, who was involved, what evidence exists. Keep documents, photographs, and maps labelled with dates and locations. Keep at least one secure copy somewhere the project's backers cannot reach it, and ideally a copy held safely outside the immediate area.

Structure the file around the three layers and around the specific violations you have found, so that when the moment comes to send it to a lender, an operator, a transboundary body, or a trusted organisation, you are assembling from an organised archive rather than scrambling.

## Common Documentation Pitfalls (What Fails)

Weak documentation is vague, undated, and general — "the company took our land" without showing which land, held how, when, and with what proof. It relies on memory rather than contemporaneous record. It mixes fact with anger, so a reviewer cannot separate the verifiable from the felt. It fails to connect a specific harm to a specific requirement of the law or the decree. And it is stored insecurely, so it can be lost, seized, or used to identify people.

Strong documentation is the opposite: specific, dated, tied to external reference points, organised around clear violations, cautious about identities, and securely kept. It reads calmly and lets the facts carry the weight.

## Documentation Budget Breakdown

Documentation is deliberately low-cost, which is part of why it is the right first priority for communities with little money. The main costs are a means of taking dated photographs; simple materials for mapping and record-keeping; secure storage, including a safe off-site copy; and, where possible, modest support for a trusted person to maintain the archive carefully over time. Occasionally there is a cost to obtaining copies of official documents or to secure communication with outside contacts. Compared to litigation or travel, these sums are small — and the return, in leverage, is the highest of any spending a community can make.
# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

This section is a practical checklist of the specific records that strengthen a Lao case, and where they can usually be obtained. You will rarely get all of them, and in present conditions some will be impossible or unsafe to pursue. Gather what you safely can, prioritising the documents that establish what is owed and that expose procedural failure, because those feed your strongest levers.

**The project's environmental documents.** The screening or categorisation decision, the environmental and social impact assessment, the Environmental Compliance Certificate and its conditions, and the management and monitoring plan. Where the system functions, these are meant to be obtainable through MONRE and DESIA and may be referenced in project materials; assessments are sometimes disclosed around consultation. If you can obtain the assessment, it is a goldmine: its own admissions of impact, and the gaps between its promises and reality, become your evidence. Where the documents cannot be obtained, the absence of a required assessment or certificate is itself documentable and important.

**The compensation and resettlement records.** The compensation and resettlement plan, the entitlement registers, the valuations, and the records of what was actually paid or provided. These flow through the district and provincial authorities and the project's resettlement office. They establish what is owed and let you compare it against what is actually delivered — and inadequate or undelivered compensation is among the most common and defensible wrongs.

**The land and concession records.** Land-use certificates, the concession or lease agreement, and any land-classification and acquisition documents. These flow through MONRE's land offices and the district and provincial authorities, and the 2019 Land Law provides for a national land database accessible for a fee. They establish who holds what on paper — which you then compare against who actually uses the land in fact.

**The developer's identity and backing.** Company registration and investment-licence records establish who owns and funds the project. This tells you whether the developer is an exposed private firm or a connected interest, and who the foreign partners, financiers, and operators are — the information on which your whole leverage analysis depends. Where official searches are difficult, international corporate databases, investigative reporting, and civil-society and academic research can often fill the gap.

**The financing trail.** Any indication of who funds the project — a development bank, a commercial lender, an export credit agency, a foreign investor. Loan announcements, corporate filings, project materials, and the work of researchers and journalists are the usual sources.

**The operator and off-taker trail.** Who builds, owns, and operates the project, and — for a power project — who buys the electricity, often across a border. For a mine or plantation, the buyers and traders at the end of the supply chain. Corporate disclosures, power-purchase arrangements, trade data, and researchers' work are the sources.

**The transboundary record.** For a river project, evidence of downstream and cumulative impacts — on flow, fisheries, sediment, and the communities below the project, including across borders. This engages the Mekong River Commission and downstream states, a real external forum for shared-river harms. Researchers, river networks, and the commission's own processes are the sources.

**The consultation and objection record.** Notices of consultation, attendance records, and — crucially — your own contemporaneous account of what the consultation actually was: when it was announced, who could attend, whether it happened before decisions were made, whether objection was genuinely possible. Where consultation was skipped or was a formality, your record of that fact is the evidence of the procedural failure.

**The baseline and impact evidence.** Dated photographs, maps, satellite imagery, older records, and signed accounts establishing what existed before and what changed — as described in the documentation section. Much of this you create yourself; some can be supplemented from public satellite imagery and historical maps.

**A note on access, and on safety.** In a functioning system many of these records are obtainable through official channels. In Laos, official channels are unreliable and some inquiries can draw dangerous attention. Weigh every act of gathering against its risk. Favour records you can obtain quietly, information already public, and the evidence you create yourself. Lean on trusted researchers, land- and river-focused organisations, and regional networks — inside and outside the country — who may already hold relevant information and who can gather or receive material more safely than an exposed villager can.
# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION

## Critical Insight About Organizing

In an open society, organising means building a large, visible movement that pressures elected officials through public demonstration. In Laos, that model can bring serious consequences and often does not even reach the actors who can move the project. So the organising this guide describes is different. Its purpose is not public spectacle; it is to build a **unified, disciplined, well-documented community** that can hold together under pressure, produce a credible record, and channel that record to external levers — while keeping its people as safe as possible.

The strength you are building is not the size of a crowd. It is unity, discipline, careful documentation, trusted outside connections, and the ability to act quietly and in concert.

## PHASE 1: FOUNDATION (Weeks 1–4)

Begin quietly and close to home. The first task is to establish, among the people directly affected, a shared and accurate understanding of what the project is, what stage it is at, and what is really at stake. A calm, factual account, built from the target-identification questions, is the foundation.

Identify the small group of trusted people who will carry the work: those respected in the community, careful in temperament, and able to keep confidences. Establish, from the very beginning, the security practices that will govern everything: how information is shared, what is written down and where, what is kept off phones and out of public view, and how people protect one another.

Begin the documentation described earlier — the baseline, the project records, the compensation and tenure evidence — as the community's first concrete shared task.

## PHASE 2: WIDENING THE CIRCLE (Weeks 4–12)

"Going public" must be understood carefully in the Lao context. It does not mean a demonstration. It means widening the circle of the affected community into a coherent, agreed position — reaching the households that will be affected, aligning them around a shared and accurate account, and building consensus about goals and limits. What does the community actually want — the project stopped, the footprint reduced, a fishery protected, fair compensation and lawful resettlement? What risks is the community willing and unwilling to take?

This is also the stage to make first, careful contact with the outside allies who will matter: trusted researchers, land- and river-focused organisations, regional networks, and — where relevant — the bodies connected to lenders or the shared river. These contacts should be approached with discretion, through trusted intermediaries where possible.

## PHASE 3: COALITION EXPANSION (Months 3–8)

Now widen the circle beyond the immediately affected community to those who share the stake or the risk: neighbouring villages facing the same project or the same company, communities along the same river or inside the same concession, and the broader networks — land, environmental, river, and regional — that connect local struggles to outside attention.

Coalition in Laos is built horizontally and quietly, through trusted relationships rather than public alliances. Its value is real: it multiplies documentation, spreads risk, connects you to the organisations that can carry your case to lenders, operators, and transboundary bodies, and ensures that if one node falls silent, the record and the effort survive elsewhere.

## PHASE 4: SUSTAINED PRESSURE (Months 6–18)

Most consequential projects are not stopped quickly. The final phase is about endurance: keeping the community united, the documentation current, and the external channels engaged over the long run, while managing risk and morale.

Sustained pressure in this context means continuously feeding the external levers — updating the lender complaint, the foreign operator's compliance office, the transboundary body — with fresh, dated evidence as the project develops; maintaining the unity and security of the community; and being ready to act when a window opens, such as a financing decision, an operator's review, or a downstream-state concern.

## Worked example: a community that unlocks the strong levers

Return to the village facing the rubber plantation on its customary land. Consider how organising, done in the Lao way, unlocks the levers that matter.

In the foundation phase, a small trusted core establishes the facts and, above all, the security practices, and begins documenting who has farmed which plots for how long and what compensation each household is owed — the record that is the community's strongest asset. In the widening phase, the affected households align around a clear, shared goal: not vague resistance, but a specific demand that their customary use be respected and that compensation and resettlement meet the decree, with the concession reconsidered where the process was unlawful. They make careful contact with a land-focused organisation and a regional network.

In the coalition phase, they connect quietly with neighbouring villages facing the same company and with researchers tracking the company's financing and buyers. Now the village has become **a community that unlocks the strong levers**: its documented, unified record of use and of inadequate compensation, carried by trusted allies, reaches the company's bank and its international buyers through their grievance and due-diligence channels — the actors who can actually impose a cost. In the sustained phase, the community keeps that pressure alive until the company, facing financing and reputational risk it did not expect, improves the terms, reduces its claim, or comes to terms.

## Opposition Building Budget: Year-Long Campaign

A careful Lao campaign is not expensive in money, though it is costly in effort and risk. The main resources needed over a year are: the low-cost documentation materials already described; secure communication and storage; modest support for the trusted people who carry the sustained work; the costs of discreet travel and contact with outside allies; and, where a lender or transboundary complaint is pursued, the support of an allied organisation, which is often provided without charge to communities. Keep the campaign's financial footprint small and its records secure.
# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES

Legal challenge in Laos must be understood realistically. There is no independent judiciary that will act against a state-backed project, and domestic litigation against a powerful developer is essentially unavailable as a route to a winning judgment. Yet "legal" work still matters — not as a path to a courtroom victory, but as a way to establish rights on the record, force procedural responses, create delay, and build the documented case that external levers rely on. Think of the legal dimension in three tracks, and put nearly all your weight on the two that actually carry force here.

## TRACK 1: THE ASSESSMENT (a real but weak gate)

The environmental assessment regime — the EIA decree, the compliance certificate, the consultation and cumulative-impact requirements, the hydropower policy — is a genuine set of legal obligations. A project that skipped a required assessment, was categorised low to avoid one, conducted a sham consultation, ignored cumulative and downstream impacts, or breached its compliance-certificate conditions is in procedural violation.

The realistic value of this track is limited but real. Domestically, an administrative complaint rarely stops a state-backed project, but it forces the authorities to respond, documents the violation on the record, and can create delay. More importantly, a documented procedural failure is powerful outside the domestic system: it is exactly the kind of finding that a lender's accountability mechanism, a foreign operator's compliance office, or a transboundary body treats as serious, because it shows the project did not meet the standards those actors require.

## TRACK 2: THE LAND, COMPENSATION, AND RESETTLEMENT (where most is won)

For most Lao communities, this is where the strongest claim lives, because compensation and resettlement failures are so common and so concrete. The relevant instruments are the Land Law, the compensation and resettlement decree, and the hydropower policy's own commitments on affected people.

The claims that carry weight are specific: that compensation offered falls short of what the decree requires; that resettlement was conducted without adequate consultation, to inadequate sites, or without restoring livelihoods; that land under long-standing customary use was taken through a concession without the process the law requires; that entitlements were promised and not delivered.

Even where no domestic court will enforce these claims, establishing them clearly on the record does real work. It is the foundation of any complaint to a lender or foreign operator, whose own standards require adequate compensation, livelihood restoration, and — increasingly — respect for the rights of affected and Indigenous communities. It supports the case that the domestic system offered no remedy. And it can, in some circumstances, win improved compensation, better resettlement, or a reduced footprint even when outright reversal is not achieved.

## TRACK 3: THE LENDERS, OPERATORS, AND THE SHARED RIVER

The third track reaches outside the domestic system entirely, and for many Lao projects it is the strongest of all.

Where an international financial institution funds the project, its **independent accountability mechanism** can receive a complaint from affected communities alleging that the project breached the institution's environmental and social safeguards — on assessment, consultation, land, compensation, resettlement, and Indigenous rights. Such a complaint can trigger an independent investigation, dialogue, and pressure that domestic channels cannot, and can force delay, mitigation, or improved terms.

Where foreign companies build, own, or operate the project, or buy its output, their **own safeguard obligations, lenders, and grievance processes** — increasingly backed by law and reputational exposure in their home countries — provide another external forum.

And for a river project, the **Mekong River Commission and downstream states** provide a transboundary forum for raising cross-border and cumulative impacts — on flow, fisheries, and sediment — that the national process ignores. This channel is imperfect and slow, and it does not bind the government, but it is a real avenue outside domestic control, and downstream-state concern has moved projects before.

## The three legal challenge types

In summary, your "legal" toolkit has three types of challenge, ranked by their realistic force in Laos: **the compensation, resettlement, and land claim**, strongest because these failures are so common and feed every external lever; **the external complaint** — to a lender's mechanism, a foreign operator or buyer, or a transboundary body — strongest where the relevant connection exists; and **the procedural (assessment) challenge**, useful mainly to build the record, force responses, and create delay. Domestic litigation sits beneath all three: effectively closed against a state-backed project, and never to be relied upon.

## Legal Strategy Decision Tree

Choose your track by answering, in order: **Are people being displaced or their land taken without adequate compensation and lawful resettlement?** If yes, the compensation-and-land track is your spine — document what is owed and the shortfall, and build from there. **Is there an international lender, a foreign operator or buyer, or a shared-river impact?** If yes, the external complaint is your most powerful lever — prepare it carefully with expert help. **Is there a procedural defect in the assessment or consultation?** If yes, use it to force responses, create delay, and strengthen the external complaints. **Is a domestic administrative filing safe and potentially useful for delay or record?** If yes, consider it — but never rely on it, and always weigh its risk.

## Litigation and Advocacy Success Factors

What distinguishes effective legal-track work in Laos is not legal brilliance but preparation and matching. The community that succeeds has documented what is owed and the shortfall meticulously; has identified the right external forum for its specific project; has enlisted expert help — a land-focused organisation, a river network, a specialist in lender complaints — early; frames its claims around the law, the decree, and the government's own policy rather than around politics; and manages the risk of every filing.

## What to Avoid

Do not pin your hopes on a domestic court against a state-backed project; that door is effectively closed. Do not file anything that requires exposing people to danger without a clear, proportionate purpose. Do not let the legal track become a substitute for documentation and external engagement — it is their servant, not their replacement. And do not proceed on the most sensitive claims, or make contact with the most powerful interests, without expert guidance on both strategy and safety.
# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

Evidence does not speak for itself. The same documented facts must be translated into different arguments for different audiences, because a lender's accountability officer, a foreign operator's compliance team, a transboundary body, and a researcher each respond to different things. Learning to reframe one body of evidence for each audience is what multiplies its force.

**For an international lender**, frame the evidence as a breach of the lender's own safeguard standards. Lenders commit to environmental and social requirements — on assessment, consultation, land acquisition, compensation, involuntary resettlement, livelihood restoration, and Indigenous peoples. Your argument is not "this project is unjust"; it is "this project, which your institution finances, violated these specific standards, in these documented ways, and the affected communities have had no remedy."

**For a foreign operator or buyer**, frame the evidence as legal and reputational risk. Their concern is exposure under their own lenders' conditions and the due-diligence laws and reputational costs of their home markets. Your argument is: "the project you build, operate, or buy from displaced these communities without lawful compensation, with these documented harms; here is the evidence; here is the risk you carry."

**For a transboundary body or downstream state**, frame the evidence as cross-border and cumulative harm to a shared river — the flow, the fisheries, the sediment, the downstream communities.

**For a researcher, human-rights body, or journalist**, frame the evidence as a documented pattern measured against a legal or human-rights standard, with precision, sourcing, and care for identities — and, for a journalist, as a story with human stakes, a clear wrong, and a powerful actor who can be held responsible, which the next section addresses.

The discipline in every case is the same: lead with the specific, dated, verifiable fact; connect it to the standard or interest that moves this particular audience; and let the calm accumulation of documented truth, not the intensity of feeling, carry the argument.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY

Media in the Lao context is a double-edged tool, and it must be handled with unusual care. Done well, independent and international media raises the cost of a project to its financiers, operators, and buyers and carries documented truth to the outside forums that can act. Done carelessly, it can expose people to grave danger. This section is about using media's real power while protecting the people who provide it.

## How Journalists Actually Work

Understand the landscape first. There is no free domestic press in Laos, and open dissent carries serious risk; local reporting on sensitive projects is not a safe avenue. The media that matters most for your strategy is therefore **regional and international** — outlets and reporters covering the Mekong region, environmental and human-rights journalists, and the specialist publications that follow dams, land, and rivers. These journalists work as journalists everywhere do: they need verifiable facts, a clear story, human voices, and evidence they can stand behind.

## The Newsworthiness Formula

A project becomes a story when it combines a clear wrong, a human stake, a powerful and nameable actor, and solid evidence. In the Lao context, stories that travel are those that connect a local harm to something the outside world is watching: a link to international finance or a foreign operator; a shared-river impact affecting downstream countries; a documented pattern of dispossession, especially on ethnic-minority land; or a connection to actors already under scrutiny.

## Know the Outlets That Cover This Beat

Identify, with the help of your allies, the specific journalists and outlets that credibly cover Lao and Mekong-region land, environment, and rivers — the regional outlets, the international wire services and papers with a Southeast Asia focus, the specialist environmental and river publications, and the researchers whose work feeds them. A precise, evidence-backed approach to the right journalist is worth far more than a broad appeal to many.

## Building Reporter Relationships (The Key)

The heart of media work is a trusted relationship with a small number of credible journalists, built through intermediaries and handled securely. Provide them with verified, well-organised evidence; be honest about what you know and do not know; and be scrupulously careful about the safety of sources. A journalist who learns to trust your material — because it is accurate, specific, and never exaggerated — becomes a durable channel to the outside world.

## Why media matters even when it doesn't make the front page

Even coverage that does not stop a project does essential work. It puts documented facts into the international record, where lenders, operators, and transboundary bodies can find them. It signals to financiers and companies that their association with the project carries reputational cost. It supports the case that the domestic system offered no remedy. And it can protect people, because actors operating in the dark behave worse than actors who know the world is watching.

## Sample Media Timeline

Media should follow, not lead, your documentation and safety work. Early on, while you build the record, media work is limited to quietly identifying the right journalists and allies. Only once you have solid, verified evidence and have thought through the risks do you make careful contact. As the campaign matures and external complaints are filed, coordinated media can amplify those complaints to the actors who can move. Throughout, the pace is set by two things: the strength of your evidence and the safety of your people. Never let a media opportunity outrun either.

## Media Measurement

Measure media not by volume but by effect: Did it reach the financiers, operators, buyers, or transboundary bodies you are trying to move? Did it enter the record your external complaints rely on? Did it raise the project's reputational cost? Did it protect, rather than endanger, your people?

# EMAILS & LETTERS

The following templates are starting points, not scripts. Adapt every one to your specific facts, and — this cannot be overstated — weigh the safety of every communication before you send it. In present conditions, consider carefully what is put in writing, how it is sent, who is named, and how the sender and sources are protected. Where a communication could endanger people, route it through a trusted ally or organisation, protect identities, and take security advice first. Several of these letters are best sent not by exposed villagers at all, but by allied organisations on the community's behalf.

## 8A. Email to a journalist (story pitch)

> Subject: Documented resettlement and river concerns — [project], [province]
>
> Dear [Name],
>
> I am writing on behalf of [community/organisation] regarding [project], a [type] project affecting [area]. We have assembled dated documentation of [inadequate compensation and resettlement / a defective or absent assessment / downstream impacts on fisheries and flow], and of the project's links to [financier/operator/buyer].
>
> We believe this raises issues of clear public interest: [one line on the wrong and the powerful actor responsible]. We can provide organised evidence and, with appropriate protection of identities and safety, connect you with people directly affected.
>
> The safety of those involved is our first concern, and we would want to discuss how to protect sources before proceeding. Could we find a secure way to speak?
>
> [Contact, via a secure channel]

## 8B. Objection during the assessment / consultation

> To: [DESIA / MONRE / relevant authority]
> Re: Objection concerning [project] — [reference]
>
> We, the undersigned affected community of [area], submit this objection regarding [project].
>
> 1. **Categorisation and assessment:** [State the concern — e.g., the project appears to warrant a comprehensive assessment but was treated as minor-impact; or the assessment omits cumulative and downstream effects.]
> 2. **Consultation:** [State the defect — e.g., consultation was not adequately announced / was held after decisions were effectively made / did not permit genuine objection.]
> 3. **Impacts:** [Summarise the documented impacts on land, fisheries, livelihoods, and resettlement.]
>
> We request that these concerns be recorded, that the required procedures be fully complied with, and that no compliance certificate issue until they are addressed. We reserve all rights.
>
> [Community / organisation]

## 8C. Assertion of use rights / objection to a concession

> To: [Relevant land authority / district / provincial office]
> Re: Land at [location] — assertion of existing use and objection to concession/acquisition
>
> The land at [location] has been under continuous customary use by the families of [community] for [period], and is essential to their livelihoods. We object to any concession, lease, or acquisition that disregards this existing use, and we assert the rights and entitlements of the families concerned.
>
> We attach documentation of long-standing use [list]. We request that no action be taken over this land inconsistent with these rights, that lawful compensation and resettlement be provided, and that this objection be placed on the record.
>
> [Community / organisation]

## 8D. Letter challenging inadequate compensation or resettlement

> To: [Project resettlement office / district / provincial authority]
> Re: [Project] — compensation and resettlement of [community]
>
> The compensation and resettlement being provided to the families of [community] do not meet the requirements of [the compensation and resettlement decree] in the following respects: [list — e.g., valuations below replacement cost, resettlement site lacking land or water, livelihoods not restored, entitlements promised but not delivered].
>
> We request that compensation and resettlement be brought into compliance with the decree before the project proceeds further, and that this letter be placed on the record.
>
> [Community / organisation]

## 8E. Complaint to an international lender's accountability mechanism

> To: [Accountability Mechanism of the financing institution]
> Re: Complaint concerning [project] financed by [institution]
>
> We, communities affected by [project], submit this complaint. We believe the project has breached [the institution's] environmental and social requirements, including on [assessment / meaningful consultation / land acquisition / compensation and involuntary resettlement / livelihood restoration / Indigenous peoples], in the following documented ways: [list, each tied to the relevant standard and to dated evidence].
>
> We have sought remedy through [domestic channels] without result [detail]. We request that the mechanism assess this complaint, and we can provide organised documentation. We ask that appropriate measures be taken to protect complainants from reprisal.
>
> [Community / organisation, via trusted ally]

## 8F. Letter to a foreign operator or off-taker

> To: [Company — developer / operator / power purchaser / buyer]
> Re: [Project] — environmental, resettlement, and river concerns
>
> As a company that [builds / owns / operates / purchases from] [project], you carry responsibility for its impacts and exposure to their consequences. We wish to bring to your attention documented [inadequate compensation and resettlement / downstream impacts on fisheries and flow / procedural failures], affecting the communities of [area]. [One or two specific, verified facts.]
>
> We request that you [use your influence / apply your safeguard obligations / review your involvement] to ensure lawful compensation, adequate resettlement, and mitigation, and we are ready to provide documentation.
>
> [Community / organisation, via trusted ally]

## 8G. Press release (for use by an allied organisation)

> **For release:** Communities in [area] document resettlement and river concerns over [project]
>
> [Area] — Communities affected by [project] have documented [the core wrong], including [one or two specific, verified facts]. The project is linked to [financier/operator/buyer]. Affected people are calling for [the specific demand — lawful compensation and resettlement, mitigation, review].
>
> "[Short, human quote — used only with consent and with identity protected as needed.]"
>
> Documentation is available to accredited journalists and relevant bodies through [allied organisation], with appropriate protection of sources.

## 8H. Letter to a land, river, or legal-aid organisation

> Dear [Organisation],
>
> We are a community in [area] facing [project], which threatens [our land / river / homes] through [summary]. We have begun documenting [compensation, resettlement, use, impacts] and seek your guidance and support on [land and resettlement strategy / a lender or transboundary complaint / safety].
>
> We would welcome a secure conversation about how to proceed. The safety of those involved is our first concern.
>
> [Community contact, via secure channel]

## Important Caveat

Every template above must yield to safety. In Laos a letter that would be routine elsewhere can carry real risk. Before sending anything: consider whether it should be sent by the community or by an ally on its behalf; protect the identities of those who could be endangered; use secure channels; take advice from trusted organisations; and never let the act of filing expose people to a danger out of proportion to what the filing can achieve. The letters are tools for building a record and reaching those who can act — not ends in themselves, and never worth a person's safety.
# IF YOU HAVE LITTLE TIME OR FEW RESOURCES: THE RAPID-FIRE VERSION

Not every community has months to prepare or allies already in place. Sometimes the machinery is arriving and you have days. This is the compressed version — what to do when time and resources are short. It will not do everything the full method does, but it captures the essentials and, above all, keeps people safe.

**First, protect people.** Before anything else, understand the risk. Do not do anything that exposes people to danger out of proportion to what it can achieve. In Laos this means: no open confrontation with authorities or a state-backed developer, no public protest against a government priority. The rapid actions below are chosen because they are comparatively safe and genuinely useful.

**Second, document immediately.** Whatever else you do, start creating a dated record of what exists now — the land and who uses it, the river and the fishery, the homes, the boundaries — with photographs, simple maps, and signed accounts by elders. This baseline can never be recreated once the project changes the ground, and it is the foundation of every future lever.

**Third, find out who is really behind it and who funds and buys from it.** Even a quick effort to learn whether there is an international lender, a foreign operator, or an export buyer tells you whether the strong external levers exist. If they do, that is where a longer effort should aim.

**Fourth, establish what is owed.** If people are being displaced, quickly record each household's land and what compensation and resettlement the decree requires versus what is being offered. Inadequate compensation is the most common and defensible wrong, and capturing the shortfall early is invaluable.

**Fifth, reach one trusted ally.** Contact — carefully, through a trusted channel — one land-, river-, or human-rights organisation or researcher who works on these issues. They can advise on safety, hold your documentation securely, and connect you to the external channels.

**Sixth, preserve, don't provoke.** If you cannot safely stop the immediate step, focus on preserving the record and the community's unity for a longer effort, rather than a confrontation that endangers people and changes nothing. Delay quietly where you can; document always; escalate through external channels when you are ready.

The rapid-fire version is not a lesser campaign — it is the safe, high-value core of the full method, done fast. Document, identify the money, capture what is owed, reach an ally, protect people. From that core, a fuller effort can grow when time allows.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

This whole guide is written for a tilted system, but it is worth naming plainly how tilted, and how that changes the strategy — because the degree of tilt varies even within Laos, and reading it correctly is essential.

Think of the situations you might face as a spectrum.

**The responsive end (rare here).** Occasionally a project is genuinely vulnerable even domestically: a private, mid-sized venture, not a state priority, dependent on outside money and markets, at an early stage, with clear procedural or compensation failures. Here the levers in this guide can bite hardest, and a careful, documented campaign reaching the project's financiers and buyers has a real chance of stopping or reshaping it.

**The mixed middle (common).** Most projects sit here: backed by the state or connected interests, but also dependent on international finance, foreign operators, or export markets that lie outside the government's control. Outright domestic reversal is unlikely, but the external levers are real. The realistic goals are delay, mitigation, better compensation and lawful resettlement, a reduced footprint, protection of the most vital areas, and a documented record that constrains the project or supports accountability elsewhere.

**The entrenched end (the hardest).** At this end sits the flagship state project — a strategic dam central to national energy policy, a venture tied to the most powerful interests, with domestic approval assured and no international lever exposed. Here outright stoppage is not a realistic near-term goal, and pretending otherwise endangers people. The honest strategy is to document thoroughly and safely, protect the community's rights and compensation as far as possible, engage whatever external attention exists, and build the record that may constrain the project's conduct, improve its terms, or support accountability in the future.

Placing your project on this spectrum, honestly, is the judgment that shapes everything: the goal you set, the risks you take, the levers you reach for. The gravest mistake is to treat an entrenched project as responsive — to mount open opposition to a flagship dam as though a petition could stop it — because that exposes people to serious danger for an outcome that was never available. Read the tilt correctly, set the goal accordingly, and put your strength where it can actually move something.

Two principles hold across the whole spectrum. **Safety governs everything**: the more tilted the system, the more you favour quiet documentation and external channels over domestic confrontation, and the more you protect identities and take expert advice. **Legality is your framing**: at every point, present your effort as insisting that existing law, the government's own decrees, and the standards of the project's financiers be followed — not as political opposition.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO IS CAPTURED

Sometimes what a community faces is not merely a tilted system working as designed, but corruption: a project advancing because someone with power benefits personally, because officials have been paid, or because the decision-makers and the developer are effectively the same interest. Recognising this changes your strategy, because the normal channels are compromised at the root.

The signs are recognisable. Approvals that come impossibly fast or ignore obvious problems. Officials with undisclosed stakes in the project. Land changing hands at absurd prices, or concessions granted to companies connected to the powerful. Consultations staged after the decision is made. Documents that cannot be obtained because they would reveal too much. Pressure, threats, or inducements to secure agreement.

When the local and even national institutions are captured, appealing to them is not just useless — it can be dangerous, because it signals your knowledge to the very people who benefit. The strategy must shift decisively outward. The external levers this guide emphasises — international lenders' accountability mechanisms, foreign operators' and buyers' obligations, transboundary bodies, international documentation and human-rights channels — become not merely the strongest option but essentially the only one, because they sit outside the captured system.

But documenting corruption is dangerous work, more so than documenting a compensation shortfall, because it threatens powerful people directly. The cautions in this guide apply with double force: protect identities absolutely, work through trusted intermediaries and expert organisations, keep evidence secure and preferably outside the country, and never let an individual expose themselves to expose corruption. Where possible, let established anti-corruption, human-rights, and investigative organisations, which have the protection and reach that a village does not, carry the corruption case.

The honest truth is that capture makes a project harder to stop and more dangerous to fight, and it raises the ceiling on what is realistically achievable domestically to near zero. But it also creates the project's greatest vulnerability, because a legitimate lender, operator, or buyer exposed to documented corruption faces legal and reputational consequences at home that they will go to great lengths to avoid.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

The steps in this guide are not a menu to pick from; they are one integrated method, and their power comes from how they build on each other. Target identification tells you which lever to reach for. Documentation produces the evidence every lever needs. Organising builds the unified community that can sustain the effort and protect the record. The legal, media, and external-channel work carries that evidence to the actors who can impose a cost.

Here is how a realistic Lao campaign fits together over time.

**Months 1–2: Foundation and assessment.** Work the target-identification questions and place your project honestly on the spectrum of tilt. Begin the baseline documentation immediately — it cannot wait. Learn who is behind the project, who funds it, who operates it, and who buys from it, so you know which levers exist. Establish the security practices that will govern everything.

**Months 2–4: Building the record and the core.** Deepen the documentation across all three layers, with priority on what is owed in compensation and resettlement and on any procedural failures. Form the trusted core group. Make first, careful contact with one or two allied organisations who can advise on safety, hold evidence, and connect you to external channels.

**Months 3–8: Widening and preparing the levers.** Widen the circle quietly to neighbouring affected communities and supporting networks. With expert help, prepare the external complaints your project's connections allow — the lender complaint, the approach to a foreign operator or buyer, the transboundary submission — each built on your documented record. Use any procedural filing to force responses and create delay. Begin careful media groundwork only when the evidence is solid and the risks are understood.

**Months 6–18: Escalation and endurance.** File and pursue the external complaints. Coordinate careful media to amplify them to the financiers, operators, and buyers who can move. Keep feeding every channel fresh, dated evidence as the project develops. Above all, sustain the community's unity and safety through the long haul, support anyone who is pressured, and be ready to act when a window opens — a financing decision, an operator's review, a downstream-state concern.

**Throughout: protect, document, adapt.** Two things never stop: protecting your people, and documenting everything. And one judgment is revisited constantly: is the goal you set still the right one given what you have learned about the project's tilt and its exposure?

The integration is the point. A community that documents but never reaches the external levers has evidence no one acts on. A community that reaches for the levers without documentation has nothing to act on. A community that does both but neglects safety and unity breaks under pressure. The method works when the pieces work together — evidence, unity, external reach, and safety, sustained over time.

# FINAL ASSESSMENT

This guide has tried, at every step, to tell you the truth rather than what you might wish to hear. It is worth ending by gathering that truth into a clear picture, because a community that understands its real situation makes better and safer decisions than one buoyed by false hope or paralysed by needless despair.

The hardest truth is that Laos is a tilted system, and some projects — the flagship state dams, the ventures tied to the most powerful interests — cannot realistically be stopped outright by community opposition, no matter how well organised. Pretending otherwise would be a lie, and in a place where open dissent carries grave risk, it would be a dangerous one. If you take only one thing from this guide, let it be this: read your situation honestly, set your goal to what is actually achievable, and never let anyone push the community into open confrontation that endangers people for an outcome that was never available.

But the tilted system is not the whole truth, and despair is as much a mistake as false hope. Even here, communities have changed outcomes. They have won better compensation and lawful resettlement. They have forced projects to be delayed, reshaped, and reduced. They have protected fisheries, forests, and flows that livelihoods depend on. They have built documented records that constrained a project's conduct or supported accountability later. They have done this not through protest and confrontation, which the system punishes, but through the quieter, stronger levers this guide is built around: meticulous documentation, unity, and reaching the actors outside the government's control — the lenders, the foreign operators and buyers, the transboundary bodies, the international record.

Those levers exist because the Lao development model depends on the outside world. The money, the companies, the electricity buyers, the shared river — these connect the project to standards, obligations, and reputations that a village, armed with a truthful documented record and supported by trusted allies, can actually reach. That is the opening a tilted system leaves, and it is a real one.

Your advantages are also real, even against powerful projects. You have the truth of what is happening on the ground, which the powerful must work to hide. You have the moral weight of a community defending its home and livelihood. You have, potentially, allies and external forums that the developer cannot control. And you have the one thing that no approval can erase: a documented, dated record of what existed and what was done to it, which grows more powerful with time. Used carefully, patiently, and safely, these advantages have moved outcomes before.

So be realistic, and be brave in the right way. Do not measure success only by the rare total stoppage; measure it by homes saved, compensation won, harm reduced, rights defended, and truth preserved. Protect your people above all — no project is worth a life or a freedom. Document everything. Build unity. Reach outside. And understand that even when you cannot stop a project, what you build — the record, the unity, the external attention, the accountability — is never wasted. It protects people now, it constrains what is done to them, and it becomes the foundation on which a later, larger reckoning can stand. In a tilted system, that is not defeat. It is the work, and it matters.

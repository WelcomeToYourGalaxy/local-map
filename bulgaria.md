# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In 2011 the government handed the US energy giant **Chevron** a permit to prospect for **shale gas** across a vast tract of the northeast, around **Novi Pazar** — a chance, ministers said, to break the country's dependence on Russian gas. But Bulgarians feared what the drilling would do to their **water and their farmland**, and a grassroots movement, **"Fracking Free Bulgaria,"** rose to meet it. Through the autumn and into January the protests grew, from Sofia and Plovdiv and Varna to **eleven cities at once**, Bulgarians filling the squares with drums and whistles and loaves of bread held aloft to stand for the land itself. The government, which had championed the project, buckled before the sheer scale of it. On **17 January 2012 it revoked Chevron's permit**, and the next day the **National Assembly voted, across every party, for a total ban on hydraulic fracturing** — making Bulgaria the **second country in the world** to ban it. From a permit to a nationwide ban in a matter of weeks, an ordinary people's movement had stopped a global energy giant and outlawed the whole activity.

Fracking Free Bulgaria drew on tools available to communities across the country — and, above all, on the sheer scale and speed of a movement that filled the squares of eleven cities at once. A **legislative ban** by the National Assembly can outlaw a whole activity, as it outlawed fracking the day after the permit fell. **Local councils and referendums** can bar a project locally. The **courts** can strike a permit or decision, and protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. And a **free press** carries a movement nationwide. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The win rode a **near-universal fear** — for water and farmland — that united the country as few issues do; a fight without that near-universal stake would be harder to swell to eleven cities. Bulgaria's **governance is weak and its institutions corruptible**, so a win must be watched and defended. And a **legislative ban can be revisited** or eroded by a later government, and pressure to revisit the fracking ban has recurred. So aim for what this lever can deliver around a cause that can swell to a nationwide scale, and defend the win once you have it.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO'S CAPTURED

Bulgaria's weak governance and corruptible institutions make capture a real risk, so this matters.

**How to spot it.** Permits granted against the public interest; a project pushed despite the fear; assessments that understate the harm; benefits to the connected while water, farmland, and communities bear the cost.

**Who's supposed to guard against it.** Bulgaria's audit and anti-corruption bodies examine public money and conduct. The **courts** can strike a flawed permit. The **National Assembly** can ban the activity. **Local councils and referendums** can bar it. And Bulgaria's **grassroots movements, civil society, and free press** — and, ultimately, a mass movement — can force a reversal.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — how a permit was granted against the public interest, who benefits, what the harm is — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect. Route serious evidence to the courts, the audit bodies, and trusted journalists rather than making accusations you can't back, and build the mass movement that is the decisive force here. Pair the exposure with the legislative-ban and court case.

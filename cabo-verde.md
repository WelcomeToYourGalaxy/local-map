# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN CABO VERDE

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Cabo Verde is a stable, functioning Atlantic democracy with few natural resources beyond its climate and its extraordinary beauty, and it has staked its future on tourism — a choice that has brought real threats: sprawling resort development on islands like Sal and Boa Vista, much of it planned before any conservation plan and now overlapping the very protected areas meant to safeguard nature; and illegal sand mining that has stripped beaches bare, salinated farmland, and contaminated groundwater across several islands. Yet Cabo Verde also hosts one of the largest loggerhead turtle nesting populations in the world, and — thanks in large part to a genuinely effective conservation movement working with government and communities — the number of nests has been rising. There is no single landmark case of a community stopping a megaproject outright. But here, more than in most countries in this series, the levers genuinely work, and communities and their allies have real, demonstrable wins to build on.

A community in Cabo Verde facing a harmful project — a resort rising over a nesting beach, sand vanishing from a shore — works with a genuinely usable toolkit, in a democracy whose tools actually function. A major project must, in principle, undergo an **environmental impact assessment** — imperfect, but a real formal channel to engage. The **protected-area system of natural and marine reserves** — including the turtle reserves — carries legal protection a community can invoke against harmful development. The **courts** are open and functioning. A **free press and democratic accountability** let a community raise a matter and hold the deciders — the central government, the municipalities — to account; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. And **deep European and international partnerships** add funding, standards, and scrutiny. Behind them all stands the distinctive Cabo Verdean lever: a **strong, effective conservation civil society** with a real track record — the movement that built the reserves and turned the tide for the turtles. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The tools work, but imperfectly: the assessment studies are not always robust, and the decision-making around them lacks transparency. Tourism-development zones were drawn before conservation plans and now overlap protected areas, creating conflicts that favour development. And illegal sand mining, banned since the late 1990s, persists because authorities sometimes look away, and because for the impoverished women who do much of the harvesting it is a matter of survival — so the honest response is not only enforcement but alternative livelihoods, never mere punishment of the poor. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a project assessed, conditioned, or moved; a protected area defended; a beach and its turtles saved — in a democracy where, as the conservation movement has shown, the levers actually win ground.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You win here by combining a documented, legally grounded case with the strong conservation civil society and sustained public pressure, in a democracy that genuinely responds. # HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

A Cabo Verdean development runs through several genuinely reachable tracks. The **central government and the municipalities** plan and approve development — the tourism zones, the permits — and are accountable, in a democracy, to the assessment, the courts, the press, and the voters. The **environmental ministry and the assessment process** must, in principle, evaluate major projects. The **protected-area system of natural and marine reserves** carries legal protections a community and the NGOs can invoke. And the **conservation civil society, the free press, and the international partners** are real forces. Map who decides a project's fate, at which level, before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A major project should undergo an environmental assessment, and — on the coast or near a reserve — must reckon with the protected-area system. That assessment is your first entry point, imperfect though it is: the moment to document the harm, invoke the reserve, and press for conditions or refusal. Beyond it lie the courts, the protected-area protections, the press, and the conservation movement's pressure. Even a development-favouring process can be moved by a documented case and an organised public.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the permission: which government or municipality approves the project, on what assessment, and whether it overlaps a protected area. Follow the money: who is developing, and what European or international funding and standards apply. Follow the protection: which natural or marine reserve the project touches. The leverage lies in the assessment, the reserve designations, the courts, and the conservation movement.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

Cabo Verde has real watchers: its environmental ministry and assessment process, its protected-area system, the courts, a free press, the strong conservation NGOs, and the European and international partners. These are the institutions a case is built to engage — and, where they fall short of their own standards, to hold to account.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Ask five questions. **What is the harm?** — the resort over a nesting beach, the sand stripped from a shore, the reserve overlapped. **What does it need?** — an environmental assessment, a permit, a clearance near a reserve. **Which protection does it touch?** — a natural or marine reserve, a turtle beach. **Who is the coalition?** — the conservation NGOs, the affected community, the press. **Is survival driving the harm?** — for sand mining especially, whether alternative livelihoods must be part of the answer. Pick the fight where the reserve or assessment offers the clearest handle and the movement is ready to join.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Build the case in layers. **Layer one: the harm** — the resort's footprint over the nesting beach, the sand mining's damage to the shore, farmland, and groundwater, the reserve overlapped. **Layer two: the protection and the process** — the reserve designation the project offends, the assessment it must satisfy and where it fell short, the law the sand mining breaks. **Layer three: the coalition and the livelihood** — the conservation NGOs to join, the courts or press to engage, and, for sand mining, the alternative livelihoods that must accompany enforcement. A case grounded in the reserve protections and the assessment — carried by the proven conservation movement — is one Cabo Verde's institutions take seriously.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

Gather the **harm record**: the project's assessment and permit, the reserve boundaries it overlaps, the sand-mining damage to beach, farmland, and groundwater, the turtle-nesting data. Gather the **protection record**: the natural and marine reserve designations, the assessment requirements, the sand-mining ban. Gather the **coalition record**: the conservation NGOs, their track record, the affected community, the press. Gather the **livelihood record**: for sand mining, who harvests and why, and what alternatives exist. Cabo Verde's openness makes most of this genuinely obtainable.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION

Cabo Verde's distinctive strength is its conservation movement. Build the coalition around it: the **strong conservation NGOs** with their proven track record, the **affected community and its residents**, the **fishers and turtle-monitoring volunteers**, and the **free press**. Where illegal sand mining is the harm, build with the harvesters too, seeking alternative livelihoods rather than only enforcement, so the coalition answers the poverty that drives the damage. Join the movement that has already shown these levers work, and make the shared cause of nature and livelihood the ground of the coalition.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

Tailor the case to each audience. **For the assessment authority**: here is the harm, the reserve it overlaps, and the corrected study or condition needed. **For the protected-area bodies and NGOs**: here is a reserve your protections cover, and here is the threat. **For the courts**: here is a project that breaches the reserve protections or the sand-mining ban. **For the press and public**: here is a nesting beach or a shore at stake, and a movement that has saved such places before. **For the international partners**: here is a project that strains the standards your funding carries. **The golden rule**: ground the case in the reserves and the assessment, carry it with the conservation movement, and — for survival-driven harm — pair enforcement with alternatives, so the fight wins nature and livelihood alike.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN INDIA

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

*A practical manual for gram sabhas, Adivasi and forest-dwelling communities, farmers, fishing communities, and citizens' groups facing a mine, a coal or thermal power plant, a dam, a smelter, a port, an expressway, or an industrial estate on land they depend on. It explains how India's approval system actually works, where the real openings are, and how to use the levers Indian law and India's own history of resistance provide. It can't promise any project can be stopped — it offers a method that gives you the best chance.*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Hold onto one fact before anything else, because it's true and it's Indian: **a village assembly of a few thousand Adivasis once voted down one of the world's largest mining companies, and the Supreme Court made that vote binding.**

That's the record of **Niyamgiri**. Vedanta Resources and the state-owned Odisha Mining Corporation planned to mine bauxite from a hill in Odisha that the **Dongria Kondh** — a particularly vulnerable tribal group of some eight thousand people — worship as their deity, Niyam Raja, to feed a refinery built below at Lanjigarh. The community resisted for years, with activists and international allies beside them. On 18 April 2013, the Supreme Court of India did something remarkable: it didn't decide the question itself, it handed it to the people whose forest and god were at stake, ordering that the affected **gram sabhas** — the village assemblies — convene independently, free of company or government pressure, and decide whether the mine would violate their rights, with their decision binding. In what became India's first environmental referendum, all twelve villages voted, unanimously, **no**. The environment ministry then refused the clearance. A tribe of eight thousand had defeated a company owned by a billionaire — using Indian law, in Indian courts.

What makes Niyamgiri winnable anywhere is the toolkit underneath. India's Supreme Court has held that the **right to a clean and healthy environment is part of the fundamental right to life** (Article 21). There's an environmental-clearance law with a **mandatory public hearing**; a specialist **National Green Tribunal** built to hear environmental cases fast and cheaply; a **Forest Rights Act** that gives Adivasi and forest-dwelling gram sabhas real power over their forests; a powerful **Right to Information Act**; an activist higher judiciary reachable through public interest litigation; and a free, fierce press; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. Few communities anywhere get to fight with a kit this full. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly: not every community has a Niyamgiri, and the system is usually tilted hard toward the project. Clearances get granted over serious defects; public hearings are held far from the affected village or quietly exempted; the government often champions the very projects communities fight; activists have faced surveillance, defamation suits, and worse; and the clearance rules themselves have been repeatedly diluted. India shows both what a determined community and India's remarkable laws can achieve — and how hard the fight is when the state backs the project.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

A big project looks unstoppable, but it's a chain of approvals, and a chain breaks at its weakest link. A large Indian project needs its **environmental clearance**, resting on an environmental impact assessment and a **genuine public hearing**; on forest land it needs **forest clearance** and, where Adivasi and forest-dwellers' rights are involved, the **consent of the gram sabhas** under the Forest Rights Act; it needs land, now hedged with consent and social-impact rules; it must survive the **National Green Tribunal**, the High Courts, and the Supreme Court; and it must avoid a watchful press. Every one of those is a link you can grab. **Find the one closest to your hand and pull.**

## Be Honest About Which Fight You're In

Before you spend a year on this, work out your real situation:

- **If Adivasi or forest-dwellers' rights are engaged, you have India's strongest shield** — gram sabha consent under the Forest Rights Act, the Niyamgiri lever.
- **If the clearance is flawed or the public hearing was a sham, and you can reach the NGT, the tools genuinely bite** — the green court routinely stays clearances.
- **If the state backs a strategic project and the process was clean, it's hard** — aim honestly at delay, conditions, and exposure, and protect your people, because activists here have faced real intimidation.

Read the whole guide once before you move. Two habits run through it: **use the rights the law gives you** — the public hearing, gram sabha consent, the NGT, the right to information — and **run every front at once**: the documents, the hearing, the NGT, the courts, the movement, the press.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

To break the chain you have to see it — who decides, how a project moves, and who's watching.

## Who Actually Decides

The core decision is the **environmental clearance**. Large "Category A" projects are cleared centrally by the **Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEF&CC)** through an expert appraisal committee; smaller "Category B" projects by the **State Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA)**. The **State Pollution Control Board (SPCB)** runs the public hearing and issues the pollution consents. On forest land, **forest clearance** under the Forest Conservation Act is separate — and the **gram sabha** holds consent rights under the Forest Rights Act. Know which body holds the decision you can contest, and aim there.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

**Screening and scoping.** The project is classified and the scope of its study set. *Your opening:* a project that should face full assessment being under-classified or exempted.

**The EIA and the public hearing.** The proponent's consultants prepare the environmental impact assessment; a **draft EIA** is made public, and the **State Pollution Control Board holds a public hearing** in the affected area. *Your opening — a mandatory, time-bound step:* get the draft EIA, read it for flaws, and appear at the public hearing to put your objections on the record. Watch for the classic abuses — a hearing held far from the village, in the wrong language, or quietly exempted.

**Appraisal and clearance.** The appraisal committee recommends, and MoEF&CC or SEIAA grants or refuses the clearance with conditions. *Your opening:* a clearance built on a defective EIA or a hollow hearing can be challenged.

**Forest and gram sabha consent.** On forest land, forest clearance and gram sabha consent are separate hurdles. *Your opening:* the gram sabha's power — the Niyamgiri lever.

**Construction and operation.** *Your opening:* breaches of clearance conditions.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Dig up three things. **Who's behind it and who owns them** — company records set the exposure. **What it still needs** — the clearance not granted or under challenge, forest consent not obtained, land not acquired, financing not closed: whatever's missing is the soft spot. **Who's paying** — banks and public financiers with their own environmental and social standards.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

Each is an ally or an arena. **MoEF&CC, SEIAA, and the SPCB** run the clearance system and can be pressed, publicly, to enforce it. The **National Green Tribunal** — the specialist green court — hears challenges to clearances and consents and can stay them. The **High Courts** (Article 226) and the **Supreme Court** (Article 32) hear public interest litigation on the fundamental right to environment. The **gram sabha** holds forest-rights consent. The **Comptroller and Auditor General** examines public money. And India's **dense environmental and Adivasi-rights movements, its public-interest lawyers, and a fierce press** are the forces that reach furthest — the combination that carried Niyamgiri.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "the project" is weak. Being specific — this clearance, this flaw, this right — is strong. Answer five questions in writing.

**1. What is it, and who's behind it?** Name the project, the company, its owners, its financiers.

**2. Where is it, and what does it touch?** Get the boundaries and lay them over forest land, Adivasi and forest-dwellers' habitat, a river, a coast, farmland, a protected area. **Forest and gram-sabha rights are India's strongest shield**, so this map is your most important document.

**3. What stage is it at, and what's the next deadline?** Is the draft EIA out and the public hearing scheduled? Is the clearance granted (starting the NGT's six-month clock)? These openings are time-bound.

**4. What does it still need that it lacks?** A clearance not granted, forest consent not obtained, land not acquired, financing not closed. The missing piece is the soft spot.

**5. Who else is affected, and who already opposes it?** The gram sabhas, the affected villages, local groups, environmental lawyers, the movement. You're rarely alone — Niyamgiri united twelve villages.

**What this looks like in real life.** A community learns a mine is planned on a forested hill their people depend on and worship. The five questions yield: a named mining company behind it; a site on forest land where gram-sabha rights apply (the shield); a public hearing due and a clearance pending; a project still needing clearance, forest consent, and land; and a coalition of the affected villages, lawyers, and the movement. That's the Niyamgiri pattern — and the position from which Niyamgiri was won.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

The difference between a community that can fight and one that can't is proof. Build it in three layers.

**Layer one: the paper trail.** Get the documents the decision rests on — the draft and final EIA, the public-hearing record, the clearance and its conditions, the forest-clearance file, the gram-sabha records. The **Right to Information Act** forces out what isn't public within 30 days. Then read the EIA hard for flaws — an understated impact, an ignored alternative, a cumulative effect skipped, a hearing that never genuinely reached the village.

**Layer two: the ground truth.** Dated, located photographs and video of the site, the forest, the river — baseline images before any damage most of all — and, carefully, the testimony of affected families and the gram sabha's own record of its decision.

**Layer three: the map of rights.** Lay the project over forest land, Adivasi habitat, and protected areas, so each shield is visible. Walking into the NGT or a hearing with the EIA, the conditions, and that map is what makes them act.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

- **The EIA, the public-hearing record, the clearance and conditions, the forest file** — from MoEF&CC/SEIAA and the SPCB; force out the rest under the RTI Act (8A).
- **Forest-rights and gram-sabha records** — from the gram sabha and the tribal-affairs authorities.
- **Ownership and financing** — company records and lender documents.
- **The rights map** — forest land, Adivasi habitat, and protected areas laid over the site.
- **Dated site evidence and the gram sabha's record** — photographs marked with date and place, and the assembly's minutes.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION

Documents give you a case; people give you power — and India's history, from Chipko's tree-hugging to Niyamgiri's twelve villages, is a history of communities winning through unity. Build your coalition in four moves — and safely.

**Start with the gram sabha and a tight core.** Where forest rights apply, the gram sabha is both the heart of the community and a rights-holder in law. Build around it, with the families most affected and a few trusted organisers.

**Widen the circle.** Pull in the neighbouring villages and gram sabhas, the local groups, the wider district. Hold meetings where people can see the evidence and understand their rights, so the community speaks with one voice at the hearing, before the NGT, and in the press. Niyamgiri's strength was twelve villages as one.

**Bring in the allies and experts — early.** Reach India's environmental and Adivasi-rights lawyers and organisations, the scientists who can dismantle a weak EIA, and the national movement. The NGT is accessible enough that a supported community can bring its own case.

**Build to last, and safely.** These fights run for years. Build clear roles, a secure shared archive, a way to decide together, and a plan for the financial, emotional, and — in India, real — legal and physical risks. Take defamation suits and intimidation seriously, protect the vulnerable, and lean on lawyers who know how to shield activists.

# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES, THE GREEN TRIBUNAL & FOREST RIGHTS

Law is arguably India's sharpest lever, because of a specialist court built for the environment and a Constitution that makes the environment a fundamental right.

## The National Green Tribunal

The most important legal fact for an Indian community is the **National Green Tribunal (NGT)** — a specialist environmental court, built to be fast, cheap, and accessible. It hears challenges to environmental clearances, forest clearances, and pollution-board consents, and it **routinely stays clearances**. It's deliberately open: **any person interested in protecting the environment can file** — you don't have to prove you're personally harmed — using a simple application template, and you can even argue your own case without an advocate, though a lawyer helps. There's a hard rule to know: applications generally must be filed **within six months** of the decision, so move quickly. Name the MoEF&CC and the project proponent as parties. The NGT has repeatedly overruled the environment ministry and stayed defective clearances — it is, for most communities, the fastest, sharpest weapon.

## The Constitutional Courts and PIL

India's Supreme Court has read the **right to a clean and healthy environment into Article 21**, the fundamental right to life. That opens the constitutional courts: **public interest litigation** in the **High Court (Article 226)** or the **Supreme Court (Article 32)**, with relaxed standing, so a public-spirited person or group can bring the case for the community and the environment. These courts can quash a clearance and order the law enforced.

## Forest Rights and Gram Sabha Consent

The **Forest Rights Act 2006** is the Niyamgiri lever. On forest land, it recognises the rights of Adivasi and forest-dwelling communities, and their **gram sabhas' consent** can be decisive — the Supreme Court made a gram sabha vote binding at Niyamgiri, and the tribunal has sent clearances back where villages refused consent. Convene the gram sabha properly, record its decision, and assert it. The **Right to Information Act** is the everyday tool that extracts the record every challenge is built on.

## The Honest Limits

Straight talk: the NGT and the courts are real and have stopped projects, but the NGT's orders are sometimes poorly enforced by state authorities, its benches are few and far from remote areas, and the six-month clock is strict; clearances are granted over defects and public hearings bypassed; the government champions strategic projects; and the clearance rules keep being diluted. So use the strong tools — the NGT, gram sabha consent, PIL — but pair them with the movement and the press, protect your people, and count a stayed or quashed clearance, delay, and exposure as wins alongside a clean stop.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

A stack of documents wins nothing until you aim it.

**A document becomes a case for the NGT.** Not "the project is bad" but *"the EIA understated the impact on this forest and the public hearing never genuinely reached our village — the clearance is unlawful and should be stayed."*

**A right becomes a shield.** *"This is our forest land; under the Forest Rights Act our gram sabha's consent is required, and it has said no."*

**Article 21 becomes a constitutional claim.** *"This threatens our right, and the public's right, to a clean and healthy environment under Article 21."*

**The harm becomes a national story.** *"A sacred hill and a forest a people have always lived from — for a mine they were never truly asked about."*

Match it to the venue: the flaws and the clearance to the NGT; the constitutional violation to the High Court or Supreme Court; the consent to the gram sabha record; the story to the press.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY

In India, national and international attention is a real lever — Niyamgiri became a global cause, and that reach both pressured the institutions and protected the people fighting. A fierce press and strong movements make the media front central.

**Lead with people and place, not procedure.** Not "the EIA was procedurally deficient" but "the sacred hill, the forest, the water our people have always depended on — to be destroyed for a mine we were never truly asked about." Use your strongest material: dated photographs, the map of the project over the forest, the gram sabha's stand, and — carefully — human testimony.

**Work several audiences at once.** Local and national press build political pressure; environmental and rights networks amplify; international media reach the company's investors and the world. Make it easy: captioned photos, accurate summaries, a map, strong voices.

**Two hard rules.** Accuracy above all — one exaggeration can be used against you (and can invite a defamation suit). And protect people — weigh what you publish and shield the vulnerable.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Make them your own; keep them factual and calm; keep copies; and protect anyone at risk.

**8A — RTI request for the clearance file (to MoEF&CC / SEIAA / SPCB).**
*Subject: Request under the Right to Information Act — [project].*
"Under the Right to Information Act, we, residents of [place], request the draft and final EIA, the public-hearing record, and the clearance and its conditions for [project] at [location]. Please provide these within 30 days. [Contact.]"

**8B — Objection at the public hearing.**
*Subject: Objection in the public hearing for [project].*
"We, residents of [place], object to the clearance of [project]: [the EIA fails to assess the impact on our forest / water / the cumulative effects; this hearing has not genuinely reached the affected village]. We ask that these be recorded and the clearance refused or a proper assessment required. [Attach evidence.]"

**8C — Application/appeal to the National Green Tribunal.**
*Subject: Application challenging the environmental clearance for [project].*
"We apply to the National Green Tribunal to challenge the clearance granted to [project] on [date], on the grounds that [the EIA was inadequate / the public hearing was defective / forest rights were ignored], and we seek a stay. We name the MoEF&CC and [proponent] as parties. [Attach the clearance, the EIA and our evidence.]"

**8D — Asserting gram sabha consent (Forest Rights Act).**
*Subject: Forest rights and gram sabha consent — [project] at [location].*
"The land affected by [project] is our forest land. Under the Forest Rights Act our gram sabha's consent is required for decisions affecting it. Our gram sabha, convened on [date], has [not consented / resolved against the project]. We enclose the resolution and call for the project to be halted. [Contact.]"

**8E — Requesting a PIL (to a public-interest lawyer).**
*Subject: Request for a public interest litigation — [project].*
"Our community faces [project], which threatens [our forest / water / rights]. We've gathered [the EIA, the clearance, the gram sabha record, photographs] and believe there are grounds for a PIL on the Article 21 right and for an NGT challenge. We ask for your help and to work as partners. [Contact and evidence summary.]"

**8F — Letter to the press.**
*Subject: [Community] moves to protect [hill / forest / river] from [project].*
"For generations the people of [place] have lived from — and revered — [the hill / forest / river] at [location]. Now [project], by [company], threatens to [mine / clear / drown] it, and we were never truly asked. We can provide photographs, documents, a map and — carefully — interviews. [Contact.]"

**8G — To a bank or financier.**
*Subject: Environmental and rights risk in [project].*
"We understand your institution may be financing [project] at [location], which [violates forest rights / faces active NGT challenge / threatens a protected forest]. We ask you to weigh this against your own environmental and social standards. [Contact.]"

**8H — Rallying the community.**
*Subject: We can stop this — here's how.*
"[Project] threatens [our forest / hill / river]. A village assembly once voted down one of the world's biggest mining companies, and the Supreme Court made it binding — that is Niyamgiri, and it is Indian. With our gram sabha, the green tribunal, the courts, and the movement behind us, we have a real chance. Please join us — carefully, protecting one another. [Contact.]"

# IF YOU HAVE LITTLE TIME OR FEW RESOURCES: THE RAPID-FIRE VERSION

If you must move fast and lean, do these six things, in order:

1. **Nail down the project and its footprint, and look for the forest/gram-sabha shield.** If it's on forest land or Adivasi habitat, your gram sabha's consent may be your strongest lever.
2. **Find the public hearing and appear at it, in writing (8B); get the draft EIA and read it.**
3. **File RTI requests for the clearance file (8A).** They gather your proof and put officials on notice.
4. **Photograph everything, with dates and places; convene and record the gram sabha (8D).**
5. **Watch the NGT's six-month clock and file there (8C) — or get a lawyer.** The green court is accessible and routinely stays clearances.
6. **Go public and find your movement (8F) — safely.** National and international attention pressures the institutions and protects your people.

Even lean, those six use your rights, protect the deadlines, build the record, and start a coalition.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

India's tools are real, but the field is often tilted, and you must plan for it.

**When the state backs a strategic project.** The government often champions the very project you oppose, and can dilute the rules to ease it through. Don't fold: use the NGT, the constitutional courts, and gram sabha consent, keep the movement intact, and make the state's backing of a flawed project a public cost.

**When the public hearing is a formality.** A hearing held far from the village, in the wrong language, or quietly exempted is not a reason to disengage but a defect that can void a clearance — document exactly how it failed.

**When the NGT's orders aren't enforced.** The tribunal can stay a clearance, but state authorities sometimes drag their feet. Pair the order with the press, the constitutional courts, and sustained pressure to make it stick.

**When activists are targeted.** Surveillance, defamation suits, and worse are real. Take them seriously, protect the vulnerable, work through the courts and institutions, and use national and international attention as a shield.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO'S CAPTURED

India has serious, documented problems with the capture of clearances by powerful interests, so this matters.

**How to spot it.** Clearances granted over the technical evidence; a public hearing engineered to exclude the affected; an EIA that reads as written for the proponent; officials moving between industry and the regulator; land or contracts steered to the connected; opaque, inflated public spending.

**Who's supposed to guard against it.** The **NGT and the constitutional courts** can quash a tainted clearance. The **Central Bureau of Investigation and the anti-corruption agencies** investigate graft. The **Comptroller and Auditor General** examines public money. And India's **investigative press** has repeatedly exposed captured clearances.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — how the process was bent, who benefits, what the law required — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect. Don't make public accusations you can't back; defamation suits are used against Indian activists. Route serious evidence to the proper bodies and trusted journalists, protect insiders and witnesses, and pair the exposure with the legal challenge and the public campaign.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

Don't run these steps one after another — run them together. The documents build your proof; pinning down the rights and the clearance stage aims it; organising builds the muscle; the NGT and the courts press the case; the media raises the cost and protects your people. Together they multiply. One at a time, they stall.

**Early on (weeks 1–8):** nail down the project, its footprint, and any forest/gram-sabha rights; find the public hearing and appear at it; file RTI requests; photograph the baseline; convene and record the gram sabha; get a lawyer before the NGT's six-month clock.

**In the thick of it (months–years):** file at the NGT to stay a defective clearance; prepare a PIL on the Article 21 right; assert gram sabha consent; deepen the coalition and the expert critique; open the media front.

**The long haul (years):** carry the case through the courts; keep the movement and the national and international attention alive; hold the line for a clearance stayed or quashed, delay, conditions, or an outright stop — and keep your people safe throughout.

# FINAL ASSESSMENT

Stopping a destructive project in India can be done — a village assembly of eight thousand Adivasis voted down one of the world's largest mining companies and the Supreme Court made it binding — and the same story is honest about the price: years of resistance and a system usually tilted toward the project. Hold both. India's tools are real and, in parts, unusually strong: a fundamental right to a healthy environment; a mandatory public hearing; a specialist green tribunal that routinely stays clearances and that a community can approach itself; a Forest Rights Act that puts real power in the gram sabha; a powerful right to information; and a fierce press. And the constraints are real: clearances granted over defects, hearings bypassed, a state that backs its projects, rules diluted, weak enforcement, and genuine risk to activists.

Here's the discipline that wins. Find the forest and gram-sabha shield, and the flaw in the clearance, and pry loose the record under the RTI Act. Appear at the public hearing. File at the National Green Tribunal within six months to stay a defective clearance. Assert your gram sabha's consent. Take the Article 21 right to the constitutional courts. Carry the story to the nation and the world, and protect your people. Brace for a long fight, and count a clearance stayed or quashed, delay, and exposure as wins alongside a clean stop. That's what Niyamgiri did — twelve villages as one, the courts, and the world's attention together. So can you.

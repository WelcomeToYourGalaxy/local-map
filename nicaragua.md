# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In 2013, the government granted a Chinese company a **fifty-year concession** to cut a **Grand Interoceanic Canal** across the country — some **two hundred and seventy-eight kilometres** from the Pacific to the Caribbean, straight through **Lake Nicaragua**, the nation's great freshwater reserve — with sweeping powers to expropriate land along its path. It was one of the largest megaprojects ever proposed in the Americas. But the people who lived on that land — **campesinos in the country's centre and south** — did not consent to being swept aside. Over the following years they **marched roughly a hundred times** against the concession, built a national movement, brought legal challenges, and refused, again and again, to give up their land. The **canal was never built. Not a single stretch was dug.** Farmers in rubber boots had, in large part, faced down a megaproject the government had staked its prestige on — and kept their land, their water, and their say.

The campesino movement against the canal drew on tools available to communities in Nicaragua — though today every one must be weighed against grave risk. Its bedrock was a refusal to consent to displacement: a community that will not sell or surrender its land is a practical, powerful obstacle to any project. **Land rights and titles**, where they hold, protect that ground in law. The **sovereignty-and-water argument** — a project drowning the nation's great freshwater reserve — is a powerful public case. The **courts** can be tried, where they still function. And **international human-rights and environmental bodies**, reachable through the diaspora and the press, can carry a cause past a closed space. But these tools have grave limits now, and this guide states them plainly. The canal's collapse was **not the movement's doing alone**: the project also foundered on its own **financing** — the Chinese backer's fortune collapsed — so the movement's pressure met a project already faltering, and a better-funded scheme would have been far harder to stop. Far more seriously, **the space to organise has since narrowed to almost nothing.** In the years after the 2018 uprising and its crushing, the government moved to **crush independent movements, jail and exile leaders, and outlaw civil-society groups**; peaceful protest that was possible during the canal fight can now bring **arrest, exile, or worse.** This guide therefore carries a warning it means with full weight: **in today's Nicaragua, protecting yourself and your community comes first.** So aim for what is realistically safe and possible, and never at the cost of your safety, because the openness that let the canal be marched against a hundred times is gone.

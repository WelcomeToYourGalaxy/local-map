# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN BELGIUM

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the cities, land, and air of Belgium, and one long fight in Antwerp showed the country that citizens can not only stop a destructive plan but redraw it. For decades, the government planned to close the Antwerp ring road — a motorway carrying a quarter of a million vehicles a day — with a huge new crossing, and the first design featured the **Lange Wapper**, a towering two-tiered viaduct that would have loomed over the city and poured its fumes onto the neighbourhoods below. Citizens fought back, but not only with "no." Action groups arose — one focused on the air people breathed, calling itself "Breathless"; one that proposed a smarter tunnel route instead of the viaduct; and, later, one made of architects, engineers, and teachers who proposed to **roof the entire ring road** and reclaim the space above it for parks and homes. They forced a **referendum in 2009**, and the people of Antwerp rejected the viaduct. Years of campaigning, lawsuits, and counter-proposals followed, until in 2017 the government, the city, and the citizen groups signed a pact to **cover the ring, reroute the traffic, and heal the city the motorway had cut in two**. The Lange Wapper was never built. Something better is being built instead. The details are worth setting down, because they show how such a fight is won. The plan to close the Antwerp ring had been on the table since the late 1990s, run by a public agency as a public-private partnership, and its first design crossed the river and the docks on a great curved double-deck viaduct that took its name, the Lange Wapper, from a giant of Antwerp folklore. The action groups did not simply protest it: one gathered the tens of thousands of signatures needed to force a binding popular consultation, another proposed boring a tunnel through the port instead, and a third, formed a few years later by architects and engineers, proposed covering the open trench of the ring entirely and building a new district of parks on the roof. When the vote came in October 2009, the city rejected the viaduct, and after years of further struggle the government, the city, and the groups signed a pact in 2017 to cover the ring and reweave the neighbourhoods it had severed.

The Antwerp story teaches two things about defending land and life here — including its honest limits, which this guide states from the outset. The first is the pattern that runs through every guide: not a single silver bullet, but the combination of every lever — the affected community, the documentation, the coalition, the legal challenge, and the country's attention — pressed together. The second is distinctly Belgian, and it turns opposition into design. **In a wealthy democracy with real civic tools, citizens can not only block a destructive project with the referendum, the courts, and public pressure, but can propose a better alternative and force it into being — trading a fight against something for a fight for something.** That power — the local referendum, the health-and-livability argument, the expert citizen groups, and above all the constructive counter-proposal — is Belgium's distinctive lever, and this guide is built around it. What made the Antwerp movement work as a model, and what a later campaign can copy, was the way it refused to be merely negative. It is easy for authorities to dismiss opposition as obstruction, as people who want nothing built; it is far harder to dismiss citizens who arrive with a fully worked design, drawn by their own architects and engineers, that plainly serves the city better than the official plan. By pairing the machinery of refusal — the referendum, the lawsuits, the protests — with the machinery of design — a credible alternative the public could rally to — the Antwerp groups changed the question from whether to build to what to build, and on that ground they could win.

But the same story carries honest warnings, and this guide will not hide them. Antwerp was not a clean stop but a **redesign** — the ring is still being completed, in a much-improved form, but completed, and critics argue the final project kept more of the concrete and less of the promise than the pact implied. The win took the better part of **twenty years** of relentless effort. And it was won in a **wealthy, well-institutioned democracy** — with a referendum tool, strong courts, expert citizens, and public money for alternatives — advantages a community in a poorer or more closed country cannot assume. It is worth being candid about how much of the Antwerp example depends on those advantages, because it shapes how far the lesson travels. A community here fights with tools much of the world lacks: a legal right to force a local vote, courts that will actually annul an unlawful permit, public money and public bodies willing to fund and study a citizens’ alternative, and a deep pool of professionals ready to design one. The strategy in this guide — block, and design a better plan — works in Belgium partly because the ground is so favourable, and a reader elsewhere should take the approach while understanding that its full power rests on institutions Belgium happens to possess. This guide is written with both truths in view: the real, distinctive power of Belgian citizens who block and redesign — and the partial wins, the long timelines, the contested implementation, and the favourable conditions that qualify it.

Most communities do not know HOW to make that power effective. They face a project and assume "the government has decided and nothing can be done," and never organise, never document, never force the referendum, never propose the alternative. They generate private grievance without a public claim. The gap between a community that reshapes a project and one that watches it built is rarely resources — Belgium's are considerable. It is method: knowing how to organise the groups, argue the health and livability, force the referendum and the courts, and, distinctively, put a better plan on the table.

**This guide shows what actually works — inside the Belgian system as it is.**

## The Strategic Framework

Effective campaigns follow the same underlying pattern, whether the goal is to stop a motorway, a development, an incinerator, or an industrial project:

```
STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION  (find the decision, the process, and the leverage)
        |
STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION          (build proof of the harm and the flaws)
        |
STEP 3: LOCAL OPPOSITION        (organise the groups and propose the alternative)
        |
STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES        (the referendum, the permits, and the courts)
        |
STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY         (make it public)
```

These steps operate **simultaneously, not in sequence**. Documentation feeds the legal case and the public argument at once; the citizen groups organise and design; a referendum drive or an alternative plan is a headline and a headline builds the movement. The community that runs all five together, each reinforcing the others, is the one that wins — a project stopped, quashed, voted down, or redesigned into something better. The Antwerp groups did exactly this — they documented the harm, organised, forced the referendum, fought in court, and, crucially, put a better plan on the table.

## The Levers That Are Distinctly Strong Here

Every country's system has its own pressure points. Belgium's give communities a distinctive set:

- **The local referendum.** Belgian cities allow a **popular consultation**, which citizens can force with enough signatures, as Antwerp forced the vote that buried the Lange Wapper. The value of this lever is that it takes the decision out of the hands of the officials and the agencies, if only advisorily, and puts it to the people of the city, which changes the politics entirely: a government can override an objection from an action group, but it defies a clear vote of the citizens at real political cost. Gathering the signatures a consultation requires is itself a mobilising act, drawing the campaign into every neighbourhood, and a vote won turns a pressure group into the demonstrated voice of the city, a status that carries weight in every subsequent negotiation, as it did for the Antwerp groups after 2009.
- **The constructive alternative.** A community can **propose a better plan** — a tunnel instead of a viaduct, a covered ring instead of an open one — and campaign for it, turning opposition into design. This is the lever that most distinguishes the Belgian repertoire, and its logic is worth grasping: a campaign that only says no leaves the government with a choice between its own plan and nothing, and governments rarely choose nothing on a project they have committed to; a campaign that offers a credible better plan gives the government a third option, a way to change course while still building something, and gives the public a positive vision to demand rather than merely a danger to fear. The Antwerp story turned on exactly this — the citizens’ covered-ring idea became something people wanted, not just something the authorities were pressured to concede.
- **Health and livability.** The **argument from air quality, health, and urban life** is powerful and unifying, as the "Breathless" campaign showed.
- **Expert citizens.** Belgium's **architects, engineers, and professionals** lend campaigns real technical authority and credible alternatives.
- **The courts and the process.** **Court procedures and the permitting process** in a strong democracy are real levers to challenge and delay a flawed project.

## What This Guide Will Not Pretend

It would be dishonest to promise these levers deliver clean stops. Antwerp was a **redesign**, not an outright halt, and its promises were **partly eroded** in the building. The win took **twenty years**. And Belgium's **wealth and strong institutions** made it more winnable than it would be elsewhere. This guide takes those limits seriously, names them plainly, and shows you how to use the real, distinctive tools Belgium offers — the referendum, the courts, the health argument, and the constructive alternative — as the Antwerp citizens used them to bury a viaduct and build something better.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

You cannot pull a lever you cannot see. Before you spend a single weekend, map who actually decides your project's fate, at which level, and where along that path the decision — or the leverage — is weakest.

## The Levels of Decision

A Belgian development or infrastructure project runs through several tracks at once, and each is a place to intervene — and Belgium's complex, multi-level structure means you must know which level decides.

**The regions.** Belgium is federal, and much power over infrastructure, planning, and environment sits with the **regions** — Flanders, Wallonia, and Brussels — not only the federal state. Know which region and which regional agency decides, as the Antwerp ring was a Flemish matter.

**The municipalities.** **Cities and communes** hold real planning power and can hold a **local popular consultation**, as Antwerp did — a distinctive and powerful lever. The municipal level deserves particular attention because it is where the distinctive Belgian tool of the local popular consultation lives, and because cities hold real planning power that a campaign can engage directly. Even where a project is formally a regional matter, as the Antwerp ring was a Flemish one, the city and its residents can force a consultation that reshapes the politics of the whole project, and a campaign should establish early whether the municipal route to a binding vote is open to it, because it is often the single most powerful lever a Belgian community holds.

**The developers and agencies.** **Public agencies, public-private partnerships, and private developers** — like the body that ran the Antwerp ring project — propose and build the projects.

**The permits and the process.** **Environmental and planning permits** and their assessments are a real constraint and a lever, open to challenge.

**The citizens and the groups.** The **citizen action groups, the professionals, and the public** are the force that documents, organises, votes, litigates, and designs — the movement that won at Antwerp.

## The Journey of a Project

A typical project moves from a regional or municipal decision, through **planning and environmental permitting**, executed by an agency or partnership, toward construction — with the **citizens, the groups, the courts, and the voters** able to document, challenge, vote, and propose at any point. **Every one of those stages is a door you can put your foot in.** The distinctive Belgian doors are the **local referendum**, the **permit and court challenge**, and, above all, the **constructive alternative** that citizens can put forward. Find out exactly where your project stands, and, crucially, **which level decides**, **whether the process was sound**, and **whether a better alternative exists**, because those are often where a project can be stopped or transformed.

## Follow the Project, the Process, and the Alternative

Behind a project stand a proponent — an agency, a partnership, the government — and the officials who approve it. Trace who decides, who profits, and — importantly in Belgium — **whether the planning and permitting were sound**, **what the project does to the air, the health, and the life of the community**, and, distinctively, **whether a better alternative can be designed and proposed**. Two questions unlock most Belgian fights: **Was the process lawful and sound, and can a referendum be forced?** and **Can a better alternative be put on the table and won?** The process and the alternative are, together, the heart of most Belgian campaigns. The two work in tandem in a way particular to this country: the flaw in the process — the health impact ignored, the alternatives never fairly weighed — gives a campaign its legal and referendum footing, while the better alternative gives it the positive vision that wins the vote and the negotiation. A campaign that finds a legal flaw but offers no alternative may win a delay only to see the same plan re-approved; one that offers an alternative but finds no flaw may lack the leverage to force it onto the table; the Antwerp campaign, like the strongest Belgian fights, pressed both at once — the flawed viaduct and the better covered ring.

## The Overseers

Finally, know the bodies and forces that can check the deciders, because they are levers too. The **courts**, including the Council of State, which reviews administrative decisions; the **environmental and planning law**; the **local popular consultation**; the **citizen action groups and the professionals**; the **free press**; and the **public and the voters**, who buried the Lange Wapper. In Belgium, the **referendum, the courts, and the constructive alternative** are the most distinctive and decisive levers. Each is a separate front, and a project with a flawed process, a health-harming design, a forced referendum against it, and a better alternative on the table rarely proceeds as planned — as the Lange Wapper did not. You do not have to choose one front: the same case — the flawed process, the health harm, the better alternative — can go to the courts, to the voters through the referendum, to the government through the negotiations, and to the press all at once, and each front that opens strengthens the others. A Belgian campaign’s power comes not from any single lever but from pressing several together — the flawed permit before the courts, the harmful plan before the voters, and the better alternative before the government and the public — so that a government facing a lost vote, a legal challenge, and a popular better plan at the same time finds the old design untenable and the new one attractive, as Antwerp’s did.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective campaign begins by finding the exact decision that governs the project — and the leverage the community holds, which in Belgium is often the referendum, the flawed process, and the possibility of a better alternative. Vague opposition goes nowhere; a fight aimed at "the viaduct that would choke our neighbourhoods, when a tunnel or a cover would serve the city better" is a campaign — the campaign that reshaped Antwerp. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. Which level decides, and can a referendum be forced?** This is the first Belgian question. Which **region or municipality** decides, and can a **local popular consultation** be forced with signatures, as Antwerp forced the vote on the Lange Wapper? This should be among the first things a campaign establishes, because the availability of a binding-in-practice popular vote transforms the balance of power in a fight. Where the municipal route to a local consultation is open, a campaign can aim to put its case directly to the citizens and win a mandate no government can lightly ignore; where it is not, the campaign must lean harder on the courts, the alternative, and public opinion. Determining early whether a referendum can be forced, and what signature threshold it requires, tells a campaign at once how strong a hand it holds and where to concentrate its organising.

**2. Is there a better alternative?** Can a **better plan** — a tunnel instead of a viaduct, a cover instead of an open road, a different site or design — be conceived and proposed, turning opposition into design, as the Antwerp groups proposed? Give this question real weight and creative energy, because in Belgium the existence of a credible alternative is often the difference between a fight that can only delay and one that can actually win. Ask not merely whether the project is bad but what specifically would be better — a tunnel where a viaduct is planned, a cover where an open trench is planned, a different site, a smaller scale, a redesign that serves the same need with less harm — and whether the professionals to develop it can be found. A campaign that can answer this question with a serious plan holds the distinctive Belgian lever; one that cannot should make developing an alternative an early priority.

**3. What does it do to the air, the health, and the life of the community?** What **harm** does the project cause — to the air, the health, the noise, the urban life, the neighbourhoods — and what does the community stand to lose?

**4. Can the groups and the experts be organised?** Can **citizen action groups** and the **professionals** — architects, engineers, doctors — be organised, as Antwerp's were, to campaign and to design? The dual character of these groups — that they campaign and design — is worth planning for from the start, because it calls for a wider range of people than a purely oppositional campaign. A Belgian campaign benefits from organisers who can run a signature drive and a protest, from communicators who can move the press and the public, and, distinctively, from professionals who can produce a credible alternative; the Antwerp fight succeeded because it assembled all three kinds, in groups focused variously on the air, the route, and the covering. A campaign should consciously build for both roles, refusal and design, because it is the combination that wins.

**5. Who decides, who benefits, and who can be engaged?** Which agency and government are behind it, who profits; and which courts, permits, professionals, press, and public can be engaged. This maps your levers and audiences at once.

## A Worked Example

Suppose a major road or development is planned in a form that would harm a community's air and neighbourhoods, when a better design is possible.

Working the five questions: which **level** decides, and can a **referendum** be forced? Is there a **better alternative**? What **harm** to the air and health? Can the **groups and experts** be organised? And the deciders are the region and the agency, with the courts, the voters, the press, and the public as the pressure points.

Within a season you have moved from "they will build it" to a target list: document the harm and the flaws; design and propose the better alternative; organise the groups and the experts; force the referendum and the court challenge; and move the public. That is a campaign, not a complaint — and it draws directly on how Antwerp was reshaped. Notice what the exercise did: it turned a vague dread of a project into a set of concrete, winnable tasks — document the flaw, design the alternative, organise the groups, force the referendum, move the public — each tied to a specific lever that Belgium’s system genuinely provides. That reframing, from resignation to a plan built on the country’s real civic strengths, is the difference the five questions make, and two of them, whether a referendum can be forced and whether a better alternative exists, often decide how strong a hand the community holds.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on. A court, an agency, a voter, a journalist, and the public all move on evidence, not indignation — and in Belgium, the health harm, the flawed process, and the credible alternative are precisely what a campaign turns into pressure, so document them well. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Project, the Process, and the Alternative

Get the documents the decision rests on. Obtain the **plans, the permits, and the environmental and health assessments** — and scrutinise them for flaws: was the assessment honest, the process lawful, the health impact faced, the alternatives fairly weighed? And, distinctively, **develop the alternative**: the tunnel, the cover, the better design, documented well enough to be credible, as the Antwerp groups documented theirs. The gap between the flawed plan and the better alternative is where many Belgian campaigns are won. The alternatives study within the environmental assessment repays especially close scrutiny, because law generally requires that reasonable alternatives to a project be considered, and a project pushed toward a favoured design often gives those alternatives only cursory treatment, dismissing a tunnel or a different route with a line or two. That cursory treatment is both a legal vulnerability, a ground on which a court may annul the permit, and an opening for the campaign’s own alternative, which can be developed to the depth the official study lacked. A community that reads the alternatives study closely often finds there the double opening the Antwerp groups exploited.

## Layer 2: The Harm to the Air, the Health, and the Community

Document the reality the paper understates, and put the **air, the health, and the urban life** at the centre. Record the **harm** — the air pollution, the health toll, the noise, the severing of neighbourhoods, the loss of green and public space — that the project would cause, as the "Breathless" campaign documented the threat to the air Antwerp breathed. Document what the community **stands to lose**. Photograph, measure, and date it. Record **testimony** and gather **expert evidence**, which Belgium's professionals supply. The health evidence in particular repays careful, quantified documentation, because air quality and its effects on health are measurable, and measurement carries a force that assertion does not: concentrations of pollutants, the numbers of people exposed, the distances to homes and schools, the projected health effects. The Antwerp campaign that called itself “Breathless” built its power on making the invisible harm of the air visible and concrete, and a campaign that similarly quantifies the health toll of a project — rather than merely fearing it — gives the courts a ground, the voters a reason, and the public a stake that a vaguer complaint could never supply. This is the case that moves the voters, the courts, and the public.

## Layer 3: The Flaw, the Harm, and the Better Way

Finally, document the case. Is the **process flawed** — the assessment dishonest, the health impact ignored, the alternatives not weighed? Is the **harm** to air, health, and community real and grave? And is there a **better alternative**, credible and documented? Each is a separate ground — for the courts, the referendum, and the campaign — and together they make a case that is hard to wave away. This is the layer that most often decides Belgian fights, because here a project sold as necessary is revealed to be flawed, harmful, and — crucially — replaceable by something better, exactly the case that reshaped Antwerp. The strength of assembling all three grounds together — the flaw, the harm, and the better way — is that they serve different fronts and reinforce one another: the flawed process is what the courts can act on, the harm to air and health is what moves the voters and the public, and the better alternative is what the government can adopt to escape the fight. A campaign that documents only the harm may stir feeling without a remedy to offer; one that documents only the flaw may win a delay without a destination; the campaigns that prevail in Belgium, as the Antwerp campaign did, build all three and press them together, so that every audience is given both a reason to reject the plan and a better plan to accept.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

This is the practical companion to Step 2: the specific records that decide Belgian cases, and where to obtain them. You will not need every item; you will need the few that establish the flaw, the harm, and the better alternative.

## The Project, the Process, and the Paper Trail

- **The plans and the permits** — how the project was authorised, and by which region or municipality.
- **The environmental and health assessments** — the studies behind the permit, and often the most vulnerable to challenge.
- **The alternatives study** — how (or whether) alternatives were weighed, a frequent legal weakness.
- **The traffic, air, and health data** — the numbers that show the harm, as the ring's quarter-million vehicles a day showed Antwerp's.
- **The agency and the partnership** — who runs the project, and how it is financed.

## How to Get Them

The plans, permits, and assessments are matters of public record, obtainable from the regional and municipal authorities and through access-to-information requests; the traffic, air, and health data through the agencies, the environment administration, and independent measurement. Lean on **allies**: Belgium's professionals — architects, engineers, urban planners, doctors — who can read the assessments, expose the flaws, and design the alternative, as they did in Antwerp; the environmental and mobility organisations; and the neighbourhood committees. Move promptly: obtaining the assessment and designing the alternative early lets you force the referendum and challenge the permit before the project hardens, as the Antwerp groups moved while the plan was still on the drawing board. Timing matters here and repays acting on early: the assessment, the permits, and the design are most open to challenge, and the alternative most able to prevail, while the project is still being decided and no ground has been broken, because once contracts are signed and construction begins, the inertia of committed money and machinery becomes formidable. A campaign that engages early, securing the documents, sketching the alternative, and forcing the vote while the plan can still be changed, gives itself by far the best chance; one that waits until the diggers arrive finds that the range of the possible has narrowed to conditions and mitigations.

## The Ground Evidence

- **The harm** — the air pollution, the health toll, the noise, the severed neighbourhoods, measured and documented.
- **The process flaws** — the gaps in the assessment, the alternatives not weighed, the unlawful steps.
- **The better alternative** — the tunnel, the cover, the redesign, worked up credibly.
- **The traffic and air data** — the numbers that prove the harm.
- **Testimony and expertise** — from residents, doctors, and the professionals who can design the alternative.

## Organise It So It Persuades

Keep a **master timeline** of the plans, the permits, the assessments, the harm, the alternative, and every step of the fight — the spine of any case, referendum, or negotiation. Keep a **document index** so any fact can be sourced in seconds. And keep an **argument bank** (developed in a later section) that pairs each fact with what it proves, to whom. A well-ordered file is not bureaucracy; it is what lets a resident, an engineer, a lawyer, a journalist, a judge, and a voter all see the same clear picture fast, and it guards against the campaign's own worst enemy, a claim that cannot be sourced when the agency pushes back. In a country where the referendum and the alternative decide, the community with the better-kept file — and, distinctively, the better-worked alternative — is very often the one that wins, as the Antwerp groups' credible alternative helped them win.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION — HOW TO ORGANISE THE GROUPS AND PROPOSE THE ALTERNATIVE

Evidence and process do not win on their own. Organised citizens — and, in Belgium, citizens who design as well as oppose — do. The Antwerp fight proved that citizen action groups, backed by professionals and a better plan, can force a government to redesign a megaproject. A campaign's strength here is that organisation: the groups, the experts, and the alternative.

## Phase 1: The Core and the Groups

Start with a committed core, and build deliberate **citizen action groups**, each with a clear focus, as Antwerp had one for the air, one for the route, and one for the cover. Assign roles — documentation, legal, the referendum drive, media, and, distinctively, the **design of the alternative**. Agree on the goal — the project stopped, voted down, or redesigned into something better — and on what the community will and will not accept. Draw on Belgium's deep civic and professional infrastructure, the architects, engineers, and organisers who make expert citizen action possible. It is worth appreciating how distinctive this resource is, because it is the foundation of the Belgian strategy: a society with a deep and civically engaged pool of professionals, accustomed to organising and willing to lend their skills to a public cause, can produce citizen groups that are not merely loud but genuinely expert, capable of matching and bettering the agencies’ own work. The Antwerp groups included people who could argue urban design and traffic engineering with the professionals employed by the state, and it was that parity of expertise, as much as any protest, that ultimately compelled the authorities to treat the citizens as partners rather than nuisances.

## Phase 2: The Broad Coalition

Then widen deliberately. Bring in the **affected neighbourhoods and residents**; the **professionals** — architects, engineers, urban planners, doctors — who give the campaign technical authority and design the alternative, as Antwerp's did; the **environmental and mobility organisations**; sympathetic **lawyers**; and the **wider public**, whose health and city are at stake. Reach the **press**, because a Belgian infrastructure fight is a public story that moves opinion and the government. The Antwerp movement drew its power from exactly this breadth — residents, professionals, campaigners, and a public that wanted to breathe — which is precisely the spread a winning Belgian campaign needs. The distinctive element in that coalition is the professionals, and it is worth recruiting them deliberately and early: architects, engineers, urban planners, and doctors bring not only credibility but the actual capacity to design the alternative and to quantify the harm, without which a campaign is left merely asserting that a better way exists rather than showing it. The Antwerp movement’s decisive turn came when professionals joined and produced a fully worked covering plan, and a Belgian campaign should treat the recruitment of such expertise not as a bonus but as a central task, because it is what lets the community become the author of the better plan rather than only its advocate.

**A worked coalition example.** Facing a harmful road plan, the neighbourhoods form the base; the professionals design the better alternative; the campaigners force the referendum; the lawyers challenge the permit; and the press moves the public. No single group could carry it; together they cover the ground, the design, the law, and the public — the model that reshaped Antwerp. What is worth drawing from that model is how each element supplied what the others lacked: the neighbourhoods brought the moral standing of those who would breathe the fumes, the professionals brought the design and the technical authority, the campaigners brought the referendum and the protests, and the lawyers brought the challenges that bought time. A community beginning a fight should honestly assess which of these it already holds and which it must recruit, because it is the completeness of the coalition, and above all the presence of the design capacity, that has proved decisive in Belgium — a campaign that can both mobilise and design is the one that wins a redesign.

## Phase 3: Sustaining It

Campaigns die of burnout, not defeat — and a Belgian infrastructure fight can run for many years, as Antwerp's ran for two decades. Meet on a rhythm. Give people concrete tasks. Mark small wins — the assessment obtained, the referendum forced, the alternative published, a court filing, a wave of coverage, a government concession. Keep the groups energised and the public engaged, and guard against the **exhaustion and the co-optation** — the wearing-down of a long fight, the offer to settle for a token change, the splitting of the groups — that will be used against you. Above all, keep the alternative alive and improving, because a campaign with a better plan has something to fight for, not only against, which sustains it.

## Phase 4: Forcing the Referendum and Winning the Redesign

Here is Belgium's distinctive strength, on two levels. First, **force the referendum**: gather the signatures a local popular consultation requires, and win the vote, as Antwerp gathered its signatures and buried the Lange Wapper. Second, and distinctively, **win the redesign**: press your better alternative through the campaign, the courts, and the negotiations, until the government adopts it or a version of it, as Antwerp won its covered ring and its healed neighbourhoods. Be ready to **negotiate and to partner**, because a redesign is often won not by pure opposition but by becoming the credible authors of the better plan, as the Antwerp groups became partners in the final pact — while guarding against a deal that keeps the harm and drops the promise. This guarding is not a minor caveat but a central task, because the Antwerp experience shows that the hardest part of such a fight can come after the pact is signed: the agreement to cover the ring and reweave the city was a real victory, but critics have argued that as the detailed design and construction proceeded, the authorities and their engineers drifted back toward a heavier, more road-centred version, honouring the letter of the pact more than its spirit. A campaign that wins a redesign must therefore stay organised and vigilant through the years of construction, holding the government to the promise it made, because a victory announced is not the same as a victory built. Keep it **disciplined, expert, and constructive**, because a campaign that offers a better city, not just a refusal, is the one that moves a government, as Antwerp's did.

# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES — THE REFERENDUM, THE PERMITS, AND THE COURTS

Belgium's local referendum, its strong courts, and its permitting process are a real legal and civic toolkit — and the distinctive instruments are the referendum and the constructive alternative, which the Antwerp groups wielded to reshape a megaproject. You do not need a courtroom victory to win: a plan voted down, a permit quashed, a delay that opens the door to the alternative, or a negotiated redesign can each be a real victory. Engage lawyers and professionals early — but understand the tools.

## The Local Referendum

The first and most distinctive lever is the **local referendum**. Belgian municipalities allow a **popular consultation**, which citizens can force by gathering the required signatures, and while it is advisory, a clear vote is politically decisive — as the 2009 Antwerp vote against the Lange Wapper was, burying the viaduct. Gather the signatures, force the vote, and win it; a referendum turns a citizens' campaign into the voice of the city, which a government defies at its peril.

## The Permits and the Courts

The second lever is the **permits and the courts**. Belgium's **courts, including the Council of State**, review the legality of permits and administrative decisions, and a permit resting on a **flawed assessment**, an ignored health impact, or **alternatives not properly weighed** can be challenged and annulled. Scrutinise the permit and the assessment, and challenge them — court procedures were part of what sustained the Antwerp fight and forced the government to the table. The strategic use of litigation in Belgium is less about a single decisive judgment than about the pressure and the time that credible legal challenges create: a well-founded case before the Council of State can suspend or annul a permit, sending the authorities back to redo the work, and each such delay keeps the project open and unbuilt while the referendum, the alternative, and the public campaign do their slower work. A community that has done the legal groundwork and can mount a serious challenge holds a lever that buys the most precious commodity in these fights, time — time in which a better alternative can gather support and a government’s resolve can weaken. Even a delay can be a victory, because it keeps the project open to the better alternative.

## The Constructive Alternative

The third lever, and Belgium's most distinctive, is the **constructive alternative**. A campaign that not only opposes but **proposes a credible, expert-designed better plan** — a tunnel, a cover, a redesign — holds a lever pure opposition lacks: it gives the government a way to say yes to something, and the public a better future to demand, as the Antwerp groups' alternatives gave. Develop the alternative rigorously, publish it, and campaign for it; it is often the key that turns a fight against a project into a win for a better one. A campaign should hold to a high standard in developing the alternative, because its power depends entirely on its credibility: a plan that engineers and planners can pick apart is worse than none, handing the authorities an easy dismissal, while a plan they must take seriously changes the terms of the whole debate. Invest in getting the alternative right — the traffic modelled, the costs estimated, the benefits shown — and present it as a serious proposal from serious people, as the Antwerp groups presented theirs, because a credible alternative is the rare kind of pressure that a government can yield to while saving face, which is often exactly what allows it to yield at all.

## Where to Turn and With Whom

Referendum drives go through the **municipality**; permit and legality challenges through the **courts and the Council of State**; the alternative through the **campaign, the professionals, and the negotiations**. Get help early: Belgium's public-interest lawyers, its architects and engineers, and its environmental and mobility organisations know the law, the design, and the routes. Help engaged early — before a project hardens, while the alternative can still prevail — is worth far more than help sought after, and in Belgium the strongest strategy marries the **referendum and the courts** to the **constructive alternative**, so that the community both blocks the bad plan and authors the better one.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

You have a file of evidence. Now you must turn it into arguments — because the same fact persuades a court, an official, a voter, a journalist, and an engineer in different ways. Learning to translate your evidence for each audience is what lets a community reach every lever at once.

## One Fact, Many Arguments

Take a single documented fact — say, that a planned elevated motorway would raise air pollution over dense neighbourhoods to harmful levels, when a tunnel or a covered road would avoid it, and the alternatives were never fairly weighed. That one fact becomes:

- **A legal argument:** the assessment failed to weigh the alternatives, and the permit is flawed — the case before the courts.
- **A health argument:** the design would poison the air people breathe, avoidably — the case that unites the neighbourhoods.
- **A referendum argument:** the city should not accept a harmful plan when a better one exists — the case that wins the vote.
- **A design argument:** a tunnel or a cover would serve the city better — the case that persuades the engineers and the planners.
- **A public argument:** they would build a wall of fumes through our city when they could heal it instead — the case that moves the public.

Same fact, five arguments, five audiences. Go through your strongest facts and, for each, ask what it proves to each audience. That table is your campaign's argument bank — and in Belgium the arguments that most often decide things are the **health** one, because it unites the public, and the **better-alternative** one, because it gives everyone something to say yes to. Keep the arguments anchored to the same documented facts, so a claim made to a reporter can be backed in the data and a claim made to a judge can be proven by the assessment; consistency across audiences is itself persuasive.

## Match the Argument to the Audience

**The courts** want the flawed assessment and the unweighed alternatives framed against the law, with the documents that prove them.

**The officials** respond to the referendum result, the legal risk, and — crucially — the availability of a better plan they can adopt.

**Journalists** need a specific, vivid story with a hook: a wall of fumes and concrete through a city, and citizens who designed something better.

**The voters** need the argument simple and true: a harmful plan, an avoidable one, and a better city on offer — vote it down.

**The engineers and planners** respond to the technical case for the alternative — the traffic, the air, the design.

**The public** needs the vision of the better city — the covered road, the parks, the reconnected neighbourhoods — that the alternative offers.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY — HOW PRESS COVERAGE SHIFTS OUTCOMES

Media is not vanity; it is a lever — and in Belgium, where public opinion and the referendum decide, coverage that shows both the harm and the better alternative is among the most powerful levers of all. Coverage shifts opinion, wins the vote, and pressures the government. The Antwerp movement succeeded in large part because it made a public story of both the threat — a wall of fumes over the city — and the promise — a covered ring with parks where the motorway had divided the city.

## Build the Story

Reporters and the public need specifics, and in Belgium they need both the danger and the vision. "A project is controversial" is not a story; "an elevated motorway would poison the air over dense neighbourhoods, when a covered ring could heal the city" is — the story that carried Antwerp. Lead with your strongest verified facts — the health harm, the flawed process — and pair them with the **better alternative**, and offer a human and expert voice: a resident, a doctor, an architect with the plan. In Belgium the story of a city choosing between a wall of fumes and a healed neighbourhood is one that moves people.

## Reach the Right Outlets

Work outward in rings. **Local and neighbourhood voices** first. Then the **regional and national outlets** — because a major infrastructure fight is a regional and national story. Then the **international ring** where the alternative is striking — because a bold idea, like roofing a ring road and building parks on top, draws international attention that adds prestige and pressure, as Antwerp's covered ring has drawn. There is a particular strategic value in a bold, positive alternative attracting international notice, because it reframes a local fight in a way that flatters rather than embarrasses the authorities: a story that a city is doing something visionary — roofing a motorway and building parks and neighbourhoods on top — is one a government may come to want to be associated with, turning a project it resisted into a showcase it claims. A campaign whose alternative is striking enough to draw admiring international attention gains a lever that pure opposition never can, the prospect of turning the government from an opponent into a proud co-author. Feed each ring what it needs, and let coverage in one pull in the next. The **regional and national ring is decisive** in Belgium, because it moves the region and the public that vote and decide.

## Sustain It, and Keep It True

A single article changes little; a drumbeat changes decisions — and a Belgian fight can run for many years. Plan a sequence — the harm, the flawed plan, the referendum, the alternative, the redesign — so there is always a next hook, and build your own clear record so the story reaches the public and endures. Keep messaging disciplined, accurate, and constructive; the agency will seize on any exaggeration, and in a well-informed democracy a credible, solution-offering campaign is what moves a government. Verified, specific, health-and-alternative-centred, and relentless — that is the coverage that shifts outcomes here, and that helped bury a viaduct and build a better ring.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Written communications are how you request, object, create a record, and reach the bodies that can act — the authorities, the agencies, the courts, and the press. Each does double duty: it asks for something and it documents that you asked. Keep copies of everything, and send important items so that receipt is provable. Below are adaptable templates for the situations you will meet most often. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the process, the harm, and the alternative.

## 8A. Request for Information (Access to Information)

**To:** the regional / municipal authority or agency
**Subject:** Request for information on [project]

Under the access-to-information law, we, [community/organisation], request the **plans, the permits, the environmental and health assessments, the alternatives study, and the traffic and air data** for [project]. We require these to assess the project's lawfulness and effects. Please provide them within the statutory period.

## 8B. Objection in the Permit Process

**To:** the permitting authority
**Subject:** Objection to [project] — flawed assessment and unweighed alternatives

We, [community/organisation], object to [project]. The assessment **understated the harm** to air and health, the process **failed to weigh the alternatives** (including [our proposed alternative]), and the design would [poison the air / sever the neighbourhoods]. We request that the permit be **refused** or the assessment redone, and we reserve our right to the courts.

## 8C. Petition for a Local Referendum

**To:** the municipality
**Subject:** Request for a local popular consultation on [project]

We, the undersigned residents of [municipality], request a **local popular consultation** on [project], as the law provides. This project affects the health, the air, and the future of our city, and the decision belongs to its people. [Signatures attached.] We call on the municipality to hold the consultation.

## 8D. Court Challenge (with a Lawyer)

**To:** the Council of State / the courts (via counsel)
**Subject:** Challenge to the permit for [project]

On behalf of [community/organisation], we challenge the permit for [project] as **unlawful**: it rests on a **flawed assessment**, **ignored the health impact**, and **failed to weigh the alternatives**. We seek its **annulment** and the suspension of works, and attach the record demonstrating the flaws.

## 8E. Presenting the Alternative

**To:** the government / the agency / the public
**Subject:** A better alternative to [project]

We, [community/organisation], present a **better alternative** to [project]: [the tunnel / the cover / the redesign], which would [avoid the harm / heal the neighbourhoods / serve the city better], as documented and attached. We call on the government to study and adopt it, and we invite the public to support it. As Antwerp chose a covered ring over a viaduct, so can [city].

## 8F. Instructing a Lawyer / Organisation

**To:** a public-interest lawyer / environmental or mobility organisation
**Subject:** [Project] — the process, the harm, and the alternative

We are opposing [project]. We attach the permits, the assessments, our evidence of harm, and our alternative, and seek advice on **challenging the permit**, **forcing a referendum**, and **advancing the alternative**. Please advise on the strongest ground and the deadlines.

## 8G. Email to a Reporter

**To:** [journalist]
**Subject:** Story tip: [specific finding] — [the project / the alternative]

I follow your coverage of [beat]. We have something the public should know, with a clear hook: [documents] show [quantified finding — e.g. "an elevated motorway would poison the air over dense neighbourhoods, when a covered ring could heal the city"]. It echoes Antwerp. I can share the file and connect you with a resident, a doctor, and an architect with the alternative. Could we talk this week?

## 8H. Public Statement

**To:** the public and the authorities
**Subject:** [Community/organisation] on [project]

We, [community/organisation], oppose [project] as planned, which would **harm our air and our neighbourhoods**, and we propose a **better alternative**: [the tunnel / the cover / the redesign]. As the people of Antwerp voted down a viaduct and won a covered ring, we will use the referendum, the courts, and our alternative to win a better [city/project]. We call on the government to choose the better way, and we will not relent.

## Using the Templates Well

Send important communications so that receipt is provable, and log every one in your timeline — the record of a request, an objection, a referendum petition, or an alternative presented is itself evidence, and exactly what the authorities, the courts, and the public will heed. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the process, the harm, and the alternative; attach your evidence; and route them, where you can, through the groups, the professionals, your lawyers, and the press. The reply, or the result, becomes part of your record, and in Belgium that record feeds the levers that matter most: the referendum, the courts, and the constructive alternative that turns a fight against a project into a win for a better one.

# IF YOU HAVE LITTLE TIME OR FEW RESOURCES: THE RAPID-FIRE VERSION

Not everyone can run a twenty-year campaign. With a few hours a week you can still do real damage to a bad project — and start building a better one. In rough order of impact for the effort:

**1. Get the assessment and find the flaw.** Obtain the plans and the environmental and health assessment and scrutinise them, especially how alternatives were weighed (Step 2, 8A).

**2. Sketch the better alternative.** Even a rough alternative — a tunnel, a cover, a different site — changes the whole fight (Step 3, 8E).

**3. Object on the record.** Put your objection, the health harm, and the unweighed alternative on the record (8B).

**4. Reach the professionals.** Connect with architects, engineers, and doctors who can strengthen the case and the alternative (Step 3, 8F).

**5. Start the referendum drive.** Begin gathering signatures for a local popular consultation (8C).

**6. Move the public.** Take the harm and the better alternative to the press and the public (Step 5, 8G).

**7. Challenge the permit.** With a lawyer, challenge a flawed permit or unweighed alternatives (8D).

Do only the first three and you will have found the flaw, sketched the alternative, and put both on the record — the foundation of a Belgian campaign. Then come back and read the rest. Even the shortest version has a spine: find the flaw, sketch the alternative, object.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Belgium's system is, by global standards, favourable — but it is not without its tilts, and you must understand them so you neither waste effort nor grow complacent.

## Where the Tilt Shows

The tilt here is milder than in most countries, but real. **Powerful economic interests** — the port, the construction industry, the road lobby — carry weight, and a project sold as an economic boost gains momentum. The **complex, multi-level structure** — federal, regional, municipal — can diffuse responsibility and let a determined government press on. **Projects acquire their own inertia**: once agencies, budgets, and contracts are committed, a plan is hard to stop, and a government may honour a redesign in word while reverting toward the old plan in the building, as critics say happened in Antwerp. This particular tilt — the erosion of a won redesign during its execution — deserves emphasis because it is subtle and easily missed in the relief of a signed agreement. The machinery of a large project has its own momentum and its own institutional preferences, and the engineers and agencies who favoured the original design do not simply vanish when a pact is signed; they carry out the detailed design, and in the thousands of technical decisions that follow, the character of the project can quietly drift back toward what they wanted. A campaign that does not anticipate this, and does not keep the expertise and vigilance to contest those decisions, may find its victory hollowed out by the time it is built. And a fight can take **so long** that a community tires before the government does. Where these tilts bite, a campaign that assumes a referendum result or a signed pact will simply be honoured, without continued vigilance, will be disappointed.

## What Still Works — And Much Is Strong

The decisive point is that Belgium's levers are, by any comparison, strong and genuinely available.

**The referendum still speaks.** A local popular consultation, forced by citizens, is a political fact a government defies at its peril.

**The courts genuinely constrain.** Belgium's courts and the Council of State really do annul unlawful permits.

**The alternative still wins.** A credible, expert-designed better plan gives a government a way to yes and the public a future to demand.

**Expert citizens are real.** Belgium's professionals lend campaigns authority and design that agencies cannot dismiss.

**Public opinion still moves the government.** In an open democracy, a roused public shifts decisions.

## The Honest Frame

So the honest frame for Belgium is genuinely hopeful, with a caution against complacency and a warning to watch the building: the levers are strong, the referendum real, the courts genuine, and the alternative powerful — but wins are often redesigns, they take years, and a pact can be eroded in the concrete. So diagnose your fight honestly: find the **flawed process**; design the **better alternative**; argue the **health and livability**; force the **referendum** and the **courts**; and, having won, **watch the implementation**, because a redesign promised is not a redesign built. Do not assume the good system will deliver without your vigilance; Antwerp's citizens won by organising, designing, and staying at the table to the end — and are still watching. The lesson in that persistence is that in Belgium, unlike in a system where the fight ends at a permit or a vote, the decisive struggle often continues through the long years of design and construction that follow a nominal victory, and the community that disbands upon signing the pact cedes that ground to the very interests it defeated. Staying at the table — keeping the groups alive, the professionals engaged, and the public informed through the building — is not a failure to accept victory but the way a Belgian victory is actually secured, because here a project is shaped as much in its execution as in its approval.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO IS CAPTURED

Belgium is, by global standards, a well-governed democracy, but no country is immune to the capture of a decision by powerful interests, so this section matters. When a project is driven not by the public interest but by the road lobby, the construction industry, or the port's weight over the authorities, your strategy includes exposing that.

## How to Tell

Look for the signatures of influence. A **plan pushed against the evidence and the public interest**; an **assessment bent** to favour a chosen design and to dismiss the alternatives; **revolving doors** between the agencies, the contractors, and the government; a **public-private partnership** whose terms favour the builders; and **benefits to the connected** — the contractors, the landowners — while the community bears the fumes. Trace the decision and the interests: who pushed the design, who profits from the contracts, whether the process was bent to suit them. In Belgium such capture is usually subtler than open graft — a matter of lobbying, inertia, and committed contracts — but where it drives a harmful design against a better alternative, it can be exposed.

## Who Can Be Captured — and Who Is Harder

Be clear-eyed about where influence reaches. **Parts of the agencies and the government, and the partnerships close to the contractors,** can be swayed by powerful interests. But some forces are far harder to capture, and they are your targets: the **courts and the Council of State**, which really constrain; the **free press**; the **expert citizen groups and professionals**; the **voters**, through the referendum; and the **public**. Belgium's institutions are, by comparison, resistant to capture; your task is to use them — to expose the influence, offer the better alternative, and let the public and the courts judge.

## What to Do

Do not assume that influence over a decision cannot be exposed. Instead, **document the capture precisely** — how the design was pushed, who profits from the contracts, what better alternative was dismissed and why — and take it to the forces that can act: the **courts**, to review an unlawful or improper decision; the **free press**, to expose the influence; the **voters**, through the referendum; and the **public**, with the better alternative in hand. Pair the exposure with the alternative, so the impropriety and the better way arrive together. In Belgium, exposing how a harmful design was pushed for the connected while a better plan was ignored, in a country that prizes its good governance, is a powerful way to turn the courts, the voters, and the public against it.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

The five steps win when they run together. Here is how they fit across the life of a fight — which in Belgium turns on the referendum, the courts, and the constructive alternative.

## Early (Months 0–8): The Flaw, the Alternative, and the Groups

Pin down the project and the deciding level (Step 1); obtain the **assessment** and document the flaws, the harm, and — distinctively — begin to **design the alternative** (Step 2). Build the **groups and recruit the professionals** (Step 3). Move at once on what is decisive: **object in the process** (8B); **request the information** (8A); begin the **referendum drive** (8C); and **sketch and publish the alternative** (8E). This phase lays the foundation, and in Belgium the documented flaw, the better alternative, and the organised groups established here are what win.

## Middle (Months 6–36): Vote, Challenge, and Design

Sustain the groups and the public (Step 3). Turn your evidence into arguments for each audience (Turning Evidence). Force the **referendum**, mount the **court challenge**, and refine and campaign for the **alternative** (Step 4). Build the media drumbeat, showing both the harm and the better way (Step 5). Each front feeds the others. Do the time-critical things first — forcing the referendum and challenging the permit before construction — because those chances do not return.

## Later (Months 24+): The Redesign, and the Watch

Pursue the decisive outcome — the **project stopped, voted down, or redesigned into something better** — and then, crucially, **watch the implementation**, because, as in Antwerp, a redesign won can be eroded in the building. Judge success honestly. Sometimes it is a project stopped or voted down. Often, in Belgium, it is a harmful plan turned into a better one — a viaduct into a covered ring — won and then defended through the years of construction. Both are victories worth the fight. And even a partial redesign compounds over time: a harmful plan improved today spares real air and real neighbourhoods, a referendum once forced shows the next campaign it can be done, and an alternative once taken seriously widens what the public believes is possible. A campaign should value what it can win now, not only for its own city but for the way each blocked viaduct and each covered ring enlarges the sense of what citizens can demand and design, as the Antwerp movement enlarged it for the whole country.

## The Shape of a Campaign

```
MONTHS 0-8    Find the flaw + design the alternative | Build the groups | Object | Begin the referendum drive
MONTHS 6-36   Force the referendum | Court challenge | Campaign for the alternative | Media
MONTHS 24+    Project stopped / voted down / redesigned | Watch the implementation, for a pact can be eroded
```

# FINAL ASSESSMENT

Belgium gives communities a genuinely powerful — and distinctive — set of tools, and the honest measure of this guide is how squarely it faces both their strength and their limits.

The strengths are real and distinctive. The **local referendum** lets citizens force a vote and bury a bad plan, as Antwerp buried the Lange Wapper. **Expert citizens** — architects, engineers, doctors — lend a campaign authority and, above all, a credible alternative. The **health and livability argument** unites a public that wants to breathe. The **courts and the Council of State** genuinely constrain a flawed project. And, most distinctively, the **constructive alternative** lets a community not only block a project but author a better one. These are the tools that let Antwerp's citizens turn a wall of fumes into a covered ring with parks. They are, notably, tools that reward organisation, expertise, and creativity rather than wealth or connections, which is why groups of residents and professionals could prevail over an agency and a government committed to a viaduct — and why, used with the same resolve, they remain available to communities across Belgium wherever a harmful plan can be met with a better one.

The limits are just as real, and this guide names them. Antwerp was a **redesign, not a clean stop**, and its promises were **partly eroded** in the building. The win took **twenty years**. And Belgium's **wealth and strong institutions** made it more winnable than it would be elsewhere. No guide should pretend these away; in Belgium, the art is to block and to design, to win the redesign and then to watch it through the concrete, and not to mistake a signed pact for a finished victory.

So the realistic promise is this: **find the flawed process; design the better alternative; argue the health and livability; force the referendum and the courts; win the redesign; and watch the implementation — running all of it together.** Do that, and where a referendum and a credible alternative are available, you can vote a plan down and win a better one, as Antwerp did; where they are not, you can still delay, improve, and record. Do it as the Antwerp groups did — citizens who did not merely refuse a viaduct but designed a covered ring, forced a vote, fought in court, and stayed at the table until the city was being healed.

A last word on blocking and building. Belgium's lesson is that in an open democracy, citizens hold more power than they think — that they can not only stop a wall of fumes but design and win the parks that replace it — but that the win is often a redesign, hard-fought over many years, and must be guarded through the building, because a pact honoured in word can be broken in concrete. The powerful count on your resignation, your exhaustion, and your assumption that a plan once decided cannot be changed. Their advantage is the inertia, the committed contracts, and the long years. Yours is a referendum you can force, courts that constrain, professionals who can design a better way, a public that wants to breathe, and the proven fact that Antwerp turned a viaduct into a covered ring. Play to that. Keep the record and the alternative, keep the groups energised and the public engaged, force the vote and the courts, offer the better plan, and treat every objection, every filing, every vote, and every metre of the better plan built as a stone in a wall that even the road lobby cannot easily climb.

The tools are here, and Belgium itself proved them: citizens who fought not only against a towering viaduct but for a covered ring, forced a referendum that buried the bad plan, and stayed at the table until the government agreed to heal the city its motorway had cut in two. What decides the outcome, in the end, is the organisation of the groups, the credibility of the alternative, the force of the referendum, and the vigilance through the building — and those a determined community can supply. Begin today, begin with the flaw and the alternative and the groups, and build from there. Communities before you have blocked a bad plan and built a better one — a viaduct buried, a referendum won, a ring being covered with parks. They did it with organisation, expertise, and a better plan, against an agency and a government committed to the old design, and over years long enough to test anyone’s resolve — which is exactly why your own community’s effort, added to theirs, is neither hopeless nor unimportant. What the Antwerp citizens won can be won again. So can yours. A movement that refused a viaduct and designed a covered ring, that forced its city to vote and then stayed to see the parks built, has shown what citizens in an open democracy can do when they meet a bad plan not only with refusal but with a better plan of their own; the referendum, the courts, the health case, the expert groups, and above all the constructive alternative are the method, and the proof that a wall of fumes can become a district of parks is now yours to take up and use.

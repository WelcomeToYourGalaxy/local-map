# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In 2016, the people of **Soamahamanina** — a commune of some fourteen thousand west of the capital — learned that a Chinese firm, **Jiuxing Mines**, had been handed a **forty-year license** to mine gold across **seventy-five hundred hectares** of their land: the rice fields and hills their families had farmed, and been buried in, for generations. They did not accept it. Week after week, **every Thursday, they marched.** When security forces met them with tear gas and arrested their leaders, they kept marching — and the arrests only drew national attention and a **United Nations human-rights rapporteur**. By July the **government had halted the project**, and by October the company announced it would pull out; the Chinese management left Soamahamanina, abandoning their tents. A farming community with nothing but its unity and its refusal had driven out a foreign mining company and forced its own government to stop the mine.

The people of Soamahamanina drove out a foreign gold miner with a sustained, disciplined refusal — marching every Thursday until the government halted the mine — and the same lever suspended the Base Toliara mineral-sands project three years later; the tools around it are available to communities across Madagascar. **National and international attention** — the press, a UN human-rights rapporteur, human-rights and environmental bodies — magnifies a refusal and shields it. The **question of a license's provenance** — a permit issued by an illegitimate or transitional regime, on unfavourable terms, as Base Toliara's 2012 licence was — is a real vulnerability. The **land-and-livelihood case** — the rice fields, the ancestral ground — is a powerful argument. And the **courts**, where they function, can be tried; protest and petition are lawful, though defenders must weigh the real risk. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Defenders here have faced **tear gas and arrest**, so this guide treats safety with care. Madagascar is **one of the world's poorest countries**, its institutions weak and its rule of law uneven, so remedies are unreliable. And a **suspension is not a burial** — Base Toliara was suspended, not permanently killed, and mining pressure continues — so wins can be partial and reversible. So aim for what this lever can deliver — a mine halted, a company driven out, a project suspended — through a refusal that will not break and attention a government cannot ignore.

**How to spot it.** Licenses granted by illegitimate or transitional regimes; sweeping concessions over communities' land on terms unfavourable to the people and the state; a project driven despite local opposition; benefits to a foreign company and connected interests while communities lose land and livelihoods.

Keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect, and never let exposure endanger anyone.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN TUVALU

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the land, coasts, and future of Tuvalu — but here the threat is unlike any other in this series, because the force destroying Tuvalu is not a project a community can oppose but the rising sea itself, driven by emissions the world's great powers produce and Tuvalu did not, and the fight is therefore less a fight to stop a particular development than a fight to defend a nation's very existence, its rights, and its dignity as its territory slowly disappears beneath the water. Tuvalu is one of the lowest-lying and most exposed nations on earth — nine coral atolls and reef islands, averaging barely two metres above the level of the sea, home to some eleven thousand people in all, and projected to be overtopped at high tide within a few short decades. No community here can build a seawall high enough to hold back the emissions of distant continents. But Tuvalu has done something genuinely remarkable and without precedent: rather than quietly accept its own erasure, it has begun to defend its existence on every available front at once — writing into its constitution the principle that its statehood endures even if its physical land is lost, fixing its ocean boundaries permanently in law, securing from a larger neighbour a treaty that recognises its enduring sovereignty and offers its people a dignified path to migrate, building a "digital nation" to preserve its identity and government even if the land goes under, pressing the world's courts to hold the great emitters accountable, and raising new, higher land above the reach of the tides. There is no victory to be had over the sea itself. But the levers — legal, diplomatic, and moral — are real.

The fight in Tuvalu is, uniquely, to defend a nation's existence, and its tools are novel legal, diplomatic, and accountability instruments — and they are real. The distinctive lever is the **continuity of statehood**: Tuvalu's statehood and ocean boundaries, fixed in law to endure beyond the land, are a novel and powerful defence of the nation's very existence, holding that Tuvalu remains Tuvalu even if the atolls go under. The **migration-with-dignity treaty and the asserted right to remain** offer both a lifeline and a defence of sovereignty — a larger neighbour's recognition of Tuvalu's enduring statehood and a dignified path for its people — with hard trade-offs to weigh. And though the sea is the defining threat, Tuvalu remains a functioning democracy: any actual domestic project — a harmful reclamation, a coastal development — can be met with the ordinary tools of environmental assessment, planning, marine protection, and the elected institutions; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. The **digital nation** preserves Tuvalu's identity, culture, and government even if the land is lost. And the **emissions accountability** — pressing the world's courts and bodies to hold the great emitters responsible — carries the fight to the very source of the harm. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The threat is external and beyond Tuvalu's power to stop, driven by emissions Tuvalu did not produce. The science is grim, and the atolls are projected to be overtopped within decades. The defences carry hard trade-offs — the treaty and migration mean, for some, leaving; the raising of land is costly and partial. And there is no victory to be had over the sea itself. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — the nation's statehood, rights, and dignity defended even as the land is threatened; the great emitters held to account; the identity preserved; a dignified future secured — through the continuity of statehood, the treaty, the digital nation, and the emissions accountability, with the raising of land a hard, partial, physical front. A nation's existence, rights, and dignity can be defended even when its territory cannot yet be saved.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN THE MARSHALL ISLANDS

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

The struggle in the Marshall Islands is unlike almost any other, because the gravest harm was done long ago by a distant and powerful state, and its consequences are still unfolding, now colliding with a second existential threat. Between 1946 and 1958, the United States detonated sixty-seven nuclear bombs on the atolls of **Bikini and Enewetak**, including the largest weapon it ever tested, and in doing so displaced whole communities, irradiated others, and rendered ancestral homelands unsafe. Decades later the harm endures: contaminated islands to which people cannot return, health effects still unaddressed, promised compensation still unpaid, and, on Enewetak, a cracking concrete dome the Marshallese call the **Tomb**, holding a hundred thousand cubic yards of radioactive waste and now leaking into the lagoon — a dome that rising seas threaten to breach entirely. For the Marshall Islands is also among the lowest-lying nations on earth, on the front line of the climate crisis, so that the old nuclear injustice and the new climate one now meet in a single place. The struggle for justice is unfinished. But a wronged people can turn its injustice into a claim the world hears.

The Marshall Islands works with real tools of justice and accountability. The distinctive lever is the **documented debt of justice** — the unpaid compensation, the unaddressed harm, the contaminated homelands — a claim the nation is owed and the world recognises. The **Compact of Free Association** that binds the nation to the United States is both its dependence and its leverage: the strategic value the nation provides is real, and the compact's terms and renewals are a genuine point of pressure. The **leaking Tomb** on Runit is a concrete, visible accountability the world can see and the responsible party must answer. The **meeting of the nuclear and climate injustice** is uniquely powerful — a small nation wronged twice over, by weapons and by a warming it did not cause. And **international law and the world's movements** stand with the wronged, from the courts to the climate negotiations; protest, petition, and advocacy are lawful. For any new domestic harm, the nation's own **democracy, environmental assessment, and marine and conservation designations** apply as well. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The responsible party is a powerful ally the nation depends on — for economic assistance, migration rights, and defence — so the leverage is real but constrained by dependence. The United States treats the nuclear matter as settled and has resisted doing more. Much of the harm is already done, and much of the climate threat is beyond the nation's control. And the struggle for justice is unfinished, and may remain so for years. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — the debt of justice pressed and partly paid, the compact leveraged, the leaking dome held to account, the double injustice made a claim the world cannot ignore — through justice, the compact, and the world's conscience, in a struggle a wronged people can carry even against the powerful.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You win here not by stopping a local project but by turning a documented injustice into a claim the world hears and the powerful must answer. Press the debt of justice the nation is owed; use the compact that binds the nation to the United States as leverage as well as dependence; make the leaking Tomb a concrete accountability the world can see; and wield the uniquely powerful meeting of the nuclear and climate wrongs, backed by international law and the world's movements. A small nation wronged by a great power can, through justice and the world's conscience, hold even the powerful to account.

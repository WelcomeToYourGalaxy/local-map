# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN RWANDA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community effort in Rwanda is unusual, because the harms that most affect communities often come wrapped in genuinely celebrated green development and conservation, and because Rwanda is a tightly organised state with real environmental achievements but limited space for open dissent. Rwanda is admired around much of the world as a leading green model: it has protected its mountain gorillas back from the very brink of extinction, set aside nearly two-fifths of its national land for conservation, banned the plastic bag outright, kept its capital city famously clean and orderly, and is restoring the degraded wetlands of Kigali to hold back the floods. These are real and genuinely hard-won achievements, and this guide honours them plainly and without any reservation whatsoever. Yet the same strong, top-down drive that delivers these achievements can also fall heavily on ordinary communities: the wetland restorations and the green-city projects have evicted residents and expropriated their plots; the expropriation and resettlement carried out for development and for the clearing of high-risk zones displace families from their homes; and, in an older and harder pattern, conservation itself has pushed communities from the very lands they long depended on. The distinctive difficulty here is that the destructive project is very often a genuinely good project carried out in a way that harms the community — a wetland park that floods a neighbourhood with eviction notices, or a conservation scheme that quietly excludes the very people who once lived beside it. So the aim here is not to oppose green development but to insist it be done with communities, not to them.

A community in Rwanda works not to oppose green development but to insist it be done with them, not to them — and, in a tightly organised state, cautiously, through legal and international channels. The distinctive lever is the **state's own community-benefit promise**: Rwanda's stated commitment that green development benefits communities is a promise it can be held to, on its own terms. The **rights to fair compensation and genuine consultation** in expropriation and resettlement are real, if imperfectly honoured — a legal claim a displaced family can press. The **principle of benefit-sharing** — that communities share in the revenue and gains of conservation and green projects — can be pressed to be real. The **social and environmental safeguards of the international funders** who finance many of these projects carry accountability mechanisms a community can invoke. And the **environmental and resettlement assessment** is a formal gate. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Rwanda's green projects are genuinely valuable and the state has real reasons for them, so the aim is never to oppose them but to make them fair. The space for open opposition is constrained, with real red lines around politics, security, and criticism of the government, so the work must be cautious, legal, technical, and international, framed around the community's rights and the state's own promise, never around challenging authority, and protecting those involved. The green model is entrenched and celebrated. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a displaced community fairly compensated, genuinely consulted, and included; the benefit-sharing made real; the funders' safeguards honoured; the state held to its own promise — so that green development is done with communities, not to them, cautiously and through the legal and international channels. Above all, keep it to the community's rights, and protect the people.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You do not oppose Rwanda's green development, which is genuinely valuable — you insist it be done with communities, not to them. Hold the state to its own promise that green development benefits communities; press the rights to fair compensation and genuine consultation in expropriation and resettlement; make the benefit-sharing real; and invoke the international funders' safeguards — all cautiously, through legal and international channels, framed around the community's rights, never around challenging authority, and protecting those involved.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

Power in Rwanda is strong and top-down, but the decisions a cautious effort can reach run through rights, promises, and funders: the **government and its lands, environment, and resettlement bodies** that carry out the expropriation and green projects; the **compensation and consultation law** that governs them; the **benefit-sharing schemes** around conservation; and the **international funders and their safeguards** that finance much of it. Keep clear of the red lines, and map who decides a project and which right or safeguard reaches it before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A green or development project — a wetland park, a resettlement, a conservation scheme — moves through the state's top-down drive, with an environmental and resettlement assessment and a compensation and consultation process. That assessment and process is your entry point: the moment to press for genuine consultation, fair compensation, and inclusion, and to invoke the state's own community-benefit promise. For internationally funded projects, the funders' safeguards are a further entry point. Enter through the community's rights, cautiously, and framed around fairness, not opposition.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the project: which green or development scheme displaces or excludes the community, and whether its consultation and compensation were genuine. Follow the promise: the state's own commitment that green development benefits communities. Follow the benefit-sharing: whether the community shares in the conservation or green revenue. Follow the finance: which international funders back it, and what safeguards they impose. The leverage lies in the state's own promise, the compensation and consent rights, the benefit-sharing, and the funders' safeguards.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

The watchers here are the lands, environment, and resettlement bodies, the compensation and consultation law, the benefit-sharing schemes, and — crucially — the international funders and their safeguard and accountability mechanisms. A case engages these cautiously, holding the state to its own promise and the funders to their safeguards, framed around the community's rights.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Ask five questions. **What is the harm?** — the eviction, expropriation, resettlement, or exclusion, wrapped in a green or development project. **Was it done fairly?** — the consultation, compensation, and inclusion, or their absence. **Which right applies?** — the fair-compensation and genuine-consultation rights. **What promise does it strain?** — the state's own community-benefit commitment. **Is it funded internationally?** — whose safeguards apply. Pick the fight where the state's own promise, the compensation rights, or the funders' safeguards give the clearest, most cautious handle — and always for fairness, not against the project.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

A case built on the community's rights and the state's own promise, framed for fairness not opposition, is one the system can receive.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN TRINIDAD AND TOBAGO

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the coasts, air, and water of Trinidad and Tobago, and the fishing communities of the southwest peninsula proved that even in a country built on oil, gas, and heavy industry, ordinary people can turn back a giant. In 2006, the world's largest aluminium producer signed a deal to build a vast smelter — a project of around one and a half billion US dollars — at Chatham and Cap-de-Ville, on a coast of fishing villages, mangroves, and coconut estates that had already watched liquefied-gas plants rise and the fish grow scarce. The communities said no. Farmers and fishers, Indo- and Afro-Trinidadian, joined with environmentalists, scientists, and activists from the towns; they held all-night vigils outside the Prime Minister's office, marched, rallied, and kept a round-the-clock camp at the site to block the soil tests and the bulldozers. By the end of 2006 the government dropped the Cap-de-Ville smelter; by 2008 the company withdrew from the country altogether, its own records showing it feared the people's hostility; and by 2010 the second smelter planned at La Brea was scrapped as well. Two smelters that would have poisoned the peninsula's air and water were stopped by the people who lived there. The details are worth setting down, because they show how such a fight is won. The first plant, proposed by a Pittsburgh-based giant that is the world’s leading producer of aluminium, was to be an enormous works — hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year, with an anode plant and a cast house — on an estate of coconut groves and fishing villages at the southwestern tip of the island. Its opponents were not professional activists but the people of the place: farmers and fishers who feared the fluoride and the emissions, the cancer and the asthma, and the ruin of the waters they fished. They kept a camp at the gate that turned back the soil-testing crews, they carried their protest to the capital and the Prime Minister’s own door, and they held out. The government dropped that site at the end of 2006; the company left the country two years later; and a second smelter, already half-built at another site with foreign contractors on the ground, was cancelled by an incoming government in 2010.

The Trinidad and Tobago story teaches two things about defending coast and community here — including its honest limits, which this guide states from the outset. The first is the pattern that runs through every guide: not a single silver bullet, but the combination of every lever — the affected community, the documentation, the coalition, the protest, and the country's attention — pressed together. The second is distinctly Trinbagonian, and it was forged on a threatened coast in a petro-state. **Even in a country whose wealth and politics are built on oil, gas, and heavy industry, a united coastal community — fishers, farmers, and their allies — can use sustained protest, the health-and-pollution argument, the environmental-clearance process, and the nation's attention to stop a giant polluting project.** That power — the united community, the protest and the camp, the health case, the clearance process, and the public — is Trinidad and Tobago's distinctive lever, and this guide is built around it. What made the anti-smelter fight work as a model, and what a later campaign can copy, was above all its unity across the lines that so often divide. The peninsula’s villages were mixed — Indo- and Afro-Trinidadian, farming and fishing families whom a promise of jobs might easily have set against one another — and the fight’s strength lay in holding them together around a shared refusal to bear the poison. To that unity it added a camp that physically obstructed the works, a health case grounded in the science of the emissions, and a story compelling enough to make the whole small nation take notice. Unite across the divides, hold the ground, make the health case, and turn the country — that is the template.

But the same story carries honest warnings, and this guide will not hide them. The victory came in **stages, over years**, and leaned partly on forces beyond the community — a **global recession** that cooled the company's appetite, and a **change of government** that scrapped the second smelter — not on protest alone. Trinidad and Tobago is a **petro-state**, structurally driven toward heavy industry, with a **powerful energy and industrial lobby** and a real hunger for the jobs such projects promise, so the pressure never fully ends. And the communities themselves were **not always united** — some, desperate for work, wanted the smelter — so the fight had to win its own people as well as the government. It is worth being candid about how much the jobs promise complicated the fight, because it will complicate any fight like it. In communities that had long felt neglected and hungry for work, the offer of employment at a great industrial plant was genuinely tempting, and some residents — near the second site especially — argued loudly for the smelter as their chance to rise out of hardship. A campaign against heavy industry in a place that needs jobs cannot simply dismiss that hope; it must take it seriously, answer it honestly with the true costs to health and livelihood, and where it can, point toward a better and safer way to prosperity, because a fight that loses its own community’s hungry has already half lost. This guide is written with both truths in view: the real, distinctive power of a coastal community that stands together and fights — and the staged wins, the outside forces, the petro-state's pressure, and the divisions that qualify it.

Most communities do not know HOW to make that power effective. They face a project and assume "the government and the investors have decided and nothing can be done," and never organise, never document, never protest, never invoke the clearance process or the health case. They generate private fear without a public claim. The gap between a community that saves its coast and one that loses it is rarely courage — the peninsula's people showed it in abundance. It is method: knowing how to unite the community, document the harm, protest with discipline, wield the clearance process, and turn the nation.

**This guide shows what actually works — inside the Trinbagonian system as it is.**

## The Strategic Framework

Effective campaigns follow the same underlying pattern, whether the goal is to stop a smelter, a petrochemical plant, a quarry, or another destructive project:

```
STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION  (find the decision, the clearance, and the leverage)
        |
STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION          (build proof of the harm and the flaws)
        |
STEP 3: LOCAL OPPOSITION        (unite the community and hold the ground)
        |
STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES        (the environmental clearance and the courts)
        |
STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY         (make it national)
```

These steps operate **simultaneously, not in sequence**. Documentation feeds the clearance challenge and the public argument at once; the community unites and holds the ground; a protest camp or a clearance objection is a headline and a headline builds the coalition. The community that runs all five together, each reinforcing the others, is the one that wins — a project stopped, a clearance refused, a coast held. The peninsula's people did exactly this — they documented the harm, united the community, kept the camp, challenged the clearance, and turned the nation.

## The Levers That Are Distinctly Strong Here

Every country's system has its own pressure points. Trinidad and Tobago's give communities a distinctive set:

- **The united coastal community.** A community of **fishers, farmers, and neighbours standing together** can hold the ground and hold out, as the peninsula's did. The value of this lever is that a project can be delayed, obstructed, and ultimately defeated by nothing more than a community’s refusal to yield the ground and its willingness to stand together over a long fight, which needs no wealth and no permission. A united community controls the site the project needs, commands the local knowledge the investor lacks, and presents a human face that a distant company and a calculating government find hard to override; and its unity, once genuine, is the hardest thing for the other side to break, which is why so much of the pressure a campaign will face is aimed precisely at splitting it.
- **Sustained protest and the camp.** **Vigils, marches, and a round-the-clock protest camp** at the site physically obstruct and dramatise, as the anti-smelter camp did.
- **The health-and-pollution argument.** The case from **health — fluoride, cancer, asthma — and pollution of air, water, and fishing grounds** is powerful and unifying. This lever is distinctively strong because health is a concern that crosses every divide of race, class, and party in a way that few others do: a farmer and a fisher, an Indo- and an Afro-Trinidadian family, a supporter and an opponent of the government may disagree on much, but none of them wants their children breathing fluoride or their water poisoned. A campaign that centres the concrete, documented threat to the community’s health — the specific emissions, the specific illnesses, the specific harm to the fish people eat — speaks to a fear the whole community shares, and it was exactly this shared fear for health that held the peninsula’s diverse villages together against the smelter.
- **The environmental-clearance process.** The **required environmental assessment and clearance** are a real procedural obstacle and a lever.
- **The nation's attention.** A **national story** in a small country moves the government, as the smelter fight moved it.

## What This Guide Will Not Pretend

It would be dishonest to promise these levers deliver clean, quick stops. The smelter win came in **stages** and leaned partly on a **recession and a change of government**. The **petro-state's pressure** for heavy industry is structural and relentless. And **divided communities**, hungry for jobs, can undercut a fight. This guide takes those limits seriously, names them plainly, and shows you how to use the real, distinctive tools Trinidad and Tobago offers — the united community, the protest, the health case, and the clearance process — as the peninsula's people used them to turn back two smelters.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

You cannot pull a lever you cannot see. Before you spend a single week, map who actually decides your project's fate, at which level, and where along that path the decision — or the leverage — is weakest.

## The Levels of Decision

A Trinbagonian development or industrial project runs through several tracks at once, and each is a place to intervene.

**The government and the state companies.** The **government and the state energy and industrial companies** drive the big projects, often as partners with the investor, as the government partnered on the smelters. They approve, finance, and champion them — and can be moved by public pressure and the ballot box, as the second smelter was scrapped by a new government. This point about the ballot box deserves emphasis, because it is a distinctive feature of the Trinbagonian fight: the government is not only a permitting authority but the active champion and often the part-owner of the big industrial projects, which means it can be held to account not merely through the courts but at the polls. A community’s cause that becomes a national issue can shift votes, and a government that stakes its standing on a project the public comes to fear can pay for it at an election, as the change of government that finally killed the second smelter showed; a campaign should therefore weigh the political calendar and the electoral stakes as real levers.

**The investors.** **Foreign and domestic investors** — an aluminium giant, a petrochemical company, a foreign state partner — propose and build the projects.

**The Environmental Management Authority.** The **EMA** grants the **Certificate of Environmental Clearance** on the basis of an environmental assessment — the process where a project is most exposed to challenge.

**The courts.** Trinidad and Tobago's **courts** can review the clearance and the decisions — a real, if slower, lever.

**The community and the coalition.** The **coastal communities, the environmental and fishers' organisations, the scientists, and the public** are the force that documents, protests, challenges, and mobilises — the coalition that stopped the smelters.

## The Journey of a Project

A typical project moves from a government and investor decision, through the **environmental assessment and the Certificate of Environmental Clearance**, toward construction — with the **community, the courts, and the public** able to protest, document, challenge, and mobilise at any point. **Every one of those stages is a door you can put your foot in.** The distinctive Trinbagonian doors are the **united community and the protest camp**, the **health-and-pollution argument**, the **environmental-clearance process**, and the **national attention**. Find out exactly where your project stands, and, crucially, **whether the clearance has been granted or is pending**, **whether the health and pollution harm can be shown**, and **whether the community can be united and hold the ground**, because those are often where a project can be stopped.

## Follow the Project, the Clearance, and the Harm

Behind a project stand a proponent — an investor, the government, or both — and the officials who approve it. Trace who profits, who decided, and — importantly in Trinidad and Tobago — **whether the environmental clearance was properly granted**, **what the project does to the air, the water, the health, and the fishing and farming**, **whether the community was heard**, and **who bears the harm and who takes the gain**. Two questions unlock most Trinbagonian fights: **Is the clearance sound, and can the health-and-pollution harm be shown?** and **Can the community be united to protest and hold the ground?** The clearance and the community are, together, the heart of most Trinbagonian campaigns. The two reinforce one another in a way particular to these industrial fights: the community’s unity and its stand create the pressure and the delay, while the environmental clearance provides the legal and procedural ground on which a project can be formally halted or its permit undone. A community that holds the ground but never contests the clearance may find the works resume the moment the camp tires; one that contests the clearance but cannot hold its community together may win a paper point while the political will to build proceeds. The anti-smelter fight, like the strongest campaigns here, pressed both — the united community at the gate and the flawed clearance before the authorities.

## The Overseers

Finally, know the bodies and forces that can check the deciders, because they are levers too. The **courts**, which can review the clearance; the **Environmental Management Authority and the clearance process**; the **environmental and fishers' organisations**; the **scientists and the university**; the **free press**; and the **public and the voters**, who can turn on a project and a government. In Trinidad and Tobago, the **united community and its protest, the health case, and the clearance process**, carried by the **national attention**, are the most distinctive and decisive levers. Each is a separate front, and a project with a contestable clearance, a demonstrable health and pollution harm, a community united and camped against it, and the nation watching rarely proceeds untouched — as the smelters did not. You do not have to choose one front: the same case — the flawed clearance, the health harm, the injustice of a community made to bear another’s poison — can go to the EMA, to the courts, to the press, and to the voters all at once, and each front that opens strengthens the others and helps sustain the community’s stand. A Trinbagonian campaign’s power comes not from any single lever but from pressing several together — the united community at the gate, the flawed clearance before the authorities, and the health harm before the nation — so that a government and an investor facing a camped community, a contestable clearance, and a roused electorate at once find a project they can no longer easily build, as they found the smelters.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective campaign begins by finding the exact decision that governs the project — and the leverage the community holds, which in Trinidad and Tobago is often the environmental clearance, the health harm, and the community's ability to unite and hold the ground. Vague opposition goes nowhere; a fight aimed at "the smelter that would poison our air and fishing grounds, whose clearance is not yet granted" is a campaign — the campaign that stopped the smelters. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. Where does the clearance stand?** This is the first Trinbagonian question. Has the **Certificate of Environmental Clearance** been granted, or is the **environmental assessment** still pending — because a clearance not yet granted, or granted on a flawed assessment, is a project's central vulnerability. This should be among the first things a campaign establishes, because the stage of the clearance largely determines the shape of the fight. A clearance not yet granted can be opposed within the assessment process itself, where a community’s objections and evidence must be considered before any permit issues; a clearance already granted must be challenged after the fact, usually in the courts, on the ground that it was unlawfully or improperly given. Determining exactly where in the process the project stands — assessment pending, clearance granted, works begun — tells a campaign at once which avenue is open and how urgent its intervention must be.

**2. What is the health and pollution harm?** What does the project do to the **air, the water, the health — the fluoride, the emissions, the cancer risk — and the fishing and farming** the community lives on, as the smelters threatened the peninsula's health and catch? Give particular weight to documenting the specific pollutants and their specific effects, because vague fears of “pollution” persuade far less than concrete, named harms. A smelter’s fluoride emissions, for instance, have well-understood effects on health and on the surrounding environment, and a campaign that can name the pollutant, cite its documented dangers, and show how it would reach the community’s air, water, and food holds a far stronger case than one that speaks only of general contamination. The peninsula’s fight drew much of its force from making the threat specific and scientific, and a campaign should invest in that same concreteness.

**3. Can the community be united and hold the ground?** Can the **community — fishers, farmers, neighbours — be united**, across any divides, to protest and, if needed, hold the ground, as the peninsula united and camped? This question of unity deserves the most honest possible answer at the outset, because a divided community is a campaign’s greatest weakness, and the other side knows it. Assess frankly where the community stands — who fears the project, who hopes for its jobs, who is undecided — and treat the winning and holding of the community’s own unity as a central task of the campaign, not a given. The peninsula succeeded because it kept its diverse villages together around a shared refusal; a campaign that neglects this internal work, assuming the community will simply stand as one, may find its stand crumbling under the pressure and the promises the other side will deploy.

**4. Who bears the harm, and who takes the gain?** Who bears the **pollution and the risk** — the community — and who takes the **profit and the jobs** — the investor, the state, some residents — because naming this honestly is the heart of the fight, and the jobs promise is what divides a community.

**5. Who decides, who benefits, and who can be engaged?** Which government body, state company, and investor are behind it, who profits; and which courts, the EMA, organisations, scientists, press, and public can be engaged. This maps your levers and audiences at once.

## A Worked Example

Suppose a polluting industrial plant is proposed on a coast the community fishes and farms, its environmental clearance not yet granted.

Working the five questions: where does the **clearance** stand? What is the **health and pollution harm**? Can the **community unite and hold the ground**? Who bears the harm and who gains? And the deciders are the government, the state company, and the investor, with the courts, the EMA, the press, and the public as the pressure points.

Within a season you have moved from "they will build it" to a target list: document the harm; unite the community; protest and hold the ground; challenge the clearance; and turn the nation. That is a campaign, not a complaint — and it draws directly on how the smelters were stopped. Notice what the exercise did: it turned a frightening arrival of a giant project into a set of concrete, winnable tasks — track the clearance, document the harm, unite the community, hold the ground, turn the nation — each tied to a specific lever that Trinidad and Tobago genuinely provides. That reframing, from helplessness to a plan built on the community’s real strengths, its unity and its coast, is the difference the five questions make, and two of them, where the clearance stands and whether the community can be united across the jobs divide, often decide how strong a hand the community holds.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on. A court, the EMA, a journalist, and the public all move on evidence, not indignation — and in Trinidad and Tobago, the health harm, the flawed clearance, and the threat to the fishing and farming are precisely what a campaign turns into pressure, so document them well. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Project, the Clearance, and the Assessment

Get the documents the decision rests on. Obtain the **project plans, the environmental assessment, and the Certificate of Environmental Clearance** (or track its pending application) — and scrutinise the assessment for flaws: was the health impact faced, the pollution honestly modelled, the alternatives and the affected community properly considered? Establish exactly **where the clearance stands**, because a clearance not yet granted, or granted on a flawed assessment, is the project's central vulnerability. The gap between what the clearance process requires and what was done is where many Trinbagonian cases are grounded. The environmental assessment behind the clearance repays especially close and, where possible, expert scrutiny, because it is both legally required and technically dense, and it is in its detail that a project’s vulnerabilities hide — the fluoride emissions understated, the health effects glossed, the impact on the fishing grounds ignored, the community’s exposure downplayed. A project driven hard by the state and a powerful investor is often pushed through the assessment stage faster than careful work allows, and a community that reads the assessment closely, ideally with a scientist’s help, rather than assuming it sound, frequently finds there the very flaw on which a challenge to the clearance can be built.

## Layer 2: The Harm to Health, Water, and Livelihood

Document the reality the paper understates, and put **health, water, and livelihood** at the centre. Record the **harm** — the air pollution and fluoride, the health risks of cancer and asthma, the poisoning of water and fishing grounds, the loss of farming and fishing — that the project would cause, as the peninsula documented the threat to its health and its catch. Document what the community **stands to lose**. Photograph, measure, and date it. Record **testimony** from fishers and farmers, and gather **expert and scientific evidence**, which Trinidad and Tobago's scientists and university supply. This is the human case that unites the community and moves the nation.

## Layer 3: The Flaw, the Harm, and the Injustice

Finally, document the case. Is the **clearance flawed or pending** — the assessment dishonest, the health impact ignored? Is the **harm** to health, water, and livelihood real and grave? And is there an **injustice** — the community bearing the pollution while others take the profit? Each is a separate ground — for the courts, the campaign, and the nation — and together they make a case that is hard to wave away. This is the layer that most often decides Trinbagonian fights, because here a project sold as development is revealed to rest on a flawed clearance, to threaten health and livelihood, and to make a community bear the harm for another's gain — exactly the case that stopped the smelters. The strength of assembling all three grounds together — the flawed or pending clearance, the health harm, and the injustice — is that they serve different fronts and reinforce one another: the clearance is what the authorities and the courts can act on, the health harm is what unites the community and rouses the nation, and the injustice of a community made to bear another’s poison is what gives the fight its moral force. A campaign that documents only the harm may move hearts without a legal hook; one that documents only the clearance flaw may win a technical point without the public behind it; the campaigns that prevail here, as the anti-smelter fight did, build all three and press them together.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

This is the practical companion to Step 2: the specific records that decide Trinbagonian cases, and where to obtain them. You will not need every item; you will need the few that establish the flawed or pending clearance, the health harm, and the injustice.

## The Project, the Clearance, and the Paper Trail

- **The project plans and the agreement** — the terms of the project, and the state's role in it.
- **The environmental assessment** — the study behind the clearance, and often the most vulnerable to challenge.
- **The Certificate of Environmental Clearance** — whether granted, pending, or refused, the central document.
- **The health and pollution data** — the emissions, the fluoride, the health projections, the water and air data.
- **The investor and the beneficiaries** — who profits, and where the project sits in the state's industrial plans.

## How to Get Them

The plans, assessments, and clearance are matters of record, obtainable from the EMA and the relevant ministries and through freedom-of-information requests; the health and pollution data through the assessment, independent measurement, and the scientists. Lean on **allies**: Trinidad and Tobago's environmental and fishers' organisations, its scientists and the university, and the experienced campaigners who fought the smelters, who know the clearance process and the health case. Move promptly: obtaining the assessment and tracking the clearance early lets you object before it is granted and unite the community while the fight is fresh, as the anti-smelter camp formed while the tests were still being attempted. Timing matters here and repays acting on early: the assessment, the clearance, and the terms of the project are most open to challenge, and the community most able to unite and hold the ground, while the project is still being decided and construction has not begun, because once the clearance is granted and the contractors and equipment arrive, the momentum of committed money and machinery becomes formidable. The second smelter, remember, was already half-built when it was finally stopped, which is far harder than stopping a project on paper; a community that acts early, tracking the clearance and uniting its people while the ground is still whole, gives itself by far the best chance.

## The Ground Evidence

- **The harm** — the pollution, the fluoride, the health risk, the poisoned water and fishing grounds, documented.
- **The flawed or pending clearance** — the gaps in the assessment, the missing health analysis, the stage of the process.
- **The injustice** — who bears the harm and who takes the gain.
- **The health and pollution data** — the numbers that show the danger.
- **Testimony and science** — from fishers, farmers, communities, and the scientists who know the emissions and the health effects.

## Organise It So It Persuades

Keep a **master timeline** of the project, the clearance, the assessment, the harm, and every step of the fight — the spine of any case or campaign. Keep a **document index** so any fact can be sourced in seconds. And keep an **argument bank** (developed in a later section) that pairs each fact with what it proves, to whom. A well-ordered file is not bureaucracy; it is what lets a fisher, a scientist, a lawyer, a journalist, a judge, and a member of the public all see the same clear picture fast, and it guards against the campaign's own worst enemy, a claim that cannot be sourced when the investor or the state pushes back. In a country where the clearance and the health case decide, the community with the better-kept file — above all the clearest proof of the health harm and the clearance's flaws — is very often the one that wins, as the anti-smelter coalition won.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION — HOW TO UNITE THE COMMUNITY AND HOLD THE GROUND

Evidence and clearance do not win on their own. A united community does — and in Trinidad and Tobago, the anti-smelter fight proved that a coast standing together, camped and determined, can turn back the world's largest producer. A campaign's strength here is that community: united across every divide, enduring, and, where it must be, immovable.

## Phase 1: The Core and the Community

Start with a committed core rooted in the **whole affected community** — the fishers, the farmers, the families — because in Trinidad and Tobago the community's unity is the decisive lever. Assign roles — documentation, legal and clearance liaison, the protest and the camp, media, and coalition liaison. Agree on the goal — the project stopped, the clearance refused, the coast held — and on what the community will and will not accept. Draw on the community's own strength and its bonds, as the peninsula drew on its tight-knit villages, Indo- and Afro-Trinidadian together.

## Phase 2: The Broad Coalition

Then widen deliberately. Bring in the **whole community and the neighbouring villages**; the **environmental and fishers' organisations**, which bring the campaigning and the clearance expertise; the **scientists and the university**, whose evidence on the emissions and the health effects is decisive; **sympathetic lawyers**; the **urban activists and the wider public**; and the **press**, because a smelter on the coast is a national story. The anti-smelter fight drew its power from exactly this breadth — a united coast, scientists, urban allies, and a roused nation — which is precisely the spread a winning Trinbagonian campaign needs. The role of the scientists and the university in that coalition deserves particular emphasis, because in a fight decided so largely on questions of health and emissions, independent scientific authority is what turns a community’s fear into a credible public case. When the state and the investor produced experts to reassure the public that the smelter posed no real danger, it was the counter-testimony of respected independent scientists — on the fluoride, the health effects, and the pollution — that kept the health case alive and credible; a campaign should therefore recruit genuine scientific expertise early, because the battle of experts is one it cannot afford to concede.

**A worked coalition example.** Facing a polluting plant, the fishers and farmers form the base and hold the ground; the environmental and fishers' organisations bring the campaign and the clearance fight; the scientists supply the health evidence; the urban activists and the press carry it to the nation. No single group could carry it; together they cover the ground, the science, the law, and the nation — the model the anti-smelter coalition built. What is worth drawing from that model is how each element supplied what the others could not: the fishers and farmers brought the moral standing and the physical presence of those whose health and livelihood were at stake; the environmental and fishers’ organisations brought the campaigning experience and the knowledge of the clearance process; the scientists brought the authority to counter the investor’s reassurances; and the urban activists and the press brought the fight to the national stage. A community beginning such a fight should honestly assess which of these it already holds and which it must recruit, because it is the completeness of the coalition, not the passion of any single part, that proved decisive against the smelters.

## Phase 3: Sustaining It

Campaigns die of burnout — and a Trinbagonian fight can mean holding a camp and a campaign for months or years, as the anti-smelter fight ran from 2006 to 2010. Organise the **protest and the camp in shifts**, so the burden is shared. Meet on a rhythm. Give people concrete tasks. Mark small wins — the assessment obtained, an objection filed, a rally held, a court filing, a wave of coverage, a wavering official won over. Keep the community united and the burden shared, and guard against the **division and the jobs promise** — the offer of work to some, the argument that the community's own prosperity depends on the plant, the splitting of neighbour from neighbour — that will be used to break the community, as the pro-smelter side pressed the promise of jobs. Meeting the jobs argument honestly is one of the campaign’s hardest and most necessary tasks, because it cannot be won by denial. A community that genuinely needs work will not be persuaded by being told the jobs do not matter; it must be shown, honestly, what the jobs would truly cost — in health, in the fishing and farming livelihoods the project would destroy, in the long-term blight of a poisoned coast — and, wherever possible, pointed toward alternative paths to the prosperity it seeks. The strongest answer to the jobs promise is not to dismiss the hunger for work but to show that the project would feed it poorly and at a terrible price, while a healthier coast could sustain the community far longer. It helps, too, to be specific about the nature of the jobs on offer, because the promise is often larger in the telling than in the reality: heavy industrial plants of this kind frequently employ fewer local people than promised once built, bring in outside labour and contractors for the skilled work, and offer their jobs at the cost of the fishing, farming, and tourism livelihoods the community already has. A campaign that can show, concretely, how few lasting local jobs the project would truly create, and how many existing livelihoods it would destroy, turns the jobs argument from the other side’s strongest card into one of its weakest.

## Phase 4: Holding the Ground

Here is Trinidad and Tobago's distinctive strength. Where a project cannot be stopped fast enough by other means, a community can **stand physically in the way** — as the peninsula kept a round-the-clock camp at the site, blocking the soil tests and the works. Organise the **protest and the camp** with discipline: keep it **peaceful**, share the shifts, document everything, and know your rights, because the authorities and the company may push back, and a disciplined, peaceful, well-documented stand keeps the community's legitimacy and the public's sympathy. The point is not confrontation for its own sake; it is to hold the ground — to keep the machines and the tests out — while the health case, the clearance challenge, and the nation's attention do their work, as they did against the smelters. Understand that this is a hard, demanding course, to be entered with broad support and open eyes, and paired always with the health case and the clearance fight that give the stand its legal and moral force. A few practical principles make such a stand sustainable rather than a brief flare. Organise the camp as a rota so that the burden of watching the gate is shared across the whole community rather than borne by a heroic few; keep it visibly peaceful and well-documented, so that any heavy-handedness by the authorities or the company rebounds on them rather than on you; and connect the physical stand at all times to the lawful fight — the clearance objection, the court challenge, the health case — so that the camp is understood not as lawlessness but as a community defending its health while it pursues every legal avenue. The peninsula’s camp endured because it was disciplined, shared, and tied to a larger, legitimate cause.

# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES — THE ENVIRONMENTAL CLEARANCE AND THE COURTS

Trinidad and Tobago's environmental-clearance process and its courts are a real lever — the required assessment and Certificate of Environmental Clearance are a genuine obstacle a project must clear, and a flawed or missing clearance is a project's central legal vulnerability. You do not need a fast courtroom victory to win: a clearance refused, an assessment reopened, a project delayed while you hold the ground, or a project abandoned under pressure can each be a real victory. Engage lawyers and scientists early — but understand the tools.

## The Environmental Clearance

The first and most distinctive lever is the **environmental clearance**. A major project requires an **environmental assessment and a Certificate of Environmental Clearance** from the EMA, and a clearance **granted on a flawed assessment** — one that understated the health harm, ignored the pollution, or failed to consider the community — can be challenged, as can the process itself. Track the clearance, object during the assessment, and challenge a flawed grant; the clearance is the procedural heart of most Trinbagonian industrial fights, and the point where a project is most exposed. Understanding the clearance process in detail is therefore worth a campaign’s early effort: learn what the assessment must contain, what opportunities the process gives the public to object and to be heard, what standards the authority must apply, and what deadlines govern each stage, because these are the specific hooks on which objections and challenges hang. A community that masters the process can intervene at exactly the right moments with exactly the right objections, rather than protesting in general terms after the decisive stages have passed, and it was disciplined engagement with the clearance process, alongside the protest, that gave the anti-smelter fight its legal spine.

## The Courts

The second lever is the **courts**. Trinidad and Tobago's **courts** can review whether the clearance and the decisions were lawfully made, and whether the EMA and the authorities followed the law and their own process. Use the courts to challenge a flawed clearance, to demand a proper assessment, and to defend the community's health and rights — and use the time a challenge buys to hold the ground and turn the nation, because delay keeps a project open to defeat.

## Health, Rights, and the Injustice

The third lever, distinctive to fights like the smelter, is the **health, the rights, and the injustice**. Frame the fight around the **community's health and its right not to bear another's pollution**, because this is both a legal and a moral ground that moves courts, the EMA, and the public — the case that a community should not be made to breathe fluoride and lose its fishing so that others may profit. The health-and-injustice frame, paired with the clearance challenge, is what gave the anti-smelter fight its force.

## Where to Turn and With Whom

Clearance objections and challenges go through the **EMA and the courts**; the health and rights case through the same and the public; the whole effort through the **organisations, scientists, and lawyers** who know the routes. Get help early: Trinidad and Tobago's environmental and fishers' organisations, its scientists, and its public-interest lawyers know the clearance process, the health case, and the courts, and their support was central to stopping the smelters. Reaching these allies early rather than late can shape the whole course of a fight, because the earliest decisions — whether to object within the assessment or to challenge a granted clearance, on what ground, within what deadline, and how to frame the health case — often determine whether a campaign can succeed at all, and the organisations and lawyers who fought the smelters carry hard-won knowledge of how the clearance process and the courts actually work here. A community that brings in that experience at the first sign of a threat, rather than after a clearance has issued and the contractors have arrived, gives itself the benefit of it when it matters most. Help engaged early — before the clearance is granted, while the community can still unite — is worth far more than help sought after, and in Trinidad and Tobago the strongest strategy marries the **clearance challenge and the health case** to the **united community and its protest**, with the courts and the nation behind them.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

You have a file of evidence. Now you must turn it into arguments — because the same fact persuades a court, the EMA, a journalist, a scientist, and the public in different ways. Learning to translate your evidence for each audience is what lets a community reach every lever at once.

## One Fact, Many Arguments

Take a single documented fact — say, that a smelter would emit fluoride and other pollutants over a fishing and farming coast, threatening cancer and asthma and poisoning the fishing grounds, on a clearance that never honestly faced the health harm. That one fact becomes:

- **A legal argument:** the clearance rests on an assessment that ignored the health impact, and can be challenged — the case before the EMA and the courts.
- **A health argument:** the project would poison the air and cause cancer and asthma — the case that unites the community and moves the nation.
- **A livelihood argument:** it would destroy the fishing and farming the community lives on — the case that binds the coast together.
- **An injustice argument:** the community would bear the poison while others take the profit — the case that shames the project.
- **A public argument:** they would poison our coast and our children for someone's smelter — the case that rouses the nation.

Same fact, five arguments, five audiences. Go through your strongest facts and, for each, ask what it proves to each audience. That table is your campaign's argument bank — and in Trinidad and Tobago the arguments that most often decide things are the **health** one, because it unites and rouses, and the **injustice** one, because it shames a project that makes a community bear another's harm. Keep the arguments anchored to the same documented facts, so a claim made to a reporter can be backed in the data and a claim made to a judge can be proven by the assessment; consistency across audiences is itself persuasive.

## Match the Argument to the Audience

**The courts and the EMA** want the flawed clearance and the ignored health impact framed against the law and the process, with the documents that prove them.

**The government** responds to the health case, the national attention, the legal risk, and — as the ballot box showed — the political cost.

**Journalists** need a specific, vivid story with a hook: a fishing coast, its children, and its catch, threatened by fluoride and cancer from a giant's smelter.

**The public** needs the argument simple and true: our health, our fishing, our coast, poisoned for someone's profit.

**The scientists** respond to the honest technical case on the emissions, the fluoride, and the health effects.

**The community** needs to see the harm is real and the fight winnable, so it stays united and holds the ground, as the peninsula held it.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY — HOW COVERAGE SHIFTS OUTCOMES

Media is not vanity; it is a lever — and in Trinidad and Tobago, a small country where a national story moves the government, coverage that dramatises the health harm and the injustice is among the most powerful levers of all. Coverage rouses the nation, shifts opinion, and raises the political cost. The anti-smelter fight succeeded in large part because it made a national controversy of a coast threatened by fluoride and cancer — and of a community camped, defiant, and unwilling to bear another's poison.

## Build the Story

Reporters and the public need specifics: a named place, a documented harm, and a human stake. "A project is controversial" is not a story; "a giant's smelter would poison a fishing coast with fluoride, threatening cancer and asthma and destroying the catch, while the community camps at the gate to stop it" is — the story that stopped the smelters. Lead with your strongest verified facts — the health harm, the pollution, the threat to the fishing — and offer a human voice: a fisher, a mother, a scientist. In Trinidad and Tobago the story of a coast defending its health and its children against a polluting giant is one the nation responds to.

## Reach the Right Outlets

Work outward in rings. **Local and community voices** first. Then the **national outlets** — because in a small country the national conversation is where the pressure builds and the government feels it. Then the **international and diaspora ring** where the story resonates — because a giant multinational is sensitive to its global reputation, as the aluminium company was, and international and diaspora attention adds real pressure. Feed each ring what it needs, and let coverage in one pull in the next. The **national ring is decisive** in Trinidad and Tobago, because national opinion and the ballot box move the government, but the international ring pressures the multinational.

## Sustain It, and Keep It True

A single article changes little; a drumbeat changes decisions — and a Trinbagonian fight can run for years. Plan a sequence — the project, the health harm, the camp, the clearance, the government's retreat — so there is always a next hook, and build your own clear record so the story reaches the nation and endures. Keep messaging disciplined, accurate, and consistent; the investor and the state will seize on any exaggeration, and a credible, health-and-injustice-centred campaign is what moves a small nation. Verified, specific, health-and-coast-centred, and relentless — that is the coverage that shifts outcomes here, and that helped stop two smelters.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Written communications are how you request, object, create a record, and reach the bodies that can act — the authorities, the EMA, the investors, the courts, and the press. Each does double duty: it asks for something and it documents that you asked. Keep copies of everything, and send important items so that receipt is provable. Below are adaptable templates for the situations you will meet most often. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the clearance, the health, and the harm.

## 8A. Request for Information (Freedom of Information)

**To:** the EMA / the relevant ministry
**Subject:** Request for information on [project] at [place]

Under the freedom-of-information law, we, [community/organisation], request the **project plans, the environmental assessment, the Certificate of Environmental Clearance (or its application), and the health and pollution data** for [project] at [place]. This project affects the public health and interest, and the public has a right to know. Please provide the documents within the statutory period.

## 8B. Objection During the Clearance Process

**To:** the Environmental Management Authority
**Subject:** Objection to the environmental clearance for [project]

We, [community/organisation], object to the environmental clearance for [project]. The assessment **understated the health harm** — the fluoride, the emissions, the cancer risk — and the **pollution of our air, water, and fishing grounds**, and failed to face the harm to our community. We demand that the clearance be **refused** or the assessment redone, and we reserve our right to the courts.

## 8C. Court Challenge (with a Lawyer)

**To:** the courts (via counsel)
**Subject:** Challenge to the environmental clearance for [project]

On behalf of [community/organisation], we challenge the environmental clearance for [project] as **unlawful**: the assessment **ignored the health impact and the pollution**, and the clearance was granted **contrary to the process and the law**. We seek its **quashing** and the halting of works, and attach the record demonstrating the flaws and the health harm.

## 8D. Letter to the Investor

**To:** the investor / the company
**Subject:** The community's opposition to [project]

We write regarding [project] at [place]. The community opposes it: it would poison our air, water, and fishing grounds, threaten our health, and make us bear the harm for your profit. We call on you to **abandon** the project, and we advise that the community will unite, hold the ground, challenge the clearance, and draw the nation and the world to its defence, as the peninsula did against the smelter.

## 8E. Instructing a Lawyer / Organisation

**To:** a public-interest lawyer / environmental organisation
**Subject:** [Project] — the clearance, the health harm, and the challenge

We are opposing [project] at [place]. We attach the plans, the assessment and clearance documents, and our evidence of health harm, and seek advice on **challenging the clearance**, **the health and rights case**, and **defending the community**. Please advise on the strongest ground and the deadlines.

## 8F. Appeal to Scientists / Allies / Press

**To:** a scientist / an environmental organisation / a journalist
**Subject:** Support for [community]'s stand against [project]

We write regarding [project], which threatens our health and our fishing coast with [the pollution and the harm]. As the scientists and allies stood with the anti-smelter fight, we ask for your **expertise, evidence, and support**, and for the **attention** that can strengthen our stand. We attach our case and can host you at the coast.

## 8G. Email to a Reporter

**To:** [journalist]
**Subject:** Story tip: [specific finding] — [the coast / the project]

I follow your coverage of [beat]. We have something the public should know, with a clear hook: [documents] show [quantified finding — e.g. "a giant's smelter would poison a fishing coast with fluoride, threatening cancer and destroying the catch"]. It echoes the smelter fight. I can share the file and connect you with a fisher and a scientist. Could we talk this week?

## 8H. Public Statement

**To:** the public and the authorities
**Subject:** [Community/organisation] opposes [project]

We, [community/organisation], oppose [project], which would **poison our air, water, and fishing grounds**, threaten our health, and make us bear the harm for another's profit. As the peninsula's people stopped the smelters, we will unite, hold our ground, challenge the clearance, and call on the nation. We call on the government to protect its people, not to poison them, and we will not relent.

## Using the Templates Well

Send important communications so that receipt is provable, and log every one in your timeline — the record of a request, an objection, or a challenge is itself evidence, and exactly what the EMA, the courts, and the nation will heed. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the clearance, the health, and the harm; attach your evidence; and route them, where you can, through the community, the organisations, your lawyers, and the press. The reply, or the ruling, becomes part of your record, and in Trinidad and Tobago that record feeds the levers that matter most: the environmental-clearance challenge, the health-and-injustice case, the united community's stand, and the national attention.

# IF YOU HAVE LITTLE TIME OR FEW RESOURCES: THE RAPID-FIRE VERSION

Not everyone can hold a camp for years. With a few hours a week you can still do real damage to a bad project. In rough order of impact for the effort:

**1. Find where the clearance stands.** Learn whether the Certificate of Environmental Clearance is granted, pending, or refused, and get the assessment (Step 1, 8A).

**2. Document the health harm.** Establish the pollution, the fluoride, the health and fishing threat (Step 2).

**3. Object during the clearance.** Put your objection and the health harm on the record with the EMA (8B).

**4. Unite the community.** Bring the fishers, farmers, and neighbours together, across any divide (Step 3).

**5. Reach the scientists and organisations.** Connect with the university, the scientists, and the environmental and fishers' groups (8F, 8E).

**6. Rouse the nation.** Take the health harm and the injustice to the press and the public (Step 5, 8G).

**7. Hold the ground only if you must, and together.** As a last resort, with broad support, camp and obstruct (Step 3).

Do only the first three and you will have found the clearance, documented the harm, and put your objection on the record — the foundation of a Trinbagonian defence. Then come back and read the rest. Even the shortest version has a spine: find the clearance, document the harm, object.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Trinidad and Tobago's system is tilted in specific ways you must understand, so you neither waste effort nor miss the levers that work.

## Where the Tilt Shows

The tilt here is real and rooted in the economy. Trinidad and Tobago is a **petro-state**, its wealth and its politics built on oil, gas, and petrochemicals, so the government and the powerful are **structurally driven toward heavy industry**, and a project is presented as the nation's prosperity. A **strong energy and industrial lobby** — the chambers, the state companies, the investors — carries weight. The **jobs promise** is potent in communities that need work, and it is used to **divide** them. And the **environmental institutions can be under-resourced or pressured** to clear a favoured project. Where these tilts bite, a campaign that assumes the state will protect the coast over industry, or that a community will stay united against the promise of jobs, will be disappointed.

## What Still Works — And Some Is Distinctive

The decisive point is that even against this tilt, real levers remain.

**A united community can still turn back a giant.** No petro-state can easily move a coast that stands together and holds the ground, as the peninsula stood.

**The clearance is still a real obstacle.** The assessment and Certificate of Environmental Clearance genuinely constrain a project and can be challenged.

**The health case still moves the nation.** The fear of fluoride, cancer, and poisoned fish rouses a small country.

**The ballot box still matters.** A government that ignores a coast can be turned out, as the second smelter's fate showed.

**The multinational still fears its reputation.** A global company is sensitive to the hostility of a determined community, as the aluminium giant's own records showed.

## The Honest Frame

So the honest frame for Trinidad and Tobago is realistic but far from hopeless: the petro-state tilts hard toward industry, the jobs promise divides, and the win may come in stages and lean on outside forces, but the united community, the health case, the clearance, and the nation's attention give a determined coast real levers. So diagnose your fight honestly: track the **clearance**; document the **health harm**; unite the **community** across the jobs divide; protest and, where you must, **hold the ground**; challenge the **clearance**; and turn the **nation** — while understanding that the pressure is relentless and the win may take years and outside help. Do not assume the petro-state will protect the coast; the smelters were stopped because a community stood together and would not bear the poison.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO IS CAPTURED

Trinidad and Tobago has real and documented concerns about how large energy and industrial deals are made, and the closeness of the state, the investors, and the connected, so this section matters. When a project is driven not by the public interest but by the connected, or by a state company's and an investor's weight over the institutions, your strategy includes exposing that.

## How to Tell

Look for the signatures of capture. A **clearance granted against the evidence and the health of the community**; an **assessment shaded** to favour a chosen project; **close ties** between the state companies, the investors, and the government; a **project pushed** despite its harm because powerful interests profit; and **benefits to the connected** while the community bears the pollution. Trace the deal and the interests: who decided, who profits, whether the health and the coast were sacrificed for the connected. In Trinidad and Tobago, a polluting project cleared against a community's health is often not a mere policy choice but an arrangement that serves the powerful.

## Who Can Be Captured — and Who Is Harder

Be clear-eyed about where capture reaches. **Parts of the government, the state companies, and the environmental institutions** can be captured or pressured by powerful energy and industrial interests. But some forces are far harder to capture, and they are your targets: the **courts**, which can review a clearance; the **scientists and the university**, whose independent evidence carries weight; the **free press**; the **environmental and fishers' organisations**; and the **public and the voters**, who can turn on a project and a government. Capture thrives on the community's division and silence; your task is to unite the community, document the capture and the health harm, and bring it to the courts, the press, and the nation.

## What to Do

Do not assume a captured clearance cannot be undone. Instead, **document the capture precisely** — how the clearance was granted, against what health evidence, who profits — and take it to the forces that can act: the **courts**, to review the flawed clearance; the **scientists**, for independent evidence; the **free press and the public**, to expose it; and the **community's stand**, to hold the ground meanwhile. Pair the exposure with the health case and the clearance challenge, so the impropriety and the harm arrive together. In Trinidad and Tobago, exposing how a polluting project was cleared for the connected against a community's health, and rousing the nation against it, is often the way to stop it — as the exposure and the pressure helped stop the smelters.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

The five steps win when they run together. Here is how they fit across the life of a fight — which in Trinidad and Tobago turns on the united community, the health case, and the clearance.

## Early (Months 0–6): The Clearance, the Harm, and the Community

Pin down the project and the deciding bodies (Step 1); track the **clearance** and document the **health harm** (Step 2). Unite the **community** across any divide and prepare to hold the ground if needed (Step 3). Move at once on what is decisive: **request the information** (8A); **object during the clearance** (8B); reach **scientists, lawyers, and organisations** early (8E, 8F); and begin to **rouse the nation** (Step 5). This phase lays the foundation, and in Trinidad and Tobago the documented health harm and the united community established here are what win.

## Middle (Months 3–24+): Protest, Challenge, and Rouse

Sustain the community and, where needed, the **protest and the camp**, sharing the burden (Step 3). Turn your evidence into arguments for each audience (Turning Evidence). Mount the **clearance challenge and the court case** (Step 4). Build the media drumbeat from local to national to international, dramatising the health harm and the injustice (Step 5). Each front feeds the others. Do the time-critical things first — objecting before the clearance is granted, holding the ground before construction — because those chances do not return.

## Later (Months 6–many): The Retreat, and Vigilance

Pursue the decisive outcome — the **project stopped, the clearance refused, the coast held** — and then stay vigilant, because, as the smelter fight showed, a petro-state keeps pushing, a dropped project can be relocated or revived, and the win may come only in stages. Judge success honestly. Sometimes it is a project stopped outright. Sometimes it is a project dropped at one site, then abandoned as the pressure, a recession, or a new government finishes what the community began. Both are victories worth the fight.

## The Shape of a Campaign

```
MONTHS 0-6    Track the clearance + document the health harm | Unite the community | Object | Reach scientists + lawyers
MONTHS 3-24+  Protest + hold the ground | Clearance + court challenge | Rouse the nation | Media

```

# FINAL ASSESSMENT

Trinidad and Tobago gives communities a genuinely powerful — and distinctive — set of tools, and the honest measure of this guide is how squarely it faces both their strength and their limits.

The strengths are real and distinctive. A **united coastal community** — fishers, farmers, and neighbours standing together — can hold the ground and turn back a giant, as the peninsula turned back the world's largest aluminium producer. **Sustained protest and the camp** physically obstruct and dramatise a fight. The **health-and-pollution case** — the fluoride, the cancer, the poisoned fish — unites the community and rouses the nation. The **environmental-clearance process** is a real procedural obstacle a community can contest. And the **nation's attention**, in a small country, moves the government and the ballot box. These are the tools that let a fishing coast stop two smelters. They are, notably, tools that depend on unity, endurance, and a just cause rather than on wealth or power, which is why a coast of fishers and farmers could prevail over the world’s largest aluminium producer and its own government — and why, used with the same resolve, they remain available to communities across the country wherever heavy industry threatens a coast, an aquifer, or a people’s health.

The limits are just as real, and this guide names them. The smelter win came in **stages, over years**, and leaned partly on a **recession and a change of government**, not on protest alone. The **petro-state's pressure** for heavy industry is structural and relentless. And a **divided community**, hungry for jobs, can undercut a fight. No guide should pretend these away; in Trinidad and Tobago, the art is to unite the community across the jobs divide, to make the health case, to hold the ground and challenge the clearance, and to keep watch, because a petro-state never stops pushing.

So the realistic promise is this: **track the clearance; document the health harm; unite the community; protest and hold the ground; challenge the clearance; and turn the nation — running all of it together.** Do that, and where the community stands together and the harm is grave, you can stop a project and hold your coast, as the peninsula did; where the community is divided or the clearance sound, you can still win delay, a record, and a partial gain. Do it as the anti-smelter fight did — a coast that stood together, camped at the gate, made the health case, and turned back a giant.

A last word on standing together. Trinidad and Tobago's lesson is that even in a petro-state built for heavy industry, a united coast holds more power than the powerful want it to believe — that fishers and farmers, standing together and refusing to bear the poison, can turn back the world's largest producer — but that the power depends on unity, the win may come in stages, and the pressure never ends. The powerful count on your division, your fear, and your hunger for the jobs they dangle. Their advantage is the petro-state's momentum, the industrial lobby, and the promise of work that splits neighbour from neighbour. Yours is a community that can stand together, a health case that rouses a nation, a clearance process that constrains, courts that review, a ballot box that answers, and the proven fact that the peninsula stopped two smelters. Play to that. Keep the record and the proof of the health harm, keep the community united across the jobs divide and the burden shared, hold the ground and challenge the clearance, rouse the nation, and treat every objection, every filing, every day the camp holds, and every clearance refused as a stone in a wall that even the petro-state cannot easily climb.

The tools are here, and Trinidad and Tobago itself proved them: a coast of fishers and farmers, Indo- and Afro-Trinidadian together, that stood at the gate, made the case for its health and its children, and turned back two smelters that would have poisoned its air and its fishing grounds. What decides the outcome, in the end, is the unity of the community, the strength of the health case, the clearance process, and the nation's attention — and those a determined community can summon. Begin today, begin with the clearance and the health harm and the uniting of the community, and build from there. Communities before you have stood together and saved their coast — a smelter stopped, a clearance refused, a coast held. They did it with unity, endurance, and a just cause, against a petro-state’s momentum and a giant’s money, and at times against the tempting promise of jobs — which is exactly why your own community’s stand, added to theirs, is neither hopeless nor unimportant. What the peninsula's people won can be won again. So can yours. A coast that stood together and would not bear the poison, that kept its camp at the gate and its case before the nation until a giant left the country and a half-built smelter was cancelled, has shown what an ordinary community can do against the momentum of a petro-state; the united community, the protest, the health case, the clearance, and the nation’s attention are the method, and the proof that even two smelters can be turned back is now yours to take up and use.

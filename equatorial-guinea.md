# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN EQUATORIAL GUINEA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Equatorial Guinea must be approached in a way unlike almost any other nation in this series — from outside, through the world's frameworks, and with the greatest care — because it is among the most closed and repressive states on earth, where open domestic opposition is essentially impossible and gravely dangerous, and where the only levers that genuinely work are the international ones, applied from outside. This small Central African and Gulf of Guinea nation struck great reserves of offshore oil in the mid-1990s and became, on paper, one of the wealthiest states in Africa by the measure of its wealth per person; yet that wealth was captured by a tiny ruling elite while most of the population, especially on the poor and neglected mainland, remained impoverished, and the riches were spent on questionable grand projects and diverted into private assets abroad. Its environment bears the marks of that same extractive, captured system: the mainland rainforest, part of the great Congo Basin and Gulf of Guinea forests, is logged and cleared — even, in one notable case, to carve an entirely new capital out of the forest — while the coast and sea bear the pollution of oil and gas. And the oil that funded it all is now declining, a window steadily closing. There is no landmark victory at home to point to, and no one inside can safely resist. But the international levers are real.

Those who would defend Equatorial Guinea's environment and resources cannot do so at home, but the international tools are real. The distinctive one is **international anti-corruption and asset-recovery**: the wealth captured and moved abroad can be pursued through anti-corruption efforts and foreign courts, which have already reached the regime's assets overseas. The **oil and timber revenue** can be pressed toward transparency through the global resource-governance frameworks. The **Congo Basin and Gulf of Guinea rainforest**, logged and cleared, can be defended through international conservation. The **oil and gas pollution** of the coast and sea can be documented and raised through the international record. And the **world's frameworks, courts, and organisations** reach where domestic opposition cannot, while **rigorous documentation** grounds it all and needs no one inside. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Equatorial Guinea is among the most closed and repressive states on earth: open domestic opposition is impossible and gravely dangerous, so the work runs almost entirely through international channels, and anyone inside must be protected absolutely. The resource wealth has been captured by an entrenched, long-ruling elite, so change is slow and external. The oil is fading. And there is no landmark victory at home to point to. And, above all, no one inside can be exposed. So the work must be external, framed as international accountability and conservation, protecting anyone inside absolutely — and the honest prize is the captured wealth pursued, the revenue pressed toward transparency, the rainforest defended, and the pollution documented, not a project stopped from within.

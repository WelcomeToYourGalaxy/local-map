# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the air, water, and deserts of Turkmenistan — but here the honest starting point is unlike any other in this series, because Turkmenistan is one of the most closed and tightly controlled states on earth, where independent organising inside the country is nearly impossible and genuinely dangerous, and where the real work of opposing environmental harm must be done largely from outside, on behalf of a silenced people and a harmed world. Turkmenistan sits atop some of the planet's largest natural-gas reserves, and its aging, poorly maintained, and leaking gas infrastructure has made it one of the very worst **methane super-emitters** in the entire world — its fields, flare stacks, and pipelines venting into the sky a potent climate super-pollutant that satellites now measure directly from space, in staggering quantities that rival the emissions of whole industrial nations. It is also a land of environmental strain in other ways: a desert country drawing down shared rivers to irrigate its cotton, a shrinking inland sea on its border, and a vast burning gas crater set deep in its sands. But its people cannot speak of any of it: there is no independent press, no political opposition, no civil society, and those who dare to criticise the state face prison or worse. There is no landmark victory to point to, and the levers are almost entirely external. But those external levers — running through the satellites that no secrecy can blind, the world's climate diplomacy, the buyers of the gas, and the exile diaspora — are real.

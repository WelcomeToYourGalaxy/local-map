# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

The situation in Gabon is unusual and, in one respect, hopeful: Gabon still has almost all of its rainforest, and is the first African country to be **paid by the world to keep it standing**. Nearly nine-tenths of the land is covered by **Congo Basin rainforest** — a forest so vast it absorbs far more carbon than the whole country emits, home to forest elephants and great apes, one of the last great intact rainforests on earth. Over three decades Gabon held its deforestation to a fraction of a percent a year, created **thirteen national parks**, and, in a landmark deal, began receiving **international payments — through the Central African Forest Initiative and the carbon framework — for the emissions its standing forest prevents.** And yet the forest is not safe: illegal logging has been rising, palm-oil plantations have cleared tens of thousands of hectares, an iron-ore mega-project threatens to fragment a forested region, and the country's dependence on oil pushes it to convert forest for new revenue.

A community in Gabon works with real, if indirect, levers, all running through the green bargain the country has struck — being paid by the world to keep its forest standing. The **climate-finance framework** (CAFI and the carbon deal) pays for real protection, and its partners can be asked to **condition the money** on genuine protection and on the rights of forest communities. Gabon's **green commitments and reputation** as a conservation leader can be held to — its great vulnerability and its great lever are the same: the gap between that green promise and any destruction that betrays it. The **thirteen national parks** and the **forest law** protect land. And forest communities hold **land and tenure rights** the money was meant to secure. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The **green image is real but incomplete** — illegal logging has risen, and palm, mining, and oil press against the trees. The **money too rarely reaches forest communities** — the climate payments have been criticised as overstated and as flowing overwhelmingly to governments and schemes rather than to the forest peoples whose land the money was meant to secure. And the **politics are uncertain** — Gabon emerged from more than half a century of one family's rule into a transition whose environmental direction is not yet set, with civil-society space constrained, so caution and safety come first. So aim for what these levers can deliver, insisting that a country paid to conserve actually protect the forest and its people.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "the project" is weak. Being specific — this broken promise, this finance condition, this forest tenure — is strong. Answer five questions in writing, carefully.

**1. What is it, and who's behind and financing it?** Name the project (logging, palm, mining, road), the company, and any international lenders.

**2. What forest and community does it harm?** Document the **forest cleared or fragmented, the parks or protected forest touched, the communities' land taken** — the human and ecological cost.

**3. What green promise and money does it betray?** The conservation commitments, the CAFI and carbon deal, the certification — because **the gap between the green bargain and the destruction is your sharpest lever**.

**4. What stage is it at, and what's the next deadline?** Is an approval, assessment, or finance decision pending? Is clearing underway? These openings matter.

**5. Is the community's tenure at stake, who's affected, and who's at risk?** The forest communities and their land rights; and — carefully, in a constrained space — organisations and watchdogs. Mind everyone's safety.

**What this looks like in real life.** An iron-ore or palm project fragments a forested region and takes a community's land, in a country paid to keep its forest standing. The five questions yield: the company and its financiers; the forest and community harm; the green promise and climate money it betrays (the lever); an approval or clearing in play; and a community whose tenure is at stake. That's the Gabonese pattern — expose the green-promise gap, ask the finance partners to condition their money, invoke the parks and forest law, and anchor on the community's land rights.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

The difference between a community that can hold Gabon to its bargain and one that can't is documented proof of the betrayal. Build it in three layers, carefully.

**Layer one: the destruction and the community harm.** Document the **forest cleared or fragmented, the parks or protected forest touched, the land taken from forest communities** — with dated, located evidence (maps, imagery where available), testimony, and the harm to tenure and livelihood.

**Layer two: the green promise and the money.** Get Gabon's **conservation commitments**, the **CAFI and carbon deal** terms, the **certification** the company claims, and identify the **gap** — how the destruction betrays the bargain the country is paid and praised for, and how the money failed to secure the forest or the communities' tenure.

**Layer three: the finance and reputation map.** Trace the **climate-finance partners** and the project's **financiers**, so you know whom to ask to condition the money and hold to their standards. Walking into the finance partners, the parks and forest authorities, international watchdogs, or the press with documented destruction and a documented broken promise is what makes them act.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN TAJIKISTAN

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the rivers, valleys, and water of Tajikistan — but this guide must begin with unusual honesty and unusual caution, because Tajikistan is among the hardest places in this series to oppose a project from within. Tajikistan is a mountainous, landlocked, authoritarian state, ruled by one president since 1992, where independent civil society is rated "closed," where human-rights defenders and journalists are imprisoned and attacked, and where most people, in the words of those who monitor the country, live in a climate of fear in which they would not dare to openly oppose a project. And the project that dominates the country's future is one of the largest in the world: the **Rogun Dam**, planned to be the tallest dam on earth, rising on the Vakhsh River, costing more than eight billion dollars, expected to displace tens of thousands of people, to flood an internationally protected floodplain, and to reduce the flow of the **Amu Darya** — the great river of Central Asia — on which millions downstream in Afghanistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan depend. There is no landmark victory to point to, and the dam is pressing ahead. But the levers, running almost entirely from outside, are real.

Because Tajikistan's great project is a transboundary mega-dam financed by international development banks, the levers that matter are almost entirely external, and they are real. The distinctive lever is the **financiers' safeguard policies and accountability mechanisms**: the development banks financing the dam are bound by social and environmental safeguards, and maintain complaint and accountability mechanisms — such as the World Bank's Inspection Panel — to which affected people's concerns can be carried from safer ground. The **transboundary water law and the downstream states** are a second: the Vakhsh feeds the Amu Darya, shared with Afghanistan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan, so the international law of shared rivers and the downstream nations' concern are a genuine lever. The **international heritage and wetland significance** of the protected floodplain the dam would flood — the Tigrovaya Balka reserve, a Ramsar wetland of international importance and a candidate for World Heritage listing — opens a further international channel. And the **international coalitions and documentation** carry the affected people's concerns, and the record of the resettlement's rights violations, from safety. These tools are general — the financier-accountability, transboundary-law, heritage, and coalition levers reach any internationally-financed transboundary project, with the Rogun dam the central case. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Opposition inside Tajikistan is dangerous, and open protest against the regime's signature project is close to impossible: civil-society space is closed, defenders and journalists are jailed, and the resettlement of tens of thousands has already brought documented rights violations, with families given land but no houses and inadequate compensation, and little safe way to complain. The dam is the regime's prestige project, decades in the making, and the government is committed to it. The environmental assessment has been criticised as outdated and incomplete, and the project is pressing ahead — this is not a story of anyone stopping it. So the work must run entirely from outside — external, documentary, coalition-led — and above all protect everyone inside absolutely, never exposing anyone to the regime. And the honest prize is the financiers held to their safeguards, the downstream states and heritage bodies engaged, the resettlement's harms documented and pressed — from outside, carried by international coalitions, and protecting everyone inside. Above all, protect the people.

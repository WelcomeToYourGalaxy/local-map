# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN CÔTE D'IVOIRE

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In Côte d'Ivoire the great destructive force is not one project but a commodity, and the lever that can reach it runs through the world's chocolate. The country is by far the world's largest producer of **cocoa**, and cocoa is the leading cause of its deforestation: it has lost the great majority of its natural forests in half a century, more than two million hectares cleared for cocoa in just the last two decades — endangered trees, elephants, and chimpanzees vanishing with the forest, and cocoa planted illegally deep inside the classified forests and national parks that were meant to be protected. At current rates, the forest cover could be all but entirely gone within a decade. Much of this destruction is driven by poor smallholder and migrant farmers seeking fertile ground; the state's response has sometimes meant evicting thousands of them, raising real human-rights concerns of its own; and the great chocolate companies' promises have too often gone unkept. A harm that flows out through the world's chocolate can be pressed back through it.

Because the destruction flows out through a commodity sold to a watching world, the levers that reach it run through the supply chain, and they are real. The **traders and chocolate companies** that buy Ivorian cocoa have made **zero-deforestation commitments** they can be held to. **New import rules barring deforestation-linked cocoa from wealthy markets** are a powerful, growing lever on the whole chain. **Satellite monitoring, supply-chain mapping, and certification** can expose deforestation and trace it to its buyers; protest, petition, and lawful advocacy have their place. And the **forest law, the classified forests, and the national parks** carry legal protection the invasion breaches. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Much of the deforestation is done by poor farmers who clear forest because virgin soil grows the best cocoa and low prices push them to expand — so the forest cannot be saved by punishing the poor, and the state's enforcement, which has sometimes meant evicting thousands from protected forests, raises real human-rights concerns of its own, in a country still carrying the scars of civil conflict and sensitive disputes over land and belonging. The chocolate companies' promises have too often gone unkept. So the honest path is to defend the forest without abandoning the farmers — pairing the supply-chain and forest-law levers with deforestation-free livelihoods, and holding both the forest and the people who live from it. And though the cocoa frontier defines the fight, a community facing any other project — a mine, a dam, a port — has the ordinary domestic tools too: the environmental assessment, the courts, and the democratic and community channels. Aim for what these levers can genuinely deliver — deforestation exposed and traced, buyers held to their commitments and the import rules, the frontier slowed, the forest and the farmers both defended — not a single triumphant stop.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot save the forest by punishing the poor farmers who clear it, and there is no single project to stop. What you can do is press the harm back through the supply chain that carries it to the world: hold the traders and chocolate companies to their zero-deforestation commitments and the new import rules, expose and trace the clearing through satellite monitoring and certification, invoke the forest law and the parks — and pair it all with deforestation-free livelihoods, defending both the forest and the farmers who live from it.

## Be Honest About Which Fight You're In

Côte d'Ivoire is unusual: the harm is a commodity, the fight is a supply-chain and justice fight, and the obstacle is bound up with poverty, not simple villainy. That is the first honest truth — much of the clearing is done by poor farmers, and enforcement that only evicts them raises human-rights concerns and cannot save the forest. So aim at the winnable — deforestation exposed and traced, buyers and import rules pressed, the frontier slowed — while pairing every lever with alternative livelihoods, so the forest is defended without abandoning the people who live from it.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

The harm runs through a supply chain that reaches the world, so the decisions that matter are spread across it. The **traders and chocolate companies** who buy the cocoa answer to their own zero-deforestation commitments and to the import rules. The **wealthy-market regulators** who bar deforestation-linked cocoa hold a growing lever. The **certifiers and monitoring bodies** trace the deforestation. The **Ivorian forest authorities, the classified forests, and the parks** hold the domestic law. And the **farmers and forest communities** are both the drivers and the key to the solution. Map where in the chain the leverage lies before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

Deforestation moves as the cocoa frontier advances into forest, and the cocoa then flows through cooperatives, traders, and companies to the world's markets. You enter where the chain can be gripped: the buyers' commitments, the import rules at the wealthy markets, the monitoring that traces the clearing, the forest law at home. And you enter, above all, at the livelihood — the point where alternative income can stop a farmer from clearing the next hectare.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the cocoa: which cooperatives, traders, and companies buy from the deforestation frontier, and what commitments and import rules bind them. Follow the forest: which classified forests and parks the cocoa invades, and what the law protects. Follow the livelihood: what pushes the farmers to clear, and what alternatives could hold them. The leverage lies in the buyers' commitments, the import rules, the monitoring, the forest law, and the livelihoods.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

The watchers here are largely in the supply chain: the wealthy-market regulators enforcing the import rules, the certifiers and satellite-monitoring bodies, the traders and companies bound by their commitments, and the Ivorian forest authorities. These are the institutions a case is built to engage — holding each to its commitments, its rules, and the forest law.

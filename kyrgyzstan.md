# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN KYRGYZSTAN

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In early 2019, when word spread that a company had begun exploring for uranium at **Kyzyl-Ompol**, near the cherished shores of **Lake Issyk-Kul**, the people of Ton district did not wait. They protested, and the protests spread — to the lakeside town of Balykchy, and then to the capital, Bishkek, in front of parliament itself. Activists launched a petition and gathered nearly thirty thousand signatures in days. Within weeks, the prime minister ordered the work suspended, a deputy prime minister stood before the protesters and declared the project shut, and **parliament voted to ban uranium and thorium mining across the whole country**. The company withdrew. A people who remembered what uranium had done to their land under the Soviets — the country still lives with the wreckage of Soviet uranium mining, dozens of abandoned tailings dumps and radioactive burial sites, many around Mailuu-Suu, that still seep into soil and water — and who would not gamble their sacred lake, had stopped a mine and outlawed a whole industry in a single season. From the first protests to the national ban was a matter of a few short weeks — the signature of how a Kyrgyz fight is won.

A community in Kyrgyzstan facing a harmful project works with real tools, and — as Kyzyl-Ompol showed — a government that fears the street can be moved fast. Major projects need an **environmental impact assessment and licensing**, a real gate a defective study can be challenged at. As a party to the **Aarhus Convention**, Kyrgyzstan owes the public **rights to environmental information, participation, and justice**. **Parliament and the legislative process** can suspend, cancel, or even ban — as they did with uranium — and protest, petition, and litigation are lawful and have won here. The **courts** can enforce these where they function, and **Lake Issyk-Kul's Biosphere Reserve** and other protected designations shield valued places. And the most powerful argument of all is the **living memory of Soviet uranium poisoning and the fierce national attachment to the sacred lake** — not ordinary environmental arguments but national reflexes felt across every region and generation, which a campaign that grounds itself in them can gather a whole nation around, fast. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Kyzyl-Ompol's deepest lesson is a hard one: a fast, protest-driven win can be fragile and even reversed, and a government that yields to the street on one issue will suppress or ignore a protest it judges it can — so the space is not fully open, and it has lately narrowed, with real risk to activists. The national reflexes work powerfully for uranium and the sacred lake, but less so for other harms. And the courts and institutions are not always reliable. So the work must be careful: where the reflexes and the mechanisms align it can move a government fast; where they do not, it needs the patient use of the assessment, the Aarhus rights, and the law. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a project assessed, suspended, cancelled, or even banned, grounded in the memory, the lake, and the law — while guarding the win against reversal and protecting everyone in a narrowing space.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

A Kyrgyz project runs through several powers, and — distinctively — the street. The **government and its ministries** grant the licences and can suspend a project. **Parliament** can cancel or ban, as it did with uranium. The **environmental and licensing authorities** administer the assessment. The **courts** review the legality where they function. And, decisively, a **roused public** can move all of them fast, because governments here fear the street. Map who decides — and whether the national reflexes are engaged — before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A mine or project needs an environmental assessment and a licence. That assessment and the Aarhus participation rights are one entry point; the licence another; parliament a third; and the street a fourth and, at Kyzyl-Ompol, decisive one. Where the harm touches the national reflexes — uranium, the sacred lake — a fight can rouse the nation and move the government in weeks. Where it does not, enter patiently through the assessment, the Aarhus rights, and the law.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the licence: who holds it, what the assessment says, and whether the Aarhus information and participation rights were honoured. The leverage lies in the assessment and Aarhus rights, the legislative power, the courts, and the national reflexes.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

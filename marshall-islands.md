# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the atolls, lagoons, and future of the Marshall Islands — but here the struggle is unlike almost any other, because the gravest harm was done long ago by a distant and powerful state, and its consequences are still unfolding, now colliding with a second existential threat. Between 1946 and 1958, the United States detonated sixty-seven nuclear bombs on the atolls of Bikini and Enewetak, including the largest weapon it ever tested, and in doing so displaced whole communities, irradiated others, and rendered ancestral homelands unsafe. Decades later the harm endures: contaminated islands to which people cannot return, health effects still unaddressed, promised compensation still unpaid, and, on Enewetak, a cracking concrete dome the Marshallese call the Tomb, holding a hundred thousand cubic yards of radioactive waste and now leaking into the lagoon — a dome that rising seas threaten to breach entirely. But the homelands and health and future of the Marshallese are worth every effort, the levers that run through justice, the compact, the dome, the double injustice, and the world's conscience are real, and the dignified, persistent work of documenting the injustice, demanding the accountability, and carrying the cause to the world is one the Marshallese have already made their own — and, standing small but unbowed before the great powers of the age, pursue justice for a people wronged twice.

## The Strategic Framework

Effective efforts follow the same underlying pattern, whether the goal is to secure coastal protection, curb phosphate pollution, or defend land:

```
STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION  (find the harm, the cause, and the deciders)
        |
STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION          (build the evidence -- harm and cause)
        |
STEP 3: LOCAL OPPOSITION        (build the community and the coalition, carefully)
        |
STEP 4: LEGAL, REGULATORY & REGIONAL  (courts, ECOWAS Court, programmes)
        |
STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY         (make it a national and regional cause)
```

These steps operate **simultaneously, not in sequence**. The evidence of harm and its cause feeds the courts, the regional court, the international programmes, and the media at once; the community organises, with care; a court case, a regional-court petition, or an approach to a coastal programme is both pressure and a spur to the next. The community that runs all five together, each reinforcing the others, gives itself the best — if still constrained — chance of defending its coast, land, and health.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective effort begins by finding the exact harm and its cause — and the leverage the community holds across its several arenas. Vague concern goes nowhere, and dangerous confrontation invites repression; an effort aimed at "the erosion devouring this village, caused by the port and the upstream dam, which the coastal programmes could fund protection against, the regional court could address as a rights matter, and the community could help adapt to" is a real, workable effort — the kind Togo's arenas can deliver. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What would be lost?** This is the first question here. What **coast, land, health, or livelihood** would the harm destroy — the village to the sea, the shore to phosphate, the beach to sand mining.

**2. What causes it — at home or across the border?** What **cause** drives the harm — the phosphate plant, the port, the upstream dam, the sand mining — since the cause, often transboundary, determines the remedy.

**3. Which coastal programmes and funding apply?** Which **international, regional, and national coastal and climate programmes** could fund protection and adaptation, the most reliable lever here.

**4. Which courts and the regional court apply?** How the **domestic courts** and the **ECOWAS Court of Justice** can address the harm or the rights it violates.

**5. How can the community adapt and act safely?** How the **community can adapt** — mangroves, shore protection, livelihoods — and organise **safely**, given the risk of repression.

## A Worked Example

Suppose the sea is devouring a fishing village, its cause the upstream dam and the port, worsened by sand mining, and the government slow to act.

Working the five questions: **what would be lost**? **What causes it**? **Which coastal programmes apply**? **Which courts and the regional court apply**? And how can the community adapt and act safely? The deciders and causes are the government, the state company, the port, the upstream works, and the transboundary process, with the coastal programmes, the courts, the regional court, and community adaptation as the levers.

Within a season the effort has moved from "the sea takes our homes and nothing can be done" to a real effort: document the harm and its cause; seek coastal-programme funding for protection; build community adaptation; use the courts and the regional court for rights and remedies; and address the sand mining with livelihoods. That is a workable effort suited to Togo — one that meets a transboundary slow disaster across the arenas open to the community. Notice what the five questions accomplish, and how they suit a harm of this unusual character: they separate the harm from its cause, and the cause from its remedy, so that a community facing something as overwhelming as a coast disappearing into the sea can break it into parts it can actually act on. The harm becomes documentable loss; the cause becomes a specific, traceable process, at home or across the border; the remedy becomes a fundable protection or adaptation; the arenas become specific funders, courts, and programmes to approach; and the safety question keeps the whole effort from tipping into the confrontation that would invite repression. That translation — from an overwhelming, seemingly unstoppable disaster into a set of specific, answerable, and largely safe tasks, each matched to an arena that can address it — is the first and most important thing a Togolese effort does, because it converts despair before a vanishing coast into a workable programme of documentation, funding, litigation, and restoration.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on — and here, where the case must persuade coastal programmes, courts, the regional court, and the public, sound evidence of both the harm and its cause is decisive. A programme funder, a judge, a regional-court panel, and a journalist all move on evidence — and in Togo, a clear record of the loss, its often transboundary cause, and the remedy that could meet it is precisely what an effort turns into a real result, so build it well. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Harm, the Cause, and the Deciders

Get the facts the case turns on. Identify the **harm, its location, and its cause** — the erosion and the works that drive it, the phosphate plant and its discharge, the land taking — and, crucially here, whether the cause is **domestic or transboundary**, since that determines the remedy and the arena. Establish the deciders and the cause precisely, because in Togo the remedy for a transboundary, infrastructural harm is different from the remedy for a local project, and knowing the cause is knowing where to press.

## Layer 2: The Loss — to Coast, Health, and Livelihood

Document what would be lost. Establish the **environmental and physical harm** — the shoreline lost, the homes and villages taken by the sea, the phosphate pollution of water and health, the beaches stripped by sand mining. And establish the **human harm** — the displaced families, the lost fisheries and farmland, the ruined livelihoods. Draw on **the coastal and scientific studies, the international programmes' assessments, and local knowledge**, much of which exists for a coast as studied as Togo's. Photographs, coastal surveys, and testimony make the loss undeniable — and fundable.

## Layer 3: The Loss, the Cause, the Remedy, and the Arena

Finally, document the case. Does the **environmental and human harm** show a real loss? What **cause**, domestic or transboundary, drives it? What **remedy** — coastal protection, pollution control, adaptation, livelihoods — could meet it, and which **arena** — the coastal programmes, the courts, the regional court, community adaptation — is open? And, where relevant, **is poverty a driver**, requiring livelihoods as part of the answer? Each is a separate element, and together they make a case that can be pressed across the arenas open to a Togolese community. This is the layer that most often decides these efforts, because a documented loss, an identified cause, a fundable remedy, and an open arena are exactly what let a Togolese community defend its coast and health. Build it well, and the coastal programmes, the courts, the regional court, and community adaptation become the community's genuine strength. One practical note on building this layer: identifying the cause precisely is more important in Togo than in almost any other country in this series, because the cause here so often determines the arena. A harm caused by a purely domestic project — a discharge from the phosphate plant, say — points toward the domestic courts and the state's own regulators. A harm caused by works across the border — the dam upstream in Ghana that starves the coast of sediment — points instead toward regional cooperation and the transboundary frameworks, because no Togolese court can order a change to a Ghanaian dam. And a harm that is really a failure of the state to protect its own people — the government's inaction as the sea takes village after village — may point toward the regional court as a rights matter, or toward the international programmes for the protection the state has failed to provide. So the file must trace the causal chain carefully, because a community that misdiagnoses the cause will press the wrong arena and waste its effort, while one that identifies the cause precisely knows exactly which of its several arenas can actually deliver a remedy.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

Evidence in hand beats evidence you assume exists. Here is where the material tends to live in a Togolese effort, and how to get it — with the **harm**, the **cause**, the **remedy**, and the **arenas** at the center, and with care for safety where confrontation is risky. Treat this as a checklist to work through, not a menu to admire.

## The Harm and Cause Record

Start with the harm itself. **Coastal surveys, erosion studies, pollution measurements, and displacement records** establish what is being lost and how fast; much of this exists in the international programmes' assessments and the scientific literature on Togo's coast, and is reachable. Establish the **cause** — the upstream dam, the ports, the phosphate discharge, the sand mining — since the cause, often transboundary, determines the arena. Establish exactly **what is lost, how fast, and what causes it**, because the cause is the key to the remedy.

## The Coastal, Environmental, and Health Evidence

This is the heart of the file. Gather the **environmental evidence** — the rate of erosion, the villages and coast lost, the phosphate pollution of sea and shore, the sand mining's damage — drawing on **coastal and oceanographic studies, the international programmes' data, and pollution measurements**. And gather the **health and livelihood evidence** — the illnesses linked to the phosphate discharge, the fisheries and farmland lost, the displaced families. Photographs, surveys, and testimony make the harm vivid and credible. On a coast so studied and so visibly changing, this evidence is powerful and often already gathered.

## The Remedy and Programme Record

Document the remedy and the funding, because these are the distinctive Togolese levers. Establish what **coastal protection** — breakwaters, groynes, nourishment, mangrove restoration — could defend the shore; what **pollution control** could curb the phosphate discharge; what **adaptation and livelihoods** could rebuild. And identify the **coastal and climate programmes** — the World Bank's regional coastal programme, the UN and regional climate funds, the national coastal plans — that could fund them, since funded remedy is the most reliable path here. A community that can show a fundable remedy to a documented harm holds the strongest card in Togo.

## The Legal and Regional Record

Document the legal and regional levers. Gather the **domestic environmental and land law** and how the harm may breach it; the basis for a **regional court petition** where rights — to health, to home, to a healthy environment — are violated, noting that the ECOWAS Court has ruled against Togo before; and the **transboundary and regional frameworks** relevant to a cross-border harm like the sediment-trapping dam. A community that can frame its harm as a rights violation for the regional court, or a transboundary matter for regional cooperation, holds levers beyond the government's control.

## The People and the Community

Finally, map the human terrain, with care for safety. Who is affected — the coastal villages, the fishers, the farmers, those poisoned by the phosphate? Who are the allies — the **domestic civil society and environmental groups, the international programmes, the regional bodies, the sympathetic experts, the diaspora**? And how can the community **organise and adapt safely**, given the risk of repression? A Togolese effort is advanced through the community and its allies, using the several arenas with care — so map that coalition and those arenas deliberately, and mind the safety of those who organise.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION

Evidence does not move deciders by itself. A community — organised, informed, and adapting — is what turns evidence into action, across the several arenas open to a Togolese community, and with care where confrontation is dangerous. And here, distinctively, the community's own power to adapt is itself a lever: restoring mangroves, protecting the shore, and building livelihoods are not merely coping but a genuine, fundable defence of the coast.

## Why the Community Is Decisive

In Togo, an organised community is decisive across every arena — but it must organise with care, because open confrontation with the state invites repression. The community supplies what an effort runs on: the affected voices, the local knowledge, the legitimacy, and — distinctively here — the hands that do the adapting, restoring the mangroves and protecting the shore. When a community organises to document, to seek funding, to litigate, and to adapt, it becomes a genuine force across the arenas open to it. **A united community here is not merely the backdrop; it is the documenter, the litigant, the fundable partner, and the adapter — the engine of a Togolese effort, provided it acts with due care.** This last role — the adapter — is genuinely distinctive to the Togolese situation and worth dwelling on, because it changes what "opposition" even means here. In many countries a community's task is to stop a project by pressing the deciders; in Togo, where the greatest harm is a slow, partly transboundary disaster that no local decider fully controls, a community's most powerful action is often not to stop something but to defend and rebuild directly — planting the mangroves that hold the shore, restoring the wetlands that buffer the sea, building the sea defences and the livelihoods that let people stay. This adaptive work is not a consolation prize for failing to stop the harm; it is, for a coast being eaten by the sea, frequently the most effective defence available, and it has the great virtue of being both fundable by the international programmes and far safer than confronting an entrenched state. A community that understands this reorients its energy toward the adaptation it can actually accomplish, and finds in that work both real protection and a form of opposition the state has no reason to repress.

## Finding the Affected Community

Begin with those the harm touches most — the **coastal villagers losing homes to the sea, the fishers, the farmers, those whose health the phosphate harms** — whose stake is direct and grave. Widen to all who depend on the coast and its fisheries, which in a coastal nation is many. Name the shared stake — **our coast, our homes, our fisheries, our health, our children's ground** — because in a country where villages are vanishing into the sea, the defence of the coast is a matter of survival that unites. # STEP 4: LEGAL, REGULATORY & REGIONAL CHALLENGES — THE COURTS, THE ECOWAS COURT OF JUSTICE, AND THE REGIONAL PROGRAMMES

This is where a Togolese effort is realistically advanced — not through dangerous confrontation, but through the **domestic courts, the regional ECOWAS Court of Justice, and the regional and international programmes** that a semi-authoritarian but not fully closed state answers to. Enter this front clear-eyed and careful: the courts, the regional court, and the programmes are Togo's distinctive levers, strongest when the **harm is provable and a remedy is fundable** — but the state is repressive, so favour the legal, regional, and funded over the confrontational.

## The Domestic Space and Courts

The first front is the constrained domestic space. Togo's **civil society, press, and courts**, though limited by a semi-authoritarian state, are not wholly closed, and offer genuine arenas. ## The ECOWAS Court of Justice

The second and distinctive front is the regional court. The **ECOWAS Court of Justice** can be petitioned directly by citizens and NGOs over violations of their rights — including the rights to health, to home, and to a healthy environment — and, crucially, it lies beyond the Togolese government's control, and has ruled against Togo before. Where a harm — the phosphate poisoning, the failure to protect a vanishing coast, a repression of those who protest — amounts to a rights violation, the regional court is a genuine and distinctive arena, reachable directly and answerable to no domestic power. Frame the harm as a rights violation, gather the evidence, and, with legal help, petition the regional court — a lever few of the harder countries in this series possess.

## The Regional and International Programmes

The third front is the programmes and the transboundary cause. The **World Bank's regional coastal programme, the UN and regional climate funds, and the national coastal plans** can fund the protection and adaptation the coast needs, and are the most reliable path to real defence. And because the erosion is partly **transboundary** — driven by the dam upstream and the regional ports — the **regional bodies and neighbouring states** are an arena for cooperation on the shared cause. Engage the programmes for funding and the regional bodies for the cross-border cause, because a harm that crosses borders is met by remedies that cross borders too. The transboundary character of Togo's coastal erosion is worth understanding fully, because it is both the harm's deepest cause and, counterintuitively, an opening. The coast is starved of sand partly because a great dam upstream in a neighbouring country holds back the sediment the shore once received, and partly because the region's ports interrupt the natural drift of sand along the coast; the same forces erode the shores of Togo's neighbours too. This means no Togolese community, and indeed no Togolese government, can solve the problem alone — but it also means the problem is a shared regional one, in which neighbouring states and communities have a common interest and regional bodies have a genuine role. Framing the erosion as a regional crisis requiring regional cooperation on sediment management and coastal protection, rather than as a purely national failure, both diagnoses it truthfully and opens the door to the regional partnerships and the international climate finance that a shared, cross-border crisis can attract. The transboundary cause, honestly named, is also a transboundary opportunity. Pursued together — the domestic space, the regional court, the programmes, and regional cooperation — these fronts are how a Togolese community defends its coast and health within a constrained but not closed state. It is worth understanding why these fronts complement one another so well in Togo's particular situation. The domestic courts and civil society supply the arena closest to home, useful for the harms the domestic law can reach, but constrained by a semi-authoritarian state that limits how far they can go. The regional court supplies exactly what the domestic space lacks — independence from the government — and so is the arena of choice for the rights violations a captured domestic system will not remedy. And the international programmes supply what neither court can: the money and the engineering to actually protect and rebuild a coast, which is, in the end, what a community losing its shore most needs. Each covers the others' weaknesses: the domestic space where the law can act, the regional court where the government cannot be trusted, the programmes where funding and physical protection are required. A community that grasps this division of labour — litigating at home where it can, at the regional court where it must, and building funded protection throughout — uses each arena for what it does best, and so makes the most of a constrained but genuinely multi-arena situation.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

Evidence does not speak for itself; you must aim it at each decider in the language that moves them. The same file of harm, cause, and remedy becomes different arguments for a coastal programme, a court, the regional court, and the public. Translate deliberately, and with care.

## For the Coastal Programmes and Funders

The programmes respond to **a fundable, well-documented need and a workable remedy**. Frame the harm as a clear case for coastal protection or adaptation: here is the erosion and its cause, here is the protection or restoration that would meet it, here is the community ready to partner. Give them the evidence, the remedy, and the community's readiness, so funding a defence of the coast becomes an obvious, deliverable project. This is the argument that most reliably brings real protection here. It is worth emphasising that the coastal programmes are, of all the levers in this guide, the one most likely to deliver tangible, physical protection to a Togolese community, and so deserve particular attention. Courts and regional petitions can vindicate rights and establish duties, and media can draw attention, but none of them can place a rock revetment along an eroding shore, restore a belt of mangroves, or nourish a beach with sand — and those physical works are, for a coast being eaten by the sea, what actually saves homes and livelihoods. The international and regional coastal and climate programmes exist precisely to fund such works, and they operate on a logic — of documented vulnerability, workable projects, and community partnership — that a well-prepared community can genuinely satisfy. So while every arena matters, an effort that treats the coastal programmes as its most reliable path to real protection, and invests heavily in building the documented, fundable, community-backed case those programmes require, is an effort aimed squarely at the outcome that most matters: a coast actually defended.

## For the Domestic Courts

Domestic courts respond to **the law**, within the limits of a semi-authoritarian state. Frame the harm as a breach of environmental or land law, or a failure of a legal duty to protect, and seek the remedy the law allows. Give the court, through a lawyer, a clean legal case. Be realistic about the constraints, but use the domestic courts where they can genuinely act, favouring the legal over the confrontational.

## For the ECOWAS Court of Justice

The regional court responds to **rights violations, and lies beyond the government's reach**. Frame the harm as a violation of rights — to health, to home, to a healthy environment — or the repression of those who defend the coast as a violation of civil rights, and, with legal help, petition the court directly. Give it the documented violation and the remedy sought. The regional court's independence from the domestic power is its great value; use it for what the domestic system cannot deliver. It is worth being clear about both the reach and the limits of this regional court, honestly. Its great value is its independence: because it sits above the national government and can be petitioned directly by citizens and organisations, it offers a forum that a captured or compromised domestic judiciary cannot, and its rulings carry real weight and international attention, as its past judgments against Togo have shown. Its limits are equally real: it is concerned with rights rather than with engineering or funding, so it can vindicate a violation and order a remedy but cannot itself build a sea wall or fund a mangrove restoration; and its processes are slow, and a determined government may drag its feet on compliance. Understanding both means using the regional court for what it does best — establishing that a harm or a repression is a rights violation the government must answer for — while relying on the international programmes and community adaptation for the physical protection that no court can supply.

## For the Public, the Region, and the World

The public and the region respond to **a vivid, human, and shared story**; the world to **a striking case of climate and infrastructural injustice**. Translate the file into a story anyone can grasp: villages vanishing into the sea, a coast poisoned by phosphate, a disaster driven partly by works across the border — and a community fighting to defend and rebuild its shore. Frame it as a shared West African coastal crisis and a matter of justice. Keep it accurate; credibility is quickly tested. And mind the safety of those who speak.

## The Golden Rule

Never fabricate, never exaggerate, and mind the safety of those who act. Your credibility is your greatest asset, and in a constrained state it is quickly tested. State the harm precisely, identify the cause honestly, and offer a fundable remedy. Favour the legal, regional, and funded arenas over dangerous confrontation, and protect those who organise.

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

For more than a decade, the farmers of **Cabañas**, the Catholic Church, water defenders, scientists, and community groups fought a foreign gold mine — the **El Dorado project of Pacific Rim, later the Australian-Canadian giant OceanaGold** — that threatened the rivers a water-scarce nation depends on. They paid a terrible price: four anti-mining activists were murdered, among them **Marcelo Rivera** and **Dora Alicia Recinos Sorto**, who was eight months pregnant. The company retaliated another way too, suing the country for hundreds of millions of dollars before a **World Bank investor-state tribunal (ICSID)**. And still the movement won: in **2016 the tribunal ruled for El Salvador** and ordered the company to pay millions in costs — a rare defeat for a mining giant. Then, in **March 2017, El Salvador became the first country on Earth to ban all metal mining**, the Legislative Assembly passing it unanimously, lawmakers holding signs that read "No to Mining, Yes to Life." A tiny, poor country had beaten a multinational and outlawed an entire industry to protect its water, because ordinary people organised, endured, and refused to give up.

The farmers of Cabañas, the Catholic Church, and the water defenders beat a foreign gold mine — and, in 2017, won the world's first national ban on metal mining — with tools that remain real in El Salvador, though narrower now. The **water argument** unites a water-scarce nation like no other. The **Catholic Church** carries deep moral authority. **National legislation** was the winning lever in 2017, and legislation can be fought for again when the ground shifts. The **courts** function, if imperfectly. And the **investor-state arbitration** system, though a threat companies wield, was beaten at ICSID in 2016 when the tribunal ruled for El Salvador. But these tools have grave limits now, and this guide states the hardest lesson plainly and up front: in **December 2024 the Legislative Assembly repealed the ban**, and metal mining was authorised again, reserved to the state under President Bukele's government. A historic, world-first victory was reversed seven years later — not a reason for despair, since the 2017 win proves what this movement can do, but a warning that even a landmark legislative victory can be undone, and that today's levers are narrower, in a political environment far more closed. Above all, keep your people safe, because activists here — among them Marcelo Rivera and Dora Alicia Recinos Sorto, eight months pregnant — have been murdered. So aim for what these levers can still deliver, sustaining the fight through a closed period until the ground shifts again.

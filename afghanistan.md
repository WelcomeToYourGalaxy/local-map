# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Concern for the environment in Afghanistan must be pursued almost entirely from outside — through the international, the regional, the diaspora, and the technical — because this is one of the most closed and repressive states in the world, where independent civil society has been crushed, much of the population is excluded from public life, and domestic environmental advocacy is all but impossible. Afghanistan is a landlocked, mountainous, drought-stricken country at the heart of a great river systemAfghanistan is a landlocked, mountainous, drought-stricken country at the heart of the Amu Darya river system of Central Asia, and its most consequential environmental question is a vast new development project: the Qosh Tepa Canal, dug at speed across the arid north to divert the waters of the Amu Darya. That project — and the transboundary river it draws from, shared with the downstream states of Central Asia and the dying Aral Sea — is the distinctive ground of environmental effort here, because it is a matter not only of Afghanistan but of a whole region, reachable through the international and regional water frameworks even when the domestic space is closed. Afghanistan is a landlocked land of high mountains and dry plains at the head of a great shared river, drought-stricken and in humanitarian crisis, its people among the poorest and least free in the world, its rulers cutting a vast canal across the northern desert to draw the waters of the Amu Darya — a project that is at once a genuine bid for food and water in a parched land and a wasteful, ungoverned diversion that imperils the downstream states and the dying Aral. This guide has hidden none of that, nor pretended that the canal can be stopped, or that Afghanistan should be denied its water, or that the domestic space is anything but closed, and there is no landmark victory to promise you. But the river is shared, and there the whole region and the world have reason to help govern it well; the canal is wastefully built, and better engineering would serve Afghanistan as much as its neighbours; and the drought can be eased and the land restored with the world's support. Those are the levers, and they are real: the shared river and the cooperation and fair agreement it calls for; the engineering and efficiency that would spare everyone's water; the international scrutiny of the minerals; and the downstream states, the frameworks, and the vast diaspora that reach a closed state from outside. There is no domestic challenge in a state where civil society has been crushed, and no halting of a flagship canal; this guide has said so throughout, and insisted above all on the absolute protection of anyone inside. But the shared river and the parched land of Afghanistan are worth every careful, cooperative, external effort, the levers that run through the region's shared interest and the case for efficiency are real, and the patient, technical, protective work of documenting the harm, pressing for cooperation, and building the efficient alternatives is one that the downstream states, the international community, and the vast diaspora can genuinely do — and, from outside and with care, help a shared river be governed well, for the north, the downstream, and the region alike. So can you.

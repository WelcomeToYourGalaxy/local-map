# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN SAMOA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community effort in Samoa once showed that the villages can defend their customary land — the heart of fa'a Samoa — even against a powerful international bank and their own government. In Samoa, most of the land is customary land, held not by individuals but by families, the **aiga**, under the authority of their chiefs, the **matai**, and protected by the Constitution from being sold away. In the early 2010s, the Asian Development Bank promoted reforms, under a programme to make "economic use" of customary land, that would have made it far easier to lease that land and to pledge those leases as security for bank loans — so that a single chief might lease family land without the family's consent, and a defaulted loan might hand large stretches of customary land to a bank for decades. A group of village chiefs saw where that road led: to the slow alienation of the land that is the foundation of Samoan life. They did not simply protest. With the help of an international advocacy organisation, they filed a complaint with the bank's own accountability mechanism, and they pressed for protection in law. In 2019, Samoa passed a law that shut off the main route to alienation and set new protections around leases of customary land. The land was defended.

A community in Samoa that would defend its land or environment works, as the chiefs did, through genuinely real tools, in an open island democracy. The distinctive lever is the **communal and constitutional protection of customary land**: held by the aiga for all its generations and shielded by the Constitution from sale, it has no single owner who can give it away — a powerful structural foundation, as it was against the reforms. Bound to this is the **authority of the matai and the aiga**, the chiefs and families who govern the land under fa'a Samoa. Where a project is backed by an international bank, the **accountability mechanisms of the financiers** — the bank's own complaint and compliance bodies — are a real lever, as the chiefs proved against the Asian Development Bank. And the **protection of the law and parliament** can be pressed to write real safeguards, as the 2019 law did; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. Ordinary harmful projects, too, must reckon with **environmental impact assessment and the planning regulation**, and the **open democratic and community channels** — the elected parliament, the village councils, the courts — genuinely work. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The customary-land victory was a specific defence of communal land against alienation; the distinctive lever is that constitutional and communal shield, and an ordinary hotel, road, or quarry without that dimension relies on the weaker ordinary tools of assessment, planning, and democratic pressure. The financier-accountability lever works only where a project is backed by an accountable international bank. The nation is small, poor, aid-dependent, and climate-threatened, so the development pressure is real. And the 2019 law did not answer every danger. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — customary land defended through its constitutional shield and the matai's authority; an internationally financed project held to its financiers' accountability; a harmful project assessed or refused — through the constitutional protection, the financier accountability, the law, the assessment, and the open democratic channels.

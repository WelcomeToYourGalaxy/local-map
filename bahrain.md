# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN BAHRAIN

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Bahrain is a small, crowded, water-scarce island kingdom that is literally building itself out into the sea. Through decades of relentless **dredging and land reclamation**, it has enlarged its territory by roughly a fifth — building housing, industry, and lavish artificial islands out over the shallow coastal waters, and in doing so burying the seagrass meadows, coral, and mangroves that sustained its fisheries, losing the great majority of its natural mangroves, pushing its fishermen ever further and more dangerously out to sea, and walling off much of the coast from the people who once fished and gathered along it. Its very name means "two seas," for the fresh springs that once welled up amid the salt; but those springs are largely gone, the aquifer drawn down and turning salty, and the water now comes almost entirely from energy-intensive desalination whose brine flows back into a sea already under strain. The political space is narrow. But the sea, the fisheries, and the water on which the nation itself depends are a genuine and defensible stake.

A community in Bahrain facing a reclamation or dredging that buries the sea works with real, if constrained, tools — kept strictly to the environmental. A major project is supposed to undergo an **environmental impact assessment**, administered by the environment authority: a gate that can be pressed where it is skipped, rushed, or defective. The **marine-science case** — the documented evidence that reclamation buries the seagrass, coral, and mangroves that sustain the fisheries — is a powerful, technical argument. The **water-security argument** carries unusual weight here, because the shallow sea the reclamation harms is bound up with the desalination and the aquifer the nation itself depends on, so the harm reaches the state's own core interest. The **sea-heritage argument** — the coast, the springs, and the fishing that are Bahrain's very identity — adds moral force. And the **international marine and heritage frameworks** reach an image-conscious state that answers to the world. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Bahrain genuinely lacks land and water, so reclamation and desalination spring from real need, not mere greed, and the honest aim is to restrain, assess, and protect — not to forbid all development. The state and the development interests behind the reclamation are powerful, and the coast is lucrative. And the political space for open opposition is narrow, in a kingdom where coastal grievances have at times touched the political — so the work must run through the technical, inside the system, and international channels, strictly environmental, with care and discretion, protecting everyone involved. So the realistic aim is a project properly assessed, restrained, or conditioned, the marine harm reduced, and the water and fisheries defended — not an open defeat of a favoured reclamation.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot forbid development in a nation genuinely short of land and water, and you cannot safely mount open political confrontation in a narrow space. What you can do is keep the fight strictly environmental and technical: force the reclamation through a real assessment, ground the marine-science and water-security case, invoke the international frameworks an image-conscious state answers to, and press through inside the system and international channels — with the greatest care, and protecting everyone involved.

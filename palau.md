# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN PALAU

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the land and reefs of Palau, and one state's people showed that a community armed with good science can persuade its own leaders to ban a destructive practice outright. When scientists demonstrated that clearing the land — and especially the mangroves along the shore — was sending sediment onto the reefs and harming the fish that feed Palauan families, the people of one state did something practical and effective: they took that evidence to their own state government and lobbied until the clearing of mangroves was banned. And that was not a lone act. Across the largest island, community after community, working together through an alliance and drawing on Palau's own tradition of the temporary moratorium the islands call "bul," established their own protected areas on the land, turning scientific understanding and cultural practice into lasting protection for the forests, the watersheds, and the reefs below them. The destructive clearing was stopped where the community made its case; the protected areas hold. It is worth setting down what those communities actually did, because it shows how ordinary people turn understanding into protection. The harm was not obvious at first: clearing land and cutting mangroves seemed a local matter, until reef scientists showed that the sediment washing off the cleared ground was settling on the reefs offshore, smothering the corals and thinning the fish that Palauan families rely on for food. Once that link between the land and the sea was shown clearly, the community had something a mere complaint never has — evidence a leader could act on. They took it to the people closest to them with the power to respond, their own state government and their traditional chiefs, rather than to a distant national office or a foreign company, and they asked for a concrete remedy: ban the clearing, protect the watershed. And because the request was grounded in solid science, rooted in the community, and carried through the authorities the community itself respects, it was granted.

The Palauan story teaches two things about stopping destructive projects here — including its honest limits, which this guide states plainly and up front. The first is the pattern that runs through every guide: not a single silver bullet, but the combination of every lever — the affected community, the documentation, the coalition, and the pressure on the deciders — pressed together. The second is distinctly Palauan, and it protected the land and the reefs. **A community can take solid scientific evidence of harm — how a clearing or a development damages the reefs, fish, and water it depends on — to its own state and traditional leaders, and, working through them and through Palau's tradition of community-owned protection and the moratorium of bul, ban the destructive practice and place the land under lasting protection.** That power — the science, the community, the state and traditional leaders, and the tradition of local protection — is Palau's distinctive lever, and this guide is built around it. What makes this lever unusual, and worth understanding precisely, is where it applies pressure. It does not try to out-fight a developer in a national court or out-spend a foreign investor; it works through the authorities closest to the community — the state and the chiefs — who are both reachable and genuinely powerful over local land, and it arms the community’s appeal with something those authorities can act on: solid science and the legitimacy of custom. A distant national government may be hard for a small community to move, and a foreign company harder still; but a state government and a council of chiefs are neighbours, answerable to the community, and receptive to evidence that the reefs and fisheries their own people depend on are being harmed. The community’s task is therefore to bring the science and the custom to the leaders who are both close and capable, and ask for a concrete, lasting protection — which is exactly what the Airai and Babeldaob communities did.

But the same story carries honest warnings, and this guide will not hide them; it places them first. The community-driven wins here are **local and modest** — a state ban on clearing, community protected areas — not the halting of a huge national project, and a community should not expect one campaign to stop everything. Palau's **largest** conservation victories — the vast marine sanctuary, the shark sanctuary — were **led by national leaders and law**, not by grassroots opposition, so a community here depends heavily on **supportive leaders and the authority of the traditional chiefs**, which is a real strength but also a real limit. And this is a **small nation** facing large forces — foreign investment, a flood of tourism, a changing climate — against which a single community's leverage is bounded, and many wins are partial. This guide is written with both truths in view: the real, distinctive power of science-backed community advocacy through local and traditional authority — and the modest scale, the reliance on supportive leaders, and the hard limits of a small nation that qualify it. These qualifications are not footnotes; they are part of an honest picture, and a community should weigh them before it begins. The modest scale matters: banning a clearing and protecting a watershed are real, lasting gains, but they are not the same as halting a national mega-project, and a community that expects one campaign to stop everything will be discouraged. The reliance on supportive leaders matters too: Palau’s greatest conservation achievements came from national leaders and law, and even the local wins depended on states and chiefs willing to act, so a community whose leaders are unwilling faces a harder road and must patiently win them over. And the limits of a small nation are real: against foreign investment, a flood of tourism, and a changing climate, a single community’s leverage is bounded, and many gains are partial. An honest campaign here aims to protect what it can, and counts a watershed and a reef defended as a real victory even when the larger forces cannot all be turned. If your fight, too, is to protect the land and reefs you depend on, and you can bring evidence to leaders who may listen, this is the guide for it.

Most communities do not know HOW to make that power effective. They watch the land cleared and the reef decline and assume "the developer has the permits and the leaders have decided and nothing can be done," and never gather the evidence, never take it to their state and chiefs, never establish the protection. They generate private worry without organised advocacy. The gap between a clearing stopped and one allowed is rarely apathy — Palauans proved a community will act to protect its reefs. It is method: knowing how to marshal the science, work through the state and the traditional leaders, and secure lasting protection.

The Airai and Babeldaob communities did exactly this — they marshalled the science, worked through their states and chiefs, and established lasting protection.

## The Levers That Are Distinctly Strong Here

Every country's system has its own pressure points. Palau's give communities a distinctive set:

- **The science.** Solid **scientific evidence of harm** — how a clearing or development damages the reefs, fish, and water the community depends on — is powerful, as the reef science was in Airai. This is the lever that most distinguishes a Palauan fight of this kind, and it rewards close attention. In a place where the sea feeds the people and the reefs are a source of national pride, evidence that a project harms the reefs and the fishery is not an abstraction — it speaks to food, livelihood, and identity, and it gives the state and the chiefs a clear, defensible reason to act. Solid science also does something a protest cannot: it converts a contested opinion into a demonstrated fact, one a leader can stand behind and a proponent finds hard to dismiss. The community that can show, with credible reef and watershed science, exactly how a clearing or development damages the sea it depends on has found the argument that moves Palau’s decision-makers most reliably, as the science of sediment and reefs moved Airai to act.
- **The state and traditional leaders.** Palau's **state governments and traditional chiefs** are close to the community and can ban a practice or protect an area, as Airai's state did.
- **The tradition of bul and local protection.** Palau's tradition of the **moratorium (bul)** and **community-owned protected areas** is a legitimate, culturally-grounded tool, as it grounded the protected areas.
- **The link between land and reef.** Showing that **protecting the land protects the reef and the fish** the community lives on is a uniquely Palauan and persuasive argument.
- **The national and international attention.** Palau's fame as a **conservation leader** draws attention and allies that reinforce a community's case.

## What This Guide Will Not Pretend

It would be dishonest to promise these levers deliver a grand or complete win. The community wins here are **modest and local**; the biggest victories are **leader-led**; and this is a **small nation** with limited leverage against large forces. This guide takes those limits seriously — it states them first — and shows you how to use the real, distinctive tools Palau offers — the science, the state and traditional leaders, the tradition of local protection, and the land-and-reef link — as Palauan communities used them to ban a destructive clearing and protect their watersheds.

## The Levels of Decision

A Palauan development runs through several tracks at once, and each is a place to intervene.

**The state government.** The **state** — Palau's sixteen states are close to their communities — permits much local land use and can ban a practice or protect an area, as Airai state banned mangrove clearing. It is worth grasping why the state, rather than the national government, is so often the better first target in a fight like this. Palau’s states are small, close to their communities, and hold real power over local land use, so a community’s appeal reaches decision-makers who are neighbours and who feel the community’s concerns directly — and who can act quickly, without the delay and distance of national politics. A state that is shown solid evidence of harm to its own people’s reefs and fisheries has both the power and the incentive to respond with a ban or a refusal. This is why so much of the distinctive Palauan strategy is organised around bringing the science to the state closest to the community, rather than only petitioning a distant national office.

**The traditional leaders.** The **traditional chiefs** hold real authority and custom, advise leaders, and can invoke the moratorium of **bul**, as the tradition grounds Palau's protections.

**The national government and law.** The **national government** and the **Protected Areas Network** provide the framework that recognises and supports local protection, as the network supports community-owned areas.

**The community and the coalition.** The **community**, its NGOs, and its scientists are the force that gathers the evidence and makes the case, as the reef science and the watershed alliance did.

**The science and the world.** The **scientific evidence** and Palau's **international standing** as a conservation leader are levers, persuading leaders and drawing allies.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective campaign begins by finding the exact project and harm that govern the threat — and the leverage the community holds, which here is often the science and the leaders close to the community. Vague opposition goes nowhere; a fight aimed at "the clearing, which the science shows will smother our reef and cut our catch, which our state and chiefs can ban, and whose land we can place under protection" is a campaign — the kind that stopped the clearing in Airai. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What harm can the science show?** This is the first Palauan question of this kind. What **solid scientific evidence** can be gathered of harm to the reefs, fish, and water the community depends on, as the reef science showed in Airai. Assess this honestly and precisely at the outset, because in Palau the solidity of the science is what most determines the strength of a campaign. A vague claim of harm, or a concern the community feels but cannot demonstrate, gives the state and chiefs little to act on and the proponent much to dispute. But credible, specific evidence — measured sediment, traced runoff, a documented decline in the reef and the catch — gives the authorities a defensible reason to ban or protect, as the reef science did in Airai. A community should therefore establish, first, what harm can actually be shown and who can help show it, for the strength of the science is the foundation on which the appeal to the leaders and every other lever depends.

**2. Which state and traditional leaders can act?** Which **state government and traditional chiefs** have authority over the project and could ban it or protect the area, as Airai's state did.

**3. Can the area be protected?** Can the land be placed under **lasting protection** — a community-owned protected area, a ban — as the watershed areas were.

**4. Who can supply the science and the support?** Which **research bodies and conservation partners** can document the harm, as reef scientists did. This question rewards early attention, because in Palau the scientific documentation is both the decisive lever and the part a community can least supply alone. A community knows its reef and its declining catch, but turning that knowledge into evidence a state or a chief can act on — and a proponent cannot easily dismiss — usually needs the reef and watershed scientists whose work carried the Airai case. Establishing early which research bodies and conservation partners can document the harm therefore shapes what the whole campaign can attempt, and a clear, answerable question presented to them is often the most productive first step a community can take.

**5. Who decides, who benefits, and what is at stake?** Which proponent and officials are behind it, who profits; and what the project does to the reefs, the fish, the water, and the community's way of life.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on. The state, the chiefs, a scientist, and the community all move on evidence, not indignation — and in Palau, the scientific proof of harm to the reefs and fish is precisely what a campaign turns into a ban or a protection, so document it well and early. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Project, the Permit, and the Deciders

Get the documents the case turns on. Identify the **project, its proponent, and its permit**; and establish **which state and traditional leaders** have authority over it, and **what protection framework** — the Protected Areas Network, a state ban — could apply. Establish the deciders and the project precisely, because they are who the science must persuade.

## Layer 2: The Harm — the Science of Reef and Land

Document the reality the project understates, and here the **science is central**. Record, with the help of research bodies, **how the project harms the reefs, fish, and water** — the sediment from clearing, the runoff, the damage to the fishery — as reef science showed how land clearing smothered Airai's reefs. Record the **value** of what is at stake — the reef, the fishery, the watershed, the way of life — and the **land-and-reef link** that shows protecting the land protects the sea. This is the evidence that persuades leaders and community alike. The science of the land-and-reef link, in particular, deserves to be documented with care, because it is both the most persuasive and the least obvious part of the case. Palauans have always understood the sea; the harder thing to show, and the thing that changed minds in Airai, is that clearing the land — ground far from the water’s edge, or the mangroves along the shore — sends sediment down the watershed and onto the reefs, harming the corals and the fish. Documenting that connection concretely, with the help of reef and watershed scientists, turns a development that looks purely terrestrial into a demonstrated threat to the sea the community depends on, and gives the state and the chiefs a reason grounded in the community’s own deepest concern. A well-documented land-and-reef link is often the single most decisive piece of evidence a Palauan campaign can hold.

## Layer 3: The Science, the Authority, and the Protection

Finally, document the case. Does the **science** solidly show harm? Which **state and traditional leaders** can act on it? And can the area be placed under **lasting protection**? Each is a separate ground — for the state, the chiefs, the community, and the partners — and together they make a case that is hard to wave away and that points to a concrete remedy: a ban, a protected area. This is the layer that most often decides Palauan fights of this kind, because here harm is scientifically shown, an authority close to the community is identified who can act, and a lasting protection is proposed — exactly the case that banned the clearing in Airai, where solid reef science brought to a reachable state government turned understanding into a ban. Assembling all three grounds together — the science, the authority, and the protection — is what gives a Palauan case its force, because each does a different job and they reinforce one another: the science supplies the demonstrated harm, the state and chiefs supply the power to act on it, and the protected area or ban supplies a concrete, lasting remedy the leaders can grant. A campaign that shows harm but names no authority who can act, or that reaches the leaders but proposes no clear remedy, leaves the decision-makers with a problem and no solution. Shown together — a scientifically demonstrated harm, an authority close to the community with power to act, and a concrete protection to grant — they make the ban or the protected area the natural response, which is how understanding became a ban in Airai.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION — HOW TO BUILD THE COMMUNITY AND THE COALITION

Evidence does not win on its own. A united community and a broad coalition do — and in Palau, the Airai and Babeldaob communities prove that a community that marshals the science and works together through its states and chiefs can ban a destructive practice and protect its watersheds. A campaign's strength here is that unity and that partnership: a community that acts together, backed by science and carried through its own leaders.

## Phase 1: The Core and the Community

Start with a committed core rooted in the **affected community and local leaders**, and do the distinctively Palauan first work: **connect the community to the science**, so people see how a clearing or development harms the reefs and fish they depend on, as reef science persuaded Airai's people to act. Assign roles — the science, the advocacy to the state and chiefs, the protection, media, and liaison with partners. Agree on the goal — the practice banned, the area protected — and the remedy you will seek. Being clear about the remedy from the start matters more here than in many places, because the authorities a Palauan campaign appeals to — the state, the chiefs — act by granting concrete things: a ban on a practice, a refusal of a permit, an area placed under protection. A community that knows exactly what it is asking for, and can name the specific ban or protection it seeks, gives those authorities something definite to grant and is far more likely to win than one that voices only opposition. Decide early what a win looks like in concrete terms, and build the whole campaign toward securing it.

## Phase 2: The Science, the Leaders, and the Broad Coalition

Then widen deliberately, and build the partnership with the **science and the leaders** that is Palau's distinctive strength, as reef scientists and the state carried the Airai case. Bring in the **whole community and its clubs and organisations**, whose unity makes the case strong; the **research bodies and conservation partners**, whose science documents the harm; the **state government and the traditional chiefs**, whose authority can ban and protect; the **watershed alliances and protected-area networks**, whose model offers lasting protection; and the **national and international conservation world** that watches Palau. The Airai and Babeldaob campaigns drew their power from exactly this partnership — the community, the science, the leaders — which is precisely the spread a Palauan campaign of this kind needs. The partnership with research bodies deserves particular emphasis, because in Palau the science is both the decisive lever and the piece a community is least able to supply on its own. A community knows its reef and its catch, but documenting the harm to scientific standard — measuring the sediment, tracing the runoff, linking the land clearing to the reef decline — usually requires the reef and watershed scientists whose work made the Airai case. A community should therefore reach out early to Palau’s research institutions and conservation partners, presenting a clear question their evidence can answer, for their science is what turns the community’s concern into a case the state and chiefs can act on. This partnership between local knowledge and formal science is at the heart of how Palauan communities protect their reefs.

**A worked coalition example.** Facing a harmful clearing, the community and its clubs unite; research bodies document the reef harm; the state government and chiefs are brought to act; and a watershed alliance offers a protected-area model. No single group could ban a practice and protect a watershed; together they cover the community, the science, and the authority — the model Airai and Babeldaob built. The lesson of that model is that no single part of a coalition needs to be able to win alone; what matters is that, between them, the parts cover every front a Palauan fight of this kind requires — the community to make the case its own, the scientists to prove the harm, the state and chiefs to grant the remedy, and the watershed alliances to supply a model of lasting protection. A community that maps these fronts and finds a partner for each builds something far stronger than the sum of its parts, and far harder for a proponent to dismiss than any one group acting alone.

## Phase 3: Sustaining It

A campaign can run for months or years, so endurance matters. Meet on a rhythm that can be kept. Renew the coalition, bringing in new people as others tire. Give people concrete tasks — the science, the advocacy, the protection, liaison with partners. Mark the wins that come — a study completed, a chief persuaded, a state moving, an area protected — because in a long fight morale is sustained by real gains. Keep the coalition broad, and guard against the **modest scale** of what one campaign can achieve, the **length of the fight**, and the honest fact that the **biggest wins are leader-led**, so a community must patiently win its leaders. Value a local win: a watershed protected is a real and lasting gain. Hold this honestly in view from the start, because the shape of success here is unusual and can be misread as failure. A single campaign will not turn back every pressure on a small nation’s land and reefs; what it can do is protect a particular watershed, ban a particular harmful practice, place a particular area under lasting care — real, durable gains that a campaign measuring itself only against the whole would overlook. Palau’s conservation has been built precisely from such local protections, watershed by watershed and reef by reef, and a community that secures one has both protected something irreplaceable and added to a growing network that protects more. Value the local win, and build the next upon it.

## Phase 4: The Community, the Science, and the Leaders

Here is Palau's distinctive strength, on two levels. First, the **community and the science**: unite the community and arm it with **solid scientific evidence** of harm, so the case is both broad and grounded, as it was in Airai. Second, and decisively, the **state and traditional leaders**: carry the science to the **state government and the chiefs**, and invoke the tradition of **local protection and bul**, so authority close to the community acts, as Airai's state banned the clearing and communities protected their watersheds. Keep it **united, evidence-based, and worked through the leaders**, because in Palau it is a community that acts together, brings solid science, and wins over its own state and chiefs that bans a destructive practice and protects its land — as the Airai and Babeldaob communities did.

# STEP 4: LEGAL & CUSTOMARY CHALLENGES — THE STATE, THE TRADITIONAL LEADERS, AND THE PROTECTIONS

Palau's states, its traditional authority, its Protected Areas Network, and — distinctively — its tradition of community-owned protection and the moratorium of bul are a real toolkit. You do not need to win in a national court to win: a state ban, a chief's authority invoked, an area placed under lasting protection — each is a real gain, and together they can stop a destructive project. Engage partners and leaders early — but understand the tools.

## The State and Its Power to Ban and Permit

The first lever is the **state government's power over local land use**. Palau's states are close to their communities and control much local development, and a **state can ban a destructive practice or refuse a permit**, as Airai state banned mangrove clearing. Bring the science to the state; it is the authority most able to act quickly and locally. It is worth being precise about why the state is often the most effective place to seek a remedy. Because Palau’s states are small and close to their communities, a state government can respond to a local harm far faster than a national process, and its authority over local land use is direct and real — a state can ban a practice or refuse a permit within its jurisdiction without waiting on national politics. A community that brings solid, locally-relevant evidence to its own state speaks to decision-makers who share its concerns and can act on them promptly, as Airai’s state did. This is why a Palauan campaign so often begins by identifying the state authority with power over the project and building the case for it to act.

## The Traditional Leaders and the Tradition of Bul

The second and most distinctive lever is the **authority of the traditional chiefs and the tradition of bul**. The chiefs hold real authority and custom, advise leaders, and can invoke **bul**, the moratorium that halts the use of a resource to let it recover, as bul grounds Palau's protections. Win the chiefs, and invoke the tradition; their authority and the legitimacy of custom carry weight the state and the community both respect. The tradition of bul deserves particular emphasis, because it is a lever peculiar to Palau and easy for an outsider to underestimate. Bul is not a quaint custom but a living practice of resource protection — a moratorium that halts the taking of a resource so it can recover — with deep legitimacy in Palauan society, and Palau’s modern conservation, including its celebrated protected areas, has drawn on it explicitly. A community that frames its request in these terms is not merely making an environmental argument but invoking a respected cultural authority, one the chiefs can exercise and the community readily accepts. Aligning a campaign with the tradition of bul and the authority of the chiefs therefore gives it a legitimacy and a durability that a purely technical appeal would lack.

## The Protected Areas and the Partners

The third lever is **lasting protection through the Protected Areas Network and the community-owned model**. An area placed under **community-owned protection** and recognised by the national network holds against future harm, as the watershed protected areas do. And **research bodies and conservation partners** supply the science and support that make the case, as they did for the reef and watershed work. Use them together with the state and the chiefs.

## Where to Turn and With Whom

The ban goes through the **state government**; the moratorium and legitimacy through the **traditional chiefs and bul**; the lasting protection through the **Protected Areas Network and the community-owned model**; the science through the **research bodies and partners**. Get help early: these leaders and partners know the states, the custom, the network, and the science, and they will support a community with a solid, evidence-based case, as they supported Airai and Babeldaob. Help engaged early is worth far more than help sought after, and in Palau the strongest strategy marries the **community and the science** to the **state and traditional leaders and the tradition of local protection**, so that a destructive project is met by a community armed with evidence and carried through its own authorities — as the mangrove clearing was in Airai, until it was banned.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

You have a file of evidence. Now you must turn it into arguments — because the same fact persuades the state, a chief, a scientist, a journalist, and the community in different ways. Learning to translate your evidence for each audience is what lets a community reach every lever at once.

## One Fact, Many Arguments

Take a single documented fact — say, that clearing the land near the shore sends sediment onto the reef and cuts the community's fish catch. That one fact becomes:

- **A scientific argument:** the evidence shows measurable harm to the reef and fishery — the case the state and chiefs can act on.
- **A state argument:** this is within the state's power to ban or refuse — the case for local action.
- **A customary argument:** the chiefs can invoke bul to protect the resource — the case grounded in tradition. Custom, here, is not weaker than law but in some ways stronger, because it carries a legitimacy the whole community already accepts.
- **A livelihood argument:** the reef feeds our families, and this harms our catch — the case that unites the community.
- **A protection argument:** this area should be placed under lasting protection — the concrete remedy. Naming the protection you seek turns a complaint into a proposal a leader can grant.

Same fact, five arguments, five audiences. Go through your strongest facts and, for each, ask what it proves to each audience. That table is your campaign's argument bank — and in Palau the arguments that most often decide things are the **scientific** and **state/customary** ones, because they move the authorities close to the community, and the **livelihood** one, because it unites the people. Keep the arguments anchored to the same documented facts, so a claim made to a chief can be backed and a claim made to the state can be proven; consistency across audiences is itself persuasive. And be honest about the state of the fight: acknowledge the scale is often local, and argue for the real, lasting protection a community win can secure.

## Match the Argument to the Audience

**The state government** responds to solid evidence of local harm and its own power to ban or protect.

**The traditional chiefs** respond to the harm to the community's resources and the legitimacy of custom and bul.

**The scientists and partners** respond to a clear question their evidence can answer, and a community ready to act on it.

**Journalists** need a specific, vivid story with a hook: a community using science to save its reef from a harmful clearing.

**The community** needs the argument clear and close to home: our reef, our fish, our water, our future. The livelihood argument, though it moves people most, must stay anchored to the documented science if it is to serve the other fronts as well. The dependence of an island community on its reef and its catch rallies people rightly; but the same case, stated precisely — the measured sediment, the specific harm, the concrete remedy — is also what the state, the chiefs, and the partners require. A campaign that lets the livelihood argument drift into exaggeration hands the proponent an easy rebuttal and weakens the case built on the same ground. Keep the livelihood argument vivid but true, so that the concern that stirs the community and the evidence that moves the leaders are one and the same, drawn from a single reliable record.

**The international conservation world** responds to Palau's example and to a community protecting globally valued nature. Because this audience is moved by exactly such stakes, a campaign gains by framing its case in those terms. The international conservation community — the reef and ocean groups, the sympathetic press abroad — responds not only to the harm to a single reef but to Palau’s standing as a global conservation leader and to the example of a community protecting nature the world values. A campaign that can show its reef is globally significant and its community determined speaks directly to what this community exists to support, and can draw the scientific help, the amplification, and the reinforcement that a small community cannot generate alone. Palau’s conservation fame is itself a resource a local campaign can draw upon.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY — HOW COVERAGE SHIFTS OUTCOMES

Media is not vanity; it is a lever — and in Palau, where a community using science to protect its reef fits the nation's proud conservation story and international coverage draws allies, coverage that carries that story is among the most powerful levers of all. Coverage unites the community, moves the state and chiefs, and draws partners and attention. The reef and watershed work became part of Palau's conservation story — the science, the community, and the lasting protection.

## Build the Story

Reporters and the public need specifics, and here they need the science and the reef. "A clearing is controversial" is not a story; "the science shows this clearing is smothering the reef our families fish, and the community is asking the state to ban it, as Airai did" is — the kind of story that fits Palau's conservation fame. Lead with your strongest verified facts — the science, the harm to the reef, the remedy — and offer a human voice and the beauty of the place: a fisher, a scientist, an image of the reef and the watershed. The specific and the local matter here: not conservation in the abstract, but this reef, these families, this catch that is thinning, and the clearing upstream that the science ties to it. In Palau the story of a community using knowledge and custom to protect its reef is one the nation and the world respond to.

## Reach the Right Outlets

Work outward in rings. **The community and local voices** first. Then the **national outlets** — because Palau's reefs and its conservation leadership are a national pride and concern. Then the **international ring** — the conservation press, the partners, the world's media — because Palau's nature is globally valued and its example widely watched. Feed each ring what it needs, and let coverage in one pull in the next. The **national and conservation rings are distinctly important** here, because they reinforce the community's case with the nation's own conservation story.

## Sustain It, and Keep It True

A single article changes little; a drumbeat changes decisions. Plan a sequence — the science, the harm, the advocacy, the ban, the protection — so there is always a next hook, and build your own clear record so the story endures. Keep messaging disciplined, accurate, and honest; the proponent will seize on any exaggeration, and a credible, science-and-reef-centred campaign is what fits Palau's conservation story and sustains a community cause. Verified, specific, science-grounded, and reef-focused — that is the coverage that shifts outcomes here, and that fits how Palau protects its nature.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Written communications are how you request, propose, create a record, and reach the bodies that can act — the state, the chiefs, the partners, and the press. Each does double duty: it asks for something and it documents that you asked. Keep copies of everything, and send important items so that receipt is provable. Below are adaptable templates for the situations you will meet most often. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the science, the authority, and the protection.

## 8A. Request for Information

**To:** the relevant state / national authority
**Subject:** Request for information on [project]

We, [community/organisation], request the **permit, the plan, and the terms** for [project] at [place]. As the affected community, we are entitled to the record. Please provide the documents.

## 8B. Appeal to the State Government

**To:** the [state] government
**Subject:** Ban [harmful practice] to protect our reef

We, [community/organisation] of [state], present evidence that [project/practice] harms [our reef and fishery] through [sediment/runoff/clearing]. We ask the state to **ban [the practice] / refuse the permit** and protect the reef our community depends on, as [Airai state banned mangrove clearing].

## 8C. Appeal to the Traditional Leaders

**To:** the [traditional chiefs / Council of Chiefs]
**Subject:** Invoke our tradition to protect [place]

We, [community/organisation], ask our traditional leaders to protect [place], whose reef and fishery are harmed by [project]. We ask you to consider **bul** or your authority to halt the harm, as our tradition of the moratorium has long protected our resources.

## 8D. Request to Research & Conservation Partners

**To:** [research body / conservation NGO]
**Subject:** Help us document the harm from [project]

We are opposing [project], which we believe harms [our reef and fishery]. We seek your help to **document the harm scientifically** — the sediment, the runoff, the damage — so we can bring solid evidence to our state and chiefs, as reef science helped Airai protect its reef.

## 8E. Proposal for a Protected Area

**To:** the [state / Protected Areas Network]
**Subject:** Place [place] under lasting protection

We, [community/organisation], propose that [place] be placed under **community-owned protection** within the Protected Areas Network, to protect our watershed and reef for the future, as communities across Babeldaob have protected theirs.

## 8F. Appeal to the Proponent

**To:** [proponent] — its management
**Subject:** [Project] harms our reef and fishery

We, [community/organisation], inform you that [project] harms [our reef and fishery], as the science shows. We ask you to reconsider, and we are bringing the evidence to our state, our chiefs, and the public.

## 8G. Email to a Journalist

**To:** [journalist]
**Subject:** Story tip: a community using science to save its reef — [specific finding]

I follow your coverage of [beat]. We have a story that fits Palau's conservation pride, with a clear hook: [evidence] shows [finding — e.g. "this clearing is smothering the reef we fish, and our community is asking the state to ban it"]. It echoes how Airai protected its reef. I can share the file and connect you with a fisher and a scientist. Could we talk this week?

## 8H. Public Statement

**To:** the community and the authorities
**Subject:** [Coalition] will protect [place] and its reef

We, [coalition], oppose [project], which the science shows harms our reef, our fish, and our water. As Airai used science to ban a harmful clearing and communities across Babeldaob protected their watersheds, we will bring the evidence to our state and chiefs, invoke our tradition of protection, and place our land under lasting care. Our reef sustains us, and we will protect it. We ask our leaders to act, and our neighbours to stand with us, as communities across our islands have acted to protect what sustains them.

Send important communications so that receipt is provable, and log every one in your timeline — the record of a request, an appeal to the state or chiefs, or a proposal for protection is itself evidence. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the science, the authority, and the protection; attach your evidence; and route them, where you can, through the community, the research partners, and the leaders. The reply, or the result, becomes part of your record, and in Palau that record feeds the levers that matter most: the science, the state and traditional leaders, the tradition of local protection, and the land-and-reef link that protected Airai's reef.

Do only the first three and you will have started the science, united the community, and taken the case to the state — the foundation of a Palauan campaign. Then come back and read the rest. Even the shortest version has a spine: get the science, unite the community, take it to the state and chiefs. A note for the resource-poor community in particular: in Palau the decisive moves are, fortunately, mostly within reach. # WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Palau's system, with states close to their communities, real traditional authority, a proud tradition of conservation, and a place of honour in the international conservation world, offers a community real tools to protect its land and reefs — but it is tilted in specific ways you must understand, so you neither waste effort nor grow discouraged.

## Where the Tilt Shows

The tilt here is real. This is a **small nation** facing large forces — foreign investment, a flood of tourism, a changing climate — against which a single community's leverage is bounded. The community-driven wins are **modest and local** — a state ban, a protected area — while the **biggest victories are led by national leaders and law**, so a community depends on winning supportive leaders and chiefs. And terrestrial protection has historically **lagged** behind marine protection, so the land can be cleared before the harm to the reef is felt. Where these tilts bite, a campaign that assumes one community can stop a national mega-project, or that the land is as protected as the sea, will be disappointed — which is precisely why the distinctive strategy is to marshal the science, work through the state and chiefs, and establish lasting local protection.

## What Still Works — And Some Is Strong

The decisive point is that even against this tilt, Palau's levers are real, and some are strong.

**Solid science still persuades.** Evidence of harm to the reef and fishery moves leaders and community, as it did in Airai.

**State and traditional leaders still act.** Authority close to the community can ban a practice or protect an area.

**Local protection still holds.** A community-owned protected area or a ban lasts, as the watershed areas do.

**The land-and-reef link still convinces.** Showing that protecting the land protects the fish is uniquely persuasive here.

**Palau's conservation story still helps.** The nation's pride and international standing reinforce a community's case.

## The Honest Frame

So the honest frame for Palau is realistic and clear-eyed: the wins are modest and local, the biggest are leader-led, and a small nation's leverage is limited — but the science, the state and traditional leaders, the tradition of local protection, and the land-and-reef link give a community real, and sometimes strong, levers, and protecting a watershed and a reef is a real and lasting victory. So diagnose your fight honestly: marshal the **science**; work through the **state and chiefs**; establish **lasting protection**; unite the **community**; make the **land-and-reef case**. Do not assume a destructive clearing cannot be banned; a community with good science banned one in Airai. And do not assume that because the land has been treated as less protected than the sea, it must stay that way: the whole thrust of the community conservation that protected Babeldaob’s watersheds was to extend protection from the reef the people already valued to the land whose clearing was harming it. The lever is to carry the community’s deep concern for the sea onto the land, showing that the two are one system and that protecting the watershed is protecting the reef.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO IS CAPTURED

Palau, like any nation, has had concerns about foreign money and the pressure of powerful interests — a foreign fishing lobby seeking to reopen protected waters, investors seeking land — so this section matters. When a project is driven not by the community's interest but by connected or foreign money, or advanced despite clear harm, your strategy includes exposing that.

## How to Tell

Look for the signatures of a captured or heedless process. **Clear scientific harm ignored**; **a permit granted despite the damage to the reef**; **foreign money pressing to open protected land or water**, as a foreign fishing lobby has pressed to reopen Palau's waters; **the community's voice and the chiefs' authority bypassed**; and **close ties** between the proponent, officials, and the powerful. Trace the project: how the permit was granted, who profits, and whether the science and the community were considered. In Palau, a project advanced despite clear harm to the reef the community depends on is worth examining for the interests behind it.

## Who Can Be Captured — and Who Is Harder

Be clear-eyed about where capture reaches. **Parts of the national government** can be pressed by foreign money or powerful investors. But some forces are far harder to capture, and they are your targets: the **states**, close to their communities; the **traditional chiefs** and the **Council of Chiefs**, whose authority rests on custom and community; the **scientists** and research bodies, whose evidence is independent; the **Protected Areas Network** and the tradition of bul; and the **community** itself. Distinctively, the traditional authority of the chiefs and the independent science of the research bodies are checks that foreign money finds hard to reach. Your task is to document the harm and the process, and bring them to the states, the chiefs, the scientists, the press, and the community.

## What to Do

Do not assume a captured or heedless project cannot be challenged. Instead, **document it precisely** — the scientific harm, the permit, the community ignored, the interest served — and take it to the forces that can act: the **states**; the **traditional chiefs**; the **scientists and partners**; the **press**; and the **community**. Pair the exposure with the science and the tradition of protection, so the harm and the remedy arrive together. In Palau, exposing a project advanced despite clear harm to the reef, and bringing solid science to the states and chiefs, is often the way to stop it — as the science of reef harm and the authority of the state banned the clearing in Airai and the chiefs' authority has defended Palau's protected waters. The pairing of the independent science and the customary authority is what makes this so hard for money to overcome: a foreign interest may sway a distant official, but it cannot easily buy the findings of independent reef scientists, nor override the legitimacy that the chiefs and the tradition of bul hold in Palauan society. A community that grounds its case in both — evidence money cannot fake and authority money cannot purchase — builds a defence against capture that is stronger than either alone. Do this with care, for challenging powerful interests invites pushback.

Keep the record and the science, unite the community, win the state and the chiefs, establish the protection, and treat every study completed, every leader persuaded, and every area protected as a lasting gain, even when the whole cannot be won at once.

The tools are here, and the Airai and Babeldaob communities proved them — modestly and honestly: reef science that showed how clearing the land smothered the reefs and cut the catch; a community that carried that evidence to its own state government until the clearing of mangroves was banned; and communities across the largest island that, drawing on the tradition of bul and the model of community-owned protection, established watershed protected areas that hold today — even as Palau's terrestrial protection still lags behind its famous seas and a small nation faces forces far larger than itself. What decides the outcome, in the end, is the solidity of the science, the closeness of the states and chiefs to the community, the strength of a tradition of protection, and the reef the people will not see destroyed — and those a determined community can summon. Begin today, begin with the science and the community and the reef, and build from there. Communities before you have used knowledge and custom to protect their land and their reefs. What Airai and Babeldaob won — a harmful clearing banned and watersheds placed under lasting protection — can be won again by others who bring the science and work through their leaders. So, in time and with patience, can yours.

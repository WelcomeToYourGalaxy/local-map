# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN BURUNDI

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Burundi is a small, mountainous, extraordinarily densely populated Great Lakes nation, among the poorest on earth, whose western edge is the shore of **Lake Tanganyika** — one of the world's largest, deepest, and most biodiverse lakes, and the lifeline of the nation's millions. Its steep, deforested slopes wash their soil into that lake with every rain, and the sediment, overfishing, and pollution are slowly degrading it. Joined to that is a second great question: the vast **Musongati nickel deposit**, whose development promises revenue a desperately poor country badly needs, yet threatens its water and land. Burundi is a land of green, over-farmed hills, of the lake and its fisheries, of a rapidly growing rural population on too little land, and of a governance that is authoritarian-leaning and a civic space that is narrowed. The shared lake and the scrutiny of the nickel give real ground.

A community in Burundi facing a harmful project — a nickel mine threatening the water and land, a development degrading the lake — works with real, if constrained, tools, kept careful and watershed-grounded. A major project like the Musongati mine is supposed to undergo an **environmental and social impact assessment** — a gate that can be pressed where it is skipped or defective. The mine can be scrutinised against **environmental, water, and governance standards**, and against the **international standards and financiers** a large nickel project answers to. The **regional framework for Lake Tanganyika** — the shared convention and authority of the riparian states — is a distinctive transboundary lever: harm to a lake four nations depend on is not Burundi's alone to permit. The **courts and functioning institutions** can be reached where it is safe. And the **technical, regional, and international channels** carry the case where the state is fraught. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The state is semi-closed and authoritarian-leaning, and the civic space constrained, so the work must be careful, inside the system, and strictly neutral on the politics and the ethnic history. The nickel is revenue a desperately poor country needs, so it must be scrutinised, not simply opposed. And the deforestation and erosion that harm the lake are bound up with poverty and density — so justice forbids mere bans, and the poor who farm the hills to survive must never be met with repression. So the work must protect everyone involved, stay watershed-grounded and apolitical, and aim at the harm scrutinised and restrained — the mine assessed, the lake's regional framework invoked, the pollution documented — not an open defeat of a needed development. Above all, protect the people, and never punish the poor.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot simply oppose a nickel deposit a poor country needs, nor safely mount open confrontation in a semi-closed state, nor justly ban the poor from the hills they farm to live. What you can do is scrutinise the harm: force the mine through a real assessment, hold it to environmental and governance standards, invoke the shared Lake Tanganyika framework four nations answer to, document the harm to the lake, and work through the technical, regional, and international channels — carefully, watershed-grounded, apolitical, and protecting everyone.

## Be Honest About Which Fight You're In

Burundi is a poor, fragile, constrained setting, and honesty about that is the first discipline. This is not a fight you win by opposing a needed development or by banning the poor, nor by open confrontation with a semi-closed state. It is a careful, inside the system, watershed-grounded effort whose realistic prize is the harm scrutinised and restrained — a mine assessed and conditioned, the lake's framework invoked, the pollution pressed — not an open stop. Frame everything around the lake and the environment, never the politics, and protect everyone involved.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Ask five questions. **What is the harm?** — the sediment and pollution to the lake, the mine's threat to water and land, the erosion. **What does it owe?** — an assessment, environmental and water standards, the shared lake's regional obligations. **Who stands behind it?** — the company, the financiers, and the standards they answer to. **Which channel is safe?** — the assessment, the regional framework, the international bodies, the courts where safe. **How is everyone kept safe, and the poor not punished?** — the overriding questions, answered by staying watershed-grounded and apolitical and contesting the harm, not the poor. Pick the harm where the assessment or the lake framework offers the clearest, safest handle.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Build the case in layers, watershed-grounded. **Layer one: the harm to the lake** — the sediment, pollution, or mine footprint documented, and the science of the lake's fragility. **Layer two: the standard and the framework** — what assessment, environmental, water, and governance standards the project owes, and what the shared Lake Tanganyika framework requires, and where they were skipped. **Layer three: the channel and the safety** — which functioning institution, regional body, or international financier to reach, and the absolute discipline of staying apolitical, protecting everyone, and never targeting the poor. A case built on the lake's science and the shared framework — technical and apolitical — is one the institutions and the region must answer.

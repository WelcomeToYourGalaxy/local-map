# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN SAINT VINCENT AND THE GRENADINES

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community effort in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines once showed that a people, joined by their descendants across the world, can keep a sacred island out of a developer's hands and win it back for the nation. When **Balliceaux** — a small, uninhabited island of deep ancestral meaning, the place to which thousands of Garifuna and Kalinago were exiled after the wars of the eighteenth century, and where about half of them died — was put up for sale on the international market for millions of dollars, open to whatever a foreign buyer might build, the descendants of those who suffered there did not let it pass in silence. The Garifuna people of the islands and, powerfully, the far larger Garifuna diaspora abroad — who return each year on pilgrimage to that ground — pressed their case: this island must not be sold for development, but protected as sacred heritage and returned to the people. Their heritage foundation made the demand, the annual pilgrimages carried it, and over several years the government refused to license any development that would cheapen the island's meaning, and at last acquired the island itself, vesting it in the nation as a protected memorial site. The island was kept from the developer. The sacred ground was reclaimed.

A community in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines that would protect a threatened place works, as the Garifuna descendants did, through genuinely real tools, in an open island democracy. The distinctive lever is the **heritage-and-justice claim**: a place of deep historical and cultural meaning is not weighed by the nation as mere land, and to sell it for development can be made to feel like a wound to the national story — the more so where, as at Balliceaux, a historical wrong is bound up with the place, so that protecting it becomes a kind of justice. Bound to this is the **national-identity and national-heroes appeal** — the Garifuna resistance and its heroes are the nation's own pride. The **government's power to refuse development and to acquire and protect the land** is a real mechanism, as Balliceaux proved, backed by **heritage designation and protected-site status**; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. Ordinary harmful projects, too, must reckon with **environmental impact assessment and the planning regulation**, and the **open democratic and community channels** — the elected House of Assembly, the community, the courts — genuinely work. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The Balliceaux lever was unusual: it rested on a heritage-and-justice claim and a passionate, far-flung diaspora, and not every fight has either — an ordinary hotel, port, or quarry without that sacred meaning relies on the weaker ordinary tools of assessment, planning, and democratic pressure. The nation is small, poor, and tourism-dependent, so the development pressure is real and constant. And the government's willingness, so decisive at Balliceaux, cannot be assumed. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a place of heritage protected and even reclaimed, as Balliceaux was; an ordinary project assessed, conditioned, or refused — through the heritage-and-justice claim where it exists, the government's protective power, the assessment and planning, and the open democratic channels, mobilising the community and, where it exists, the diaspora.

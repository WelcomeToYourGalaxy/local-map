# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN SÃO TOMÉ AND PRÍNCIPE

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community effort in São Tomé and Príncipe once showed that a small population can turn back a powerful foreign company. When a large European agro-industrial group secured a government concession to plant thousands of hectares of industrial oil palm across both islands — a plantation that would have cleared forest and remade the land — the smaller island of **Príncipe** did something decisive: its people opposed the deal and refused to let the plantation take root on their island, saving more than a thousand hectares of forest and the unique wildlife that lives nowhere else on earth. The plantation never came to Príncipe. And there was a second lesson in how they won: the islanders' resistance ran alongside a competing vision of Príncipe as a place of ecotourism and protected nature rather than monoculture — an alternative future that powerful interests also favoured, and that gave the community's opposition extra weight. Between the popular resistance and the alternative vision, the plantation lost the battle for Príncipe.

A community in São Tomé and Príncipe that would turn back a harmful concession works, as Príncipe did, through genuinely real tools, in an open island democracy. The distinctive lever is the **alternative-land-use vision and the biosphere and protected-nature designations**: the most powerful way to break a government's bargain of land-for-investment is not merely to refuse but to offer a better yes — the same land worth more as protected nature and ecotourism than as a plantation that exhausts the soil within decades, as Príncipe's biosphere future made the palm rows out of place. The islands' **unique biodiversity** — wildlife found nowhere else on earth — commands the **world's conservation frameworks**. A concession or project must reckon with **environmental impact assessment and the forest and land law**. Because the proponent is often a **foreign company**, its financiers and reputation are a real pressure point; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. And the **open democratic and community channels** — the elected government and assembly, the community, the courts — genuinely work. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Príncipe's victory was distinctive: the better-yes worked partly because powerful interests also favoured its ecotourism future, and not every fight has such backing. The plantation still came to the larger island of São Tomé; the refusal saved Príncipe, not both islands. An ordinary project without a credible alternative vision relies on the weaker ordinary tools of assessment, law, and democratic pressure. And the nation is small, poor, and keen for investment, so the pressure to grant concessions is real. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a harmful concession turned back where a better yes and the biodiversity can be marshalled, as on Príncipe; a project assessed, conditioned, or refused — through the alternative vision and designations, the conservation frameworks, the assessment and law, the foreign-company pressure, and the open democratic channels.

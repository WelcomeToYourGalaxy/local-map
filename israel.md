# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN ISRAEL

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

On the Mediterranean coast south of Tel Aviv, one of the last undeveloped, natural stretches of beach near Kibbutz Palmachim was slated for a large holiday resort — hundreds of rooms close to the shore — on land a developer had won at a public tender years before. The beach's defenders did not accept that the matter was settled. What began with a high-school student who found the plans and camped on the sand in protest grew, over years, into a national campaign: tents, festivals, and fundraisers, environmental organisations lending their planning and legal expertise, neighbouring towns offering to buy back the land for the public, and, in time, ministers and the courts. The planning authorities cancelled the resort and moved to make the beach a national park; and a court, weighing the public interest against the developer's, ruled that building there would be a tragedy for generations. The beach was saved. On a tiny, crowded coastline where open beach is rare and precious, that fight showed that a campaign — begun by a single determined person — can overturn even an approved development and save the shore.

A community in Israel facing a harmful development works with genuinely strong tools, in an open democracy with a robust planning system and an independent judiciary. Major projects run through the **planning system and its objections process** — a real gate where the public can formally object — and require **environmental impact assessment**. The distinctive lever is the **scarcity-of-the-commons argument**: on a small, crowded country where open beach and land are rare and most already built up or closed off, the loss of one of the last wild stretches is felt as a national loss, understood to be irreversible — a power that transforms a local planning dispute into a matter the whole public will fight for. Valued land can be protected through **national-park and nature-reserve designation**; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. And, crucially, even a settled approval can be reopened — through the planning process, the law, and, as Palmachim showed, legislative reform — with the **independent courts**, which weigh the public interest against the developer's, enforcing it all. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The country is tiny and crowded, and development pressure is intense. A settled, tendered approval is genuinely hard to reopen, and doing so took Palmachim years of patient work. The developer has real rights and interests the courts must weigh. And no single victory ends the pressure on the commons. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a harmful development objected to, reassessed, redesigned, refused, or even overturned after approval, and a scarce commons saved and protected — through the planning system, the scarcity argument, the designations, and the courts, rousing the public to defend what, once built over, will never return.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

An Israeli project runs through a detailed planning system. The **local, district, and national planning committees** decide the plans, the objections, and the approvals. The **environmental authorities and the impact-assessment process** review major projects. The **Israel Nature and Parks Authority** holds the national parks and nature reserves. And the **independent courts** — up to the High Court of Justice — weigh the legality and the public interest, as Palmachim showed. The **environmental organisations** lend real planning and legal expertise. Map who decides a plan's fate, at which level, before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A project moves through the planning committees, with a formal objections stage where the public can be heard, and — for major projects — an environmental impact assessment. That objections stage is your first entry point: the moment to document the harm, dramatise the scarcity, and press for refusal or redesign. And, as Palmachim proved, even a settled approval is not the end: the planning process, the law, and legislative reform can reopen it, and the courts can weigh the public interest against the developer's.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the plan: where a project stands in the planning process, and whether it has completed its objections and assessment. Follow the land: whether it is one of the last open, scarce stretches of coast or countryside. Follow the tender: what rights the developer won, and what public interest weighs against them. The leverage lies in the planning objections, the scarcity argument, the designations, and the courts.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Ask five questions. **What is the harm?** — the resort, development, or building over a scarce open coast or countryside. **Where is it in the planning process?** — at objections, approved, or tendered. **How scarce is the commons?** — how little open land or coast remains, how much is already gone, how many it serves. **What protects it?** — a possible national-park or nature-reserve designation. **Who is the coalition?** — the neighbours, the environmental organisations, the public. Pick the fight where the scarcity is starkest and the planning or legal handle clearest — even, as Palmachim showed, against a settled approval.

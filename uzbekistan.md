# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN UZBEKISTAN

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the land and cities of Uzbekistan, and one summer of resident protest showed that even in a tightly controlled country, careful public pressure can force a government to abandon a plan it had ordered from the top. In August 2020, when authorities announced a scheme to redraw the boundaries of some Tashkent neighbourhoods — a change that would have pushed residents outside the city and its protections — hundreds of people did something rare and risky in Uzbekistan: they came out into the streets, and within days, after the attention their protest drew, the authorities scrapped the plan. It was not the only such moment. When developers felled a grove of tall old trees for an elite complex, the public outcry — pointing to the government's own decree protecting the trees — turned a routine clearance into a scandal. When bulldozers threatened the historic hearts of the old cities, residents and independent media pushed officials to halt a reconstruction "in the nick of time." None of these was a clean or final victory, and many battles were lost. But together they proved something real: that in Uzbekistan, careful public pressure, amplified by media and anchored to the government's own promises, can move even a reluctant state. The levers, though bounded, are real.

A community in Uzbekistan works, as those residents did, through genuinely real if bounded levers, in a constrained but partially opening system — carefully, for the risks are real. The distinctive lever is the **government's own decrees and promises**: a plan that violates the state's own decree — a tree moratorium, a heritage protection, a reform pledge — can be challenged on the state's own terms, as the tree defenders challenged it, because a government that proclaims a decree does so partly to be seen proclaiming it, and breaking its own word is a scandal it feels. Bound to this is the **appeal to the state's reformist legitimacy**: a state that wishes to be seen reforming, and values its legitimacy, can be moved by being shown to betray that image. The **heritage and environmental designations** — the protected trees, the old cities — are concrete protections to invoke. The **developing environmental-assessment and legal channels** offer a formal, if limited, gate. And the **independent and social media** amplify a case the state media would bury — the force that turned each of these fights. These tools are general — the government's own word, the designations, the assessment, and the media reach any destructive plan, from a redistricting to a tree-felling to a heritage demolition. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The system is tightly controlled and only partially opening, so coming out carries real risk, and the work must be careful and protect those who take part. None of the wins was clean or final, and many battles were lost. The state values its legitimacy but can also repress. And the appeal to the government's own word works only where the word exists. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a destructive plan reversed or halted, as the redistricting and the tree-felling were — by careful public pressure anchored to the government's own decrees and amplified by media, challenging the state on its own terms, carefully, and protecting those who take part.

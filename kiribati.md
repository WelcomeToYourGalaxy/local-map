# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN KIRIBATI

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the reefs, lagoons, and ocean of Kiribati — but this guide must begin honestly, because Kiribati is a poor, remote, low-lying atoll nation where the hardest destructive decisions are driven not by greed but by genuine need, and there is no community victory to point to. Kiribati is a scattered republic of atolls in the central Pacific, home to around 120,000 people, with an ocean territory larger than India and more tuna caught in its waters than in those of any other nation on earthmore tuna caught in its waters, year after year, than in those of any other nation anywhere on earth — and with fishing licences supplying more than seventy percent of the government's revenue. In that context, the country's most consequential recent decision was to reverse one of its own greatest conservation achievements: in late 2021 the Cabinet decided to reopen the Phoenix Islands Protected Area — some four hundred thousand square kilometres of ocean, an area the size of California, a UNESCO World Heritage site and once one of the largest marine reserves on earth — to commercial fishing, because the fund meant to replace the lost revenue had raised only a fraction of what was promised, and a poor nation could not afford to keep so much of its ocean closed.a poor nation, in the end, could not afford to keep so much of its ocean closed to the fishing that sustains it. This guide will not pretend that opposing such a decision is simple, or that the community has beaten it; it has not.

The Kiribati story teaches two things about opposing destructive projects here, and this guide states both plainly. The first is the pattern that runs through every guide: not a single silver bullet, but the combination of every lever — the affected community, the evidence, the coalition, and the pressure on the deciders — pressed together. The second is distinctly I-Kiribati, and it is what gives this guide its real, if difficult, force. **In a poor atoll democracy where development need is real, conservation survives only if it is made to serve the people — and a community's levers are its own customary institutions of collective decision, its democratic process, and the international partners who can make protecting the ocean pay.** That combination — the maneaba, the nation's ancient meeting-house of collective governance; the elected Assembly; and the World Heritage system, the regional bodies, and the conservation partners who can fund and reward protection — is the distinctive lever here, and this guide is built around it, because the lesson of the Phoenix Islands is precisely that conservation that does not meet the people's needs will not last.

But the same story carries honest qualifications this guide states plainly and takes seriously. **Here, the obstacle is often genuine poverty and genuine sovereignty, not villainy.** The government reopened the marine reserve because a low-income nation depends on fishing revenue and the conservation funding fell short — a real dilemma, not a scandal — and it rightly resists having conservation imposed on it from outside as a kind of neo-colonial demand. The historical devastation of Banaba, whose phosphate was strip-mined until the island was left barely habitable and its people resettled far away, is a warning of what extraction can do; but it is also a reminder that a poor island's resources are often all it has. This guide does not pretend the choices are easy or the community has prevailed. What it shows is how, in a poor and sovereign atoll democracy, a community can use its maneaba, its democracy, and its international partners to defend the ocean and the reefs — not by lecturing a struggling nation, but by helping make protection pay, and by insisting that development be done without destroying what the islands depend on.

Most communities do not know HOW to make protection serve the people rather than oppose them. They see a marine reserve reopened, a reef dredged, a lagoon degraded, or seabed mining proposed, and either accept it as the price of survival or oppose it in ways that pit conservation against the nation's needs — and lose. They rarely bring the matter to the maneaba as a collective decision, rarely press the Assembly, rarely reach the partners who could make protection financially worthwhile. The gap between the ocean defended and the ocean lost is here, above all, a gap of method — the method of making conservation and the people's needs one cause, through the community's own institutions.

**This guide shows what can actually be tried — inside the Kiribati reality as it is, which is poor, remote, sovereign, and democratic.** It is written for the person who sees a reef, a lagoon, a fishery, or an island threatened — by reversal of protection, by damaging development, or by extraction — and who wants to know what can be done in a nation that must weigh every acre of ocean against the needs of its people. The answer is a qualified yes: the levers exist — the maneaba, the democracy, the international partners — with the honest qualifiers that the development need is real, the nation is sovereign and poor, conservation must be made to pay, and there is no victory to promise, only a harder and more honest fight to make protection serve the people. A note on who this guide is for, and on the spirit in which it must be used. It is for the person — an islander, a member of an affected community, or a respectful ally — who understands that in Kiribati the fight for the ocean is not against a villain but alongside a poor and proud nation weighing genuine needs, and who is willing to do the harder work of making protection affordable rather than the easier work of denouncing a hard choice. That is not a lesser form of advocacy but the only one that can succeed here, and the only one this guide can honestly recommend. Anyone hoping to shame Kiribati into conservation should understand that a struggling, sovereign nation will rightly reject such pressure, and that the real path runs through the maneaba, the democracy, and the patient building of funded protection. This guide is an honest map of that harder path — the path of serving the ocean and the people as one cause.

## The Levels of Decision

A Kiribati decision runs through several tracks, some customary and some modern, and each is a place to intervene.

**The government and the Cabinet.** The **President and Cabinet** make the great decisions — as the Cabinet decided to reopen the marine reserve — weighing revenue and development, and are answerable, in a democracy, to the Assembly and the people.

**The elected Assembly.** The **Maneaba ni Maungatabu**, the national parliament named for the meeting-house, must approve major measures, and its members answer to their island communities.

**The maneaba.** The **local maneaba** — the meeting-house and its collective deliberation — is the customary heart of community decision, where a community forms and voices its collective will, and the most legitimate forum for local opposition.

**The World Heritage and conservation systems.** **UNESCO's World Heritage status**, and the **international conservation partners and funds**, can reward, finance, and pressure for protection, and can make it pay.

**The regional and fisheries bodies.** The **Pacific regional and fisheries bodies** give Kiribati collective strength over its tuna and its ocean, and are a lever for sustainable management.

The great question is almost always the same: not whether to develop or protect in the abstract, but **how the people's real needs can be met without destroying the ocean they depend on**. The distinctive levers are the **maneaba**, the **Assembly**, the **World Heritage and conservation partners**, and the **regional bodies**. Find out exactly where the decision stands, and, crucially, **what would be lost**, **what need is driving it**, and **how protection might be made to serve that need**, because that is where a destructive decision can be turned.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective effort begins by finding the exact decision and harm that govern the threat — and the leverage the community holds, which here is the maneaba, the democracy, and the partners who can make protection pay. Vague concern goes nowhere, and conservation appeals that ignore the nation's need tend to fail; an effort aimed at "the reopening of this reserve, which would harm this fishery and this reef, driven by a revenue shortfall that conservation finance could instead meet, and which the maneaba and the Assembly can weigh" is a real, honest effort — the kind that can actually turn a decision here. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What would be lost?** This is the first question here. What **ecological and livelihood harm** would the decision do — the reef, the lagoon, the fishery, the marine reserve — as the Phoenix Islands' reopening threatens tuna, reefs, and deep-sea habitats.

**2. What need is driving it?** What **revenue or development need** — as the reserve was reopened for fishing revenue — is driving the decision, since meeting that need honestly is the key to turning it.

**3. Can protection be made to pay?** How might **conservation finance, sustainable fisheries, or livelihoods** make protection serve the people rather than oppose them, as the reserve's endowment was meant to but failed to.

**4. How can the maneaba and Assembly be engaged?** How can the **community's collective voice in the maneaba** and the **elected Assembly** be brought to weigh the decision.

**5. Which partners and bodies can help?** Which **conservation partners, World Heritage bodies, and regional and fisheries bodies** can fund, recognise, and support protection.

## A Worked Example

Suppose a marine reserve is to be reopened to fishing, or a reef dredged for development, to meet a revenue or infrastructure need.

Working the five questions: **what would be lost**? **What need is driving it**? **Can protection be made to pay**? **How can the maneaba and Assembly be engaged**? And which partners can help? The deciders are the Cabinet, the Assembly, and the ministries, with the maneaba, the Assembly, the partners, and funded protection as the pressure points.

Within a season you have moved from "the nation needs the revenue and there is nothing to be done" to a real effort: document the loss and the need; bring it to the maneaba; press the Assembly; and — decisively — enlist partners to make protection pay for the need it would otherwise cost. That is an honest effort suited to Kiribati — one that meets the nation's need rather than ignoring it. Notice what the five questions accomplish, and how they differ from the questions one would ask in a wealthier or a more repressive country: they place the nation's genuine need at the very centre of the analysis, rather than treating it as an obstacle to be overcome or an excuse to be exposed. The loss is documented, yes — but so is the need that drives the decision, and the funded alternative that might meet it, because in Kiribati these are not secondary considerations but the heart of the matter. This reframing — from 'how do we stop this decision' to 'how do we meet the need behind this decision without the loss' — is the single most important move a Kiribati effort makes, because it converts a confrontation that a poor nation would win into a shared problem that the community, the Assembly, and the partners can solve together. It is the difference between opposing the people and joining them.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on — and here, where the case must persuade a community in the maneaba, an elected Assembly, and conservation partners, sound evidence of both the loss and the need is decisive. The maneaba, a member of the Assembly, and a conservation funder all move on evidence — and in Kiribati, a clear record of what would be lost, what need drives the decision, and how protection could meet that need is precisely what an effort turns into a real result, so build it well. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Decision, the Need, and the Deciders

Get the facts the case turns on. Identify the **decision, the ministry or Cabinet behind it, the reef, fishery, or reserve it affects, and the process it requires** — and, crucially here, the **need driving it**: the revenue, the development, the shortfall, as the reserve's reopening was driven by lost fishing revenue and a conservation fund that underdelivered. Establish the deciders and the driving need precisely, because in Kiribati you cannot turn a decision without honestly addressing the need behind it.

## Layer 2: The Loss — to Reefs, Fisheries, and the Future

Document what would be lost. Establish the **ecological harm** — to reefs, lagoons, tuna and fish stocks, marine mammals and birds, and the deep-sea and reef ecosystems, as the Phoenix Islands hold. And establish the **livelihood harm** — to the fishery the people depend on, to the food security of island communities, to the tourism and future the ocean sustains. Draw on **the conservation partners' science, the World Heritage documentation, and local knowledge**. And let **Banaba's devastation** stand as documented warning of what unchecked extraction can do to an island forever. Banaba's history deserves to be understood and invoked with care, because it is both a genuine warning and a delicate matter. Over decades, the phosphate of Banaba was mined until much of the island's surface was stripped away, and its people were resettled far from their home, on another island in another country, their island left barely habitable — a stark and well-documented example of how the extraction of an island's one valuable resource can destroy the island itself and displace its people permanently. Invoked honestly, this history is a powerful reminder, in a nation now weighing the exploitation of its ocean and its seabed, of what can be lost forever when a resource is taken without limit. But it must be invoked with respect for the Banaban people, whose loss is real and whose story is their own, and as a shared cautionary memory rather than a rhetorical weapon — a reminder to a proud nation, from its own history, of the permanence of some losses. Photographs, scientific data, and local testimony make the loss undeniable.

## Layer 3: The Loss, the Need, the Funded Alternative, and the Channel

Finally, document the case. Does the **ecological and livelihood harm** show a real loss? What **need** drives the decision? Could **conservation finance, sustainable fisheries, or livelihoods** meet that need while protecting the ocean — the funded alternative that is the heart of a Kiribati case? And which **channel** — the maneaba, the Assembly, the partners — is open? Each is a separate element, and together they make a case that can persuade a community and a nation that must weigh the ocean against its needs. This is the layer that most often decides these efforts, because a documented loss, an honestly-addressed need, a funded alternative, and an open channel are exactly what let a Kiribati community defend the ocean without opposing the people. Build it well, and the maneaba, the democracy, and the funded alternative become the community's strength. One practical note on building this layer: the funded alternative is the part of the file that a Kiribati effort most often neglects and most needs, because it is the part that answers the government's actual reason for the destructive decision. It is not enough to document the ecological value of a reef or a reserve, however rigorously; the file must also grapple honestly with the revenue or development need the decision serves, and set out a credible way to meet that need without the loss. This means understanding, concretely, how much revenue is at stake, what a conservation endowment or a debt-for-nature swap or a sustainable-fishery arrangement could realistically provide, and why previous funding models — like the Phoenix Islands endowment that raised only a fraction of what was hoped — fell short, so the new proposal is realistic rather than aspirational. A file that pairs a rigorous account of the loss with a credible, funded alternative speaks directly to the deciders' real dilemma, and is far more persuasive than one that documents only what would be lost.

Map which apply, because in Kiribati the maneaba and the funding partners together are the levers that can make protection serve the people.

## The People and the Community

Finally, map the human terrain. Who is affected — the island communities, the fishers, the future generations? Who holds authority — the **unimwane**, the elders, in the maneaba; the **church**, a central moral force in island life; the **Assembly members** answerable to their islands? Who abroad can help — the **conservation partners, the diaspora, the regional bodies**? A Kiribati effort is advanced through the community's own institutions — the maneaba, the elders, the church — joined to the international partners who can fund protection, so map that coalition, customary and international together, deliberately.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION

Evidence does not move deciders by itself. A community — united, informed, and speaking through its own maneaba — is what turns evidence into a legitimate collective voice, in a culture where collective decision is the very heart of governance. And here, distinctively, the community already has an ancient institution for exactly this: the maneaba, the meeting-house where the community deliberates and forms its united will. The task is not to invent opposition, but to bring the matter, properly, to the maneaba.

## Why the Community Is Decisive — and Speaks Through the Maneaba

In I-Kiribati culture, the maneaba is where the community's collective will is formed and voiced, and a decision reached there carries deep legitimacy. The community supplies what an effort runs on: the affected voices, the local and ecological knowledge, the legitimacy, and the collective authority that the maneaba embodies. A concern carried by one person is easily set aside; a concern deliberated and voiced by the maneaba, as the united will of the community, is not. **A united community here does not merely support the effort; through the maneaba, it becomes the effort's legitimate collective voice — the most authentic and powerful form opposition can take in Kiribati.** This is a genuinely distinctive feature of the Kiribati situation, and it changes the character of the whole effort. In many countries, a community must construct its opposition from scratch, building organisation and legitimacy where none existed; in Kiribati, an ancient and deeply respected institution for collective decision already exists in every community — the maneaba, where islanders have deliberated and decided together for generations. The effort's task is therefore not to invent a movement but to bring the matter properly before this institution, so that the community's response emerges through its own trusted, customary process and carries the full weight of that tradition. A position reached in the maneaba is not one group's campaign but the community's collective will, formed in the way I-Kiribati have always formed such wills — and that gives it a legitimacy, at home and before the Assembly, that no imported form of protest could match. Working through the maneaba is thus not only culturally respectful but strategically powerful.

## Bringing the Matter to the Maneaba

Begin by bringing the decision, honestly, to the **maneaba** — the meeting-house of the affected island or community. Lay out what would be lost, what need drives the decision, and what funded alternative might meet that need, so the community can deliberate as it traditionally does, weighing the ocean against the need with full knowledge. Respect the **unimwane**, the elders whose voice carries weight, and the customary process of consensus. Name the shared stake — **our reef, our fishery, our ocean, and our children's future, and how to keep them without failing our needs** — because in the maneaba the community can forge a collective position that honours both the ocean and the people. This dual honouring — of the ocean and of the people's needs — is essential to how a matter must be brought to the maneaba, because the maneaba is not a place for one-sided advocacy but for weighing the whole community's interests together. A person who comes to the meeting-house demanding conservation while ignoring the community's need for income or food will not carry the maneaba; the community lives with those needs daily and will not set them aside. But a person who lays out honestly both what would be lost and how the need behind the decision might still be met — through a funded alternative, a sustainable fishery, a livelihood — invites the maneaba to do what it does best: to find, through collective deliberation, a path that serves the whole community. The maneaba is far more likely to unite behind protection that also feeds the people than behind conservation that seems to ignore them, so the matter must always be brought as a question of holding both, never of choosing the ocean over the community's survival.

## Coalition-Building Through Custom and Partnership

A community allied is stronger than a community alone, and in Kiribati the coalition joins custom to partnership. Reach to the **elders and the maneaba** of neighbouring communities; the **church**, whose moral voice carries far in island life; the **Assembly members** answerable to the islands; and — decisively for the funded alternative — the **conservation partners, World Heritage bodies, and regional organisations** who can make protection pay. Frame the coalition around a shared defence of the ocean that also serves the people, uniting custom, faith, democracy, and international partnership. Breadth here means joining the community's own institutions to the partners who can fund protection.

## Sustaining the Effort

Kiribati efforts can be sustained through the community's own institutions and the rhythms of island life. Keep the community **informed and united through the maneaba**; mark steps — a maneaba resolution, a matter raised in the Assembly, a conservation partner engaged, a funded alternative advanced. And keep the effort grounded in the community's needs, not against them, because an effort that pits conservation against the people's survival will lose the maneaba itself. The effort that lasts is the one that stays united through the maneaba, joined to the partners who can fund protection, and always honest about meeting the people's needs. Sustaining such an effort is helped, in Kiribati, by the very rhythms of island and maneaba life, in which the community regularly gathers and deliberates, so that a matter can be kept before the community over time through its ordinary customary processes rather than requiring some separate campaign to be maintained. But it is also challenged by the smallness and remoteness of island communities and the constant pressure of real need, which can wear down resolve when protection seems to cost more than the community can bear. The answer is to keep the funded alternative always at the centre — to be able to show, at each gathering, progress toward making protection something the community can actually afford — because a community sustained by the visible prospect of protection that serves its needs will endure, while one asked to sacrifice indefinitely for an unfunded conservation will not.

# STEP 4: LEGAL, CUSTOMARY & INTERNATIONAL CHALLENGES — THE MANEABA, THE ASSEMBLY, AND THE WORLD HERITAGE SYSTEM

This is where a Kiribati effort is realistically advanced — not through confrontation, but through the **maneaba's customary authority, the democratic Assembly, and the international World Heritage and conservation systems**, all bent toward making protection serve the people. Here the customary, legal, and international levers come together, because in Kiribati the community's own institutions and the international partners are, together, how the ocean is defended.

## Setting Expectations Honestly

First, the honest truth. The levers are **real but bounded by the nation's need and sovereignty**. The maneaba carries **deep legitimacy but not legal veto** over national decisions; the Assembly is **democratic but weighs revenue heavily**; and the World Heritage and conservation systems can **recognise, fund, and pressure but not override a sovereign choice** — and imposed conservation is resisted. What these levers realistically achieve is **a legitimate collective voice, a measure questioned or reshaped, protection funded, and recognition defended** — not usually the simple overriding of a decision driven by real need. Enter this front clear-eyed: the maneaba, the Assembly, and the international systems are Kiribati's distinctive levers, strongest when protection is **made to serve the people** — but the need is real, and unfunded conservation is often reversed.

## The Maneaba and Customary Authority

The first and most distinctive front is the maneaba. The **meeting-house and its collective deliberation** are the customary heart of I-Kiribati decision, and a community's united position, formed in the maneaba and voiced by its elders, carries a legitimacy that national deciders cannot lightly ignore. Bring the matter to the maneaba; forge a collective position that honours both the ocean and the people's needs; and carry that united voice to the Assembly and the government. This customary authority is the community's most authentic and powerful lever, because it speaks with the whole community's legitimacy. It is worth being clear about both the strength and the limit of this lever, honestly. Its strength is legitimacy: a position reached in the maneaba is the authentic collective will of the community, and no national decider can dismiss it as the agitation of a fringe, because it is the community itself speaking through its most trusted institution. Its limit is authority: the maneaba's legitimacy is moral and customary, not a legal veto over national decisions, and a determined government weighing national revenue can proceed despite a community's maneaba even as it takes a political cost for doing so. Understanding both is essential — the maneaba gives a community a voice of unmatched authenticity and weight, but that voice persuades and pressures rather than compels, which is precisely why it must be joined to the Assembly's decision-making power and the partners' funding to become fully effective.

## The Democratic Assembly

The second front is the democracy. The **Maneaba ni Maungatabu**, the elected national parliament, must approve major measures — as the reserve's reopening required — and its members answer to their island communities. Bring the community's maneaba-formed position to **sympathetic members**; press for the decision to be debated, questioned, and reshaped; and, crucially, offer the **funded alternative** that lets members serve both conservation and their constituents' needs. The Assembly weighs revenue heavily, so the argument that wins there is not conservation against need, but protection that meets the need. This is worth stating as plainly as possible, because it is where many well-meaning efforts fail: an Assembly member from a poor island constituency cannot vote to forgo revenue their constituents depend on merely because a reef or a reserve is ecologically precious, however genuinely they may value it. To ask them to do so is to ask them to fail the very people who elected them, and they will not, and should not. But to offer them a funded alternative — a way to protect the ocean while still delivering to their constituents the revenue or the livelihoods they need — is to give them a vote they can actually cast, one that serves both their islands and the sea. The entire art of moving the Assembly in Kiribati lies in this: never forcing a choice between the ocean and the people, but always supplying the funded path that lets a member choose both.

## The World Heritage and Conservation Systems

The third front is international. **UNESCO's World Heritage status** — which the Phoenix Islands hold, and which their reopening puts at risk — is a lever of international recognition and pressure, and its loss is a real cost a nation may wish to avoid. The **international conservation partners and funds** can, decisively, make protection pay — through endowments, debt-for-nature swaps, blue-economy finance, and payments for protection — meeting the very need that drives destruction. And the **regional and fisheries bodies** give collective strength over the tuna. Pursued together — the maneaba's legitimacy, the Assembly's decision, and the international partners' funding — these fronts are how a Kiribati community defends the ocean by making its protection serve, rather than oppose, the people. It is worth understanding why these three fronts must work together, because each supplies what the others lack. The maneaba supplies legitimacy — the authentic, collective voice of the community — but it cannot fund protection or bind a national decision. The Assembly supplies the legal decision — it can approve, reshape, or refuse a measure — but it weighs revenue heavily and answers to a nation's needs. And the international partners supply the funding — the conservation finance that can make protection affordable — but they cannot, and must not, override a sovereign choice. Woven together — a community united in the maneaba, an Assembly offered a funded way to serve both its constituents and the ocean, and partners ready to pay for protection — they form a combination that can defend the ocean without asking a poor nation to choose between the sea and its survival. The failure of the Phoenix Islands protection was, in a sense, a failure of this combination: the conservation was real and the legitimacy was there, but the funding fell short, and so protection could not be sustained. A Kiribati effort must get all three right.

But the maneaba still gathers, the democracy still answers to the islands, the world still values these oceans, and partners still exist who can make protection pay — and the lesson of these losses is not despair but direction: that in a poor and sovereign nation, the ocean is defended not by lecturing the people but by making its protection serve them. There is no easy victory to promise, and no community precedent of triumph to inherit; this guide has said so throughout. But your reefs, your fisheries, and your ocean are worth defending, your maneaba is a genuine and legitimate power, your democracy answers to your islands, and partners stand ready to help make protection affordable. The way here is not to set conservation against your people, but to make them one cause — and that is a fight worth waging, and one your community, through its own maneaba, can lead. So can you. And the way to lead it is close at hand, in an institution your community already trusts and has trusted for generations. Gather in the maneaba, lay the loss and the need honestly before your people, and let them do what they have always done — deliberate together and forge a united will; then carry that will to your Assembly and to the partners who can help make protecting your ocean something your nation can afford. That is not a borrowed method or an imported protest, but your own way of deciding, turned to the defence of your reefs and your fisheries and your sea. It has not yet won back the Phoenix Islands, and this guide has not pretended otherwise; but it is a real and living power, and joined to the funding that makes protection affordable, it is how your ocean can be held for your children without failing your people today.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN SOMALIA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Concern for the environment in Somalia must be pursued with great care and largely through the international, the diaspora, and the community, because this is a fragile, conflict-affected state where an armed insurgency contests the countryside, enforcement is weak, and environmental work carries real danger. Somalia is a Horn of Africa nation with one of the continent's longest coastlines and a semi-arid interior of acacia woodland and rangeland, and its environmental story is defined by three interlocking harms of a kind found almost nowhere else in this series. The first is the **charcoal trade**: the felling of the acacia woodlands for charcoal, the main driver of a deepening deforestation and desertification, and — notoriously — a taxed source of revenue for the armed insurgency, banned by the United Nations yet continuing still. The second is the **plundering of the sea**: the foreign fleets that have illegally fished one of Africa's longest coastlines, and the hazardous and toxic waste dumped off it in the vacuum left when the state collapsed — the very injuries that once drove coastal fishing communities to desperation and toward piracy. The third is the **drought**: the recurrent, deepening droughts and famines that degrade the land, ruin the pastoralist livelihoods, and drive desperate people into the very charcoal trade that worsens them. The levers here are largely international, and, used with care, are real.

In a fragile, conflict-affected state, the tools are largely international and community, used with great care and protecting anyone inside absolutely — and they are real. The distinctive lever is the **charcoal trade's UN ban and the foreign markets that buy it**: the trade is banned by the world and reaches identifiable foreign markets that can be pressed to enforce the ban, cutting the demand that drives the felling — and the revenue that funds the insurgency. The **illegal foreign fishing and the toxic dumping** are foreign harms the **international maritime and anti-illegal-fishing frameworks**, and the fleets' own home and flag states, can reach. The **coast and its mangroves** can be defended, where it is safe, through conservation and community management. And the **international bodies, the diaspora, and the rebuilding state** offer real channels — the frameworks and donors, the Somali diaspora that documents and advocates from outside, and the federal government where it can act. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The charcoal trade is bound up with the conflict, so confronting it is dangerous and cannot be done by enforcement alone — and the people who cut and burn the wood do so in desperation, so they must never be blamed or punished, only offered alternatives. The plundering of the sea is foreign-driven and exploits a state that cannot yet defend its own waters. The drought is severe and climate-driven, beyond Somalia's power. The political space is fragile and parts of the country are contested and perilous, so the work must run through international, diaspora, and community channels and protect anyone inside absolutely. And progress is slow and partial. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — the charcoal ban enforced at the foreign markets; the illegal fishing and dumping pressed through the maritime frameworks and the fleets' home states; the coast defended where safe; the international bodies and diaspora engaged — with great care, never punishing the poor, and protecting everyone inside. Above all, protect the people.

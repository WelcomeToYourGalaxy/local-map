# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN SLOVENIA

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community effort in Slovenia once proved its power at the ballot box. In March 2021 the government pushed amendments to the **Waters Act** through parliament in a shortened procedure, with little public consultation. On paper the changes were technical; in effect, critics warned, they would loosen the rules that kept building away from the water's edge and open Slovenia's rivers, lakes, and short, precious Adriatic coast to hotels, malls, and private development — threatening public access and the drinking water beneath. Slovenians did not accept it. Environmental and civil-society groups — Eko Krog, Balkan River Defence, and others — formed a **"Movement for Drinkable Water,"** and, using a right written into their constitution, collected **52,230 signatures**, well past the forty thousand needed, to force a national referendum to strike the law down. On **11 July 2021, in one of the highest turnouts in years, 86.6 percent of voters said no.** The amendments were rejected — a government that had just assumed the presidency of the European Union handed a decisive defeat by its own citizens, defending their water. As one campaigner put it, it was "the awakening of civil society."

A community in Slovenia has, distinctively, two constitutional levers few countries possess, plus the full apparatus of an EU democracy. The first distinctive lever is the **constitutional right to drinking water**: Article 70a, added in 2016 — the first in the European Union — declares water a public good and not a commodity, a powerful legal and moral lever against any project that threatens it. The second is the **rejection referendum**: citizens can gather forty thousand signatures to force a national vote to strike down a law parliament has passed — the very tool that defeated the water amendments. Beyond these, as an EU member, Slovenia is bound by **EU environmental law** — the environmental-assessment, habitats, and water-framework directives, enforceable through the European Commission and the Court of Justice over a national government's head. Major projects must reckon with **environmental impact assessment and the planning and water-protection regulation**; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. And the **courts** — including a Constitutional Court — and the **open democratic and civil-society channels** genuinely work. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The rejection referendum is precise and demanding: forty thousand signatures force the vote, but the law falls only if a majority vote against it and at least a fifth of the whole electorate does so — a high bar that not every fight can clear. The water victory combined a threat to the water with a process that cut the public out, a combination that galvanised a nation; not every project stirs a national referendum. An ordinary development without that galvanising force relies on the ordinary tools of assessment, EU law, and the courts. And the constitutional water right is most powerful where water itself is threatened. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a water-threatening law or project struck down or blocked, as the water amendments were; an ordinary project assessed, challenged under EU law, or refused — through the constitutional water right, the rejection referendum where a fight can galvanise one, the EU law, the assessment, and the courts, with the mobilised public and civil society behind them.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

A Slovenian decision runs through an EU democracy with two extra constitutional tools. The **government, National Assembly, and their environment and water bodies** decide the laws, permits, and assessments. The **citizens** can force a rejection referendum. The **Constitutional Court and the courts** enforce the constitution — including the right to water — and the law. And the **European Commission and Court of Justice** enforce EU environmental law over the government's head.

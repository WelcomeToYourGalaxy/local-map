# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN HUNGARY

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action decides project outcomes in Hungary, and one river movement proved it against a communist state at the height of its power. In 1984, when open political dissent was dangerous, a biologist named János Vargha founded the **Danube Circle** — Duna Kör — to oppose the **Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros** dam, a Soviet-era mega-project that would have altered two hundred kilometres of the Danube, flooded islands and forests, drowned the **Danube Bend**, threatened Budapest's drinking water, and dried Europe's last inland delta. The government backed it; a treaty bound it. But Hungarians would not lose their river. The Danube Circle began with **samizdat** — underground pamphlets — and the authority of the **Hungarian Academy of Sciences**, grew through petitions and an international conference, and by 1988 had put a hundred and fifty thousand signatures and tens of thousands of demonstrators onto the streets of Budapest, protests on a scale not seen since 1956. In **May 1989**, the government gave way and halted the Nagymaros dam; its democratic successors abandoned it. A movement that began as a handful of scientists passing around pamphlets had stopped a mega-project the whole communist state wanted — and helped open the door to democracy itself. The scale of what they faced is worth setting down. The dam complex, agreed by a 1977 treaty between Hungary and Czechoslovakia, was a three-billion-dollar scheme of two great dams and their power plants that would have reworked some two hundred kilometres of the Danube, flooded dozens of islands, and destroyed well over a hundred square kilometres of forest and field. Against that stood, at first, a single biologist from the Academy of Sciences and a few colleagues with a duplicating machine; by 1988 it was a petition of a hundred and fifty thousand names and marches through Budapest, and by the spring of 1989 it was a parliament that no longer dared proceed. That arc — from pamphlet to petition to a state’s retreat — is the Hungarian lesson in miniature.

The Danube Circle story teaches two things about winning in Hungary — including its hard limits, which this guide will not hide. The first is the pattern: not a single silver bullet, but the combination of every lever — the affected communities, the independent science, the coalition, the mass mobilisation, the law, and the world's attention — pressed together until the state gave way. The second is distinctly Hungarian, and it has a new and urgent relevance. **The environmental cause can be the space where civic resistance survives and grows when open political opposition is constrained** — the Danube Circle mobilised where direct dissent was dangerous, and became a force that outgrew the single fight. This is worth grasping because it is not merely a historical curiosity but a live strategic insight: under a regime that punished open political opposition, the defence of a river was something people could rally to with less immediate danger, and yet the skills, the networks, and the confidence built in that defence flowed directly into the broader civic awakening of 1989. Where politics is closed, a concrete environmental threat — to a river, a water supply, a loved landscape — can be the ground on which a public rediscovers that it can act together and win, and that discovery rarely stays confined to the single issue. That, together with the **authority of independent science**, **mass petitions and the demand for a say**, the protection of **shared resources of national identity** like the Danube and its water, and — today — the **legal leverage of European Union and international environmental law** against a domestic government, gives communities here a real and distinctive power.

But the same story carries honest warnings, and this guide states them plainly. The Danube Circle **did not save the whole river**: Nagymaros was stopped, but neighbouring Czechoslovakia completed the **Gabčíkovo** dam and later diverted the Danube unilaterally, damaging the Szigetköz delta, and the dispute dragged on for years at the International Court of Justice. The aftermath is a sobering part of the lesson: Hungary’s neighbour finished its own dam and, in 1992, unilaterally diverted a long stretch of the shared Danube into a canal, taking the water and the power and drying out much of the Szigetköz wetland on the Hungarian side, and the two states ended up before the World Court, which found fault on both sides and sent them back to negotiate. So the victory a community should study is a real but partial one: a mega-dam on Hungarian soil was stopped for good, but the river was not wholly saved, and the campaign’s success and its limits must be learned together. The 1989 victory came at a **unique historical moment** — the collapse of communism, the thaw of perestroika — harder to repeat in ordinary times. And today's Hungary, though a European Union member with real legal tools, is a country where **the independence of courts and media is under strain** and civic space is more constrained than the transitional moment of 1989, so domestic institutions cannot always be relied on. This guide is written with both truths in view: the real, distinctive power of an environmental movement that can carry civic resistance and draw on European law, and the unfinished victory, the unique moment, and the strained institutions that qualify it.

Most communities do not know HOW to make opposition effective. They discover a project already decided by the government, assume "the powerful have decided and nothing can be done," and never build the independent science, never petition, never demand a say, never invoke European law, never build the coalition. They generate private despair without a public campaign. The gap between a community that stops a project and one that watches it built is rarely courage — Hungarians filled the streets for the Danube. It is method: knowing that even against a powerful state, an environmental movement armed with science, numbers, the law, and a river the nation loves can prevail.

**This guide shows what actually works — inside the Hungarian system exactly as it is today.**

## The Strategic Framework

Effective opposition campaigns follow the same underlying pattern, whichever project they face:

```
STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION  (find the decision, the science, and the weak point)
        |
STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION          (build proof of harm with independent expertise)
        |
STEP 3: LOCAL OPPOSITION        (build the coalition and the movement)
        |
STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES        (EU and Aarhus law, the courts, and participation)
        |
STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY         (make it national and international)
```

These steps operate **simultaneously, not in sequence**. Documentation feeds the legal challenge and the public argument at once; the coalition sustains the mobilisation; a petition or a lawsuit is a headline and a headline builds the movement. The community that runs all five together, each reinforcing the others, is the one that wins. The Danube Circle did exactly this — it built the science, documented the harm, organised a coalition, mobilised the public, invoked the law, and carried the fight to the world, until the state gave way.

## The Levers That Are Distinctly Strong Here

Every country's system has its own pressure points. Hungary's give communities a distinctive set:

- **Environmentalism as civic resistance.** The environmental cause can mobilise and unite where open political dissent is constrained, as the Danube Circle did — and can grow into a broader civic force.
- **The authority of independent science.** The **Hungarian Academy of Sciences** and independent experts carry a credibility that is hard for a government to dismiss, and it anchored the Danube fight. The role of the Academy in the Danube case is instructive: when a respected scientific body publicly detailed the environmental damage the project would do, it was, in the words of one account, the opposition most troublesome to the regime, precisely because it could not be waved away as mere agitation. Independent scientific authority converts a protest into a documented, expert warning that a government must answer on the merits, and a Hungarian campaign that secures such authority early gives everything that follows a foundation the state finds hard to attack.
- **Mass petitions and the demand for a say.** Large petitions and the call for a **referendum or genuine consultation**, as at Nagymaros, put public will on the record and pressure the state.
- **European and international law.** As an EU member, Hungary is bound by **EU environmental law and the Aarhus Convention** — access to information, participation, and justice — levers against a domestic government that domestic institutions may not provide. This is a lever the Danube Circle did not have and that today’s campaigners do: Hungary’s accession to the European Union bound it to a body of environmental law — on impact assessment, on protected habitats and birds — and to the Aarhus Convention’s guarantees of public rights, all enforceable not only in Hungarian courts but through the European Commission and the Court of Justice of the European Union. Where the domestic system is strained, this external law is the single most important addition to the toolkit the Danube Circle built, because it does not depend on the goodwill of the domestic government.
- **Shared resources of national identity.** The Danube, its water, its landscapes are treasured across politics, so a threat to them unites the public. This is a strategic asset a Hungarian campaign should use deliberately: a river like the Danube, or a supply of drinking water, or a landscape woven into the national story, is loved across every political division, so a fight framed around such a resource can draw support from people who agree on little else. The Danube Bend was not a partisan cause but a national one, and a campaign that keeps its fight anchored to a shared, non-partisan resource is far harder for a government to dismiss as the agenda of one faction.

## What This Guide Will Not Pretend

It would be dishonest to promise these levers always deliver. The Danube Circle **saved one dam but not the whole river**; Gabčíkovo was built and the Danube diverted. The 1989 win came in a **unique moment**. And today the **independence of courts and media is strained**, so domestic remedies can be unreliable, and the European lever is **slow and politically fraught**. This guide takes those limits seriously, names them plainly, and shows you how to fight anyway — building the science, organising the movement, mobilising the public, and invoking European law, as the Danube Circle built the tradition you now inherit.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

You cannot pull a lever you cannot see. Before you spend a single weekend, map who actually decides your project's fate, at which level, and where along that path the decision is weakest.

## The Levels of Decision

A Hungarian development project runs through several tracks at once, and each is a place to intervene.

**The government and the permit.** The **government and its ministries** approve projects and grant permits, based on an **environmental impact assessment**. This is often the proponent, and, where institutions are captured, not an ally — but the assessment is a vulnerability.

**The courts.** Hungarian **courts** can review permits, though their independence is under strain, so treat them as a real but not always reliable lever.

**The European level.** As an EU member, Hungary is bound by **EU environmental law** — the environmental-assessment, habitats, and birds directives — and the **Aarhus Convention**, enforceable through the European institutions and, ultimately, the **Court of Justice of the EU**. This is a distinct and powerful lever against a domestic government. It matters because it changes who the ultimate decision-maker is: a permit that a domestic authority grants and a strained domestic court upholds can still be found unlawful in Luxembourg, and a government that ignores a domestic objection cannot so easily ignore an infringement action brought by the European Commission. For a Hungarian community, this means the fight is not confined to a domestic arena the government may control; there is an arena above it, governed by law the government agreed to be bound by, in which the odds are different.

**The public and the movement.** A **mobilised public and an organised movement** — petitions, demonstrations, the demand for a say — are the force that moved the state at Nagymaros and can again.

**The science.** **Independent experts and academies** carry an authority that anchors every other lever.

## The Journey of a Project

A typical project moves from a government decision and a **permit** based on an **assessment**, through the **courts** if challenged, and past the **European institutions** if EU law is breached, toward construction — with the **public, the movement, and independent science** able to document, mobilise, and litigate at any point. **Every one of those stages is a door you can put your foot in.** The distinctive Hungarian doors are the **movement and the science**, which moved a communist state, and — today — the **European legal lever**, which reaches past a strained domestic system. Find out exactly where your project stands, and, crucially, whether it **breaches EU or Aarhus law**, because that is often the reachable lever.

## Follow the Project, the Science, and the Law

Behind the project stand a proponent — the government, an authority, or a connected company — and the officials who approved it. Trace who profits, who decided, and — critically in Hungary — **what independent science says about the harm**, **whether the assessment and the process breached EU or Aarhus law**, and **whether the public was genuinely consulted**. Two questions unlock most Hungarian fights: **What does independent science show, and did the process break EU or Aarhus law?** and **Can a movement be built around a threat the nation feels?** The science-and-law and the movement are, together, the heart of most Hungarian campaigns. The two reinforce each other in a specific way: the science and the European-law breach give the campaign its formal weapons — the grounds for a court, a complaint, an infringement action — while the movement gives it the public pressure that makes those weapons bite and that a government feels, so that a campaign strong in one but not the other is only half-armed. The Danube Circle had both, the Academy’s science and the streets’ numbers, and a modern Hungarian campaign should build both, adding the European law the Circle lacked.

## The Overseers

Finally, know the bodies that watch the deciders, because they are levers too. The **courts**, strained but real; the **European Commission and the Court of Justice of the EU**, which enforce EU environmental and access-to-justice law; the **Aarhus Convention's compliance mechanism**; the **independent scientists and academies**; the **independent media and NGOs** that survive; and the **mobilised public**, the ultimate overseer, which moved the state at Nagymaros. In Hungary, the **European legal lever and the mobilised movement** are the two most distinctive and decisive, because they reach where a strained domestic system may not — one through law from outside, the other through numbers from below. Each is a separate front, and a project that independent science condemns, that breaches European law, and that faces a roused public rarely survives pressure on several at once. You do not have to choose one front: the same well-built file — the independent science, the European-law breach, the denied participation, the threatened river — can go to the domestic courts, the European Commission, the Aarhus mechanism, the press, and the public at once, and each front that opens strengthens the others. A Hungarian campaign’s power comes not from any single lever but from pressing several together, with the European law above the strained domestic system and the mobilised movement below it as the two that reach furthest.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective campaign begins by finding the exact decision that governs the project — and its weakest point, which in Hungary is often the science or the breach of European law. Vague opposition goes nowhere; opposition to "the dam that independent science says would drown the Danube Bend and threaten Budapest's water, approved on an assessment that breaches EU law and without a genuine public say" is a case — the case the Danube Circle made. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What does independent science say?** This is the first Hungarian question, because the **authority of independent experts and academies** — as the Hungarian Academy of Sciences anchored the Danube fight — gives a campaign a credible, hard-to-dismiss case for harm. Establish what the science shows. Do this first and thoroughly, because in Hungary the scientific case is not one argument among many but the foundation the whole campaign rests on: it is what gives the movement its credibility, what a court and the European institutions weigh, and what a government finds hardest to dismiss. A campaign that leads with independent science, rather than with assertion or emotion, inherits the mantle of the Danube Circle, whose greatest early asset was precisely the authority of the scientists who warned of the harm.

**2. Does it breach European or Aarhus law?** Was the **environmental assessment** deficient, the **public participation** inadequate, the process contrary to **EU directives or the Aarhus Convention**? This is the lever that reaches past a strained domestic system. It is worth treating this question as seriously as the science, because a breach of European or Aarhus law is often the most powerful single fact a Hungarian campaign can establish: it opens a route — the Commission, the Court of Justice, the Aarhus mechanism — that lies outside the domestic government’s control, and it converts a matter of policy, on which the government may claim the last word, into a matter of law, on which it may not. Examine the assessment and the participation process against the directives and the Convention with particular care, because a defect there can be decisive.

**3. Can a movement be built around it?** Does the project threaten something the **public feels** — a river, a landscape, drinking water, a place of national identity — around which a movement can be mobilised, as the Danube Bend mobilised Hungary?

**4. Was the public genuinely consulted?** Was there a real chance to participate, or the demand for a **referendum or consultation** denied — a ground under Aarhus and a rallying cause, as at Nagymaros?

**5. Who decides, who benefits, and who is watching?** Which authority or company is behind it, who profits; which communities, rivers, and landscapes are affected; and which scientists, lawyers, European institutions, media, NGOs, and public can be engaged. This maps your levers and your audiences at once.

## A Worked Example

Suppose the government approves a dam or development that independent science condemns, on a deficient assessment and without genuine consultation, threatening a river the nation loves.

Working the five questions: what does **independent science** say? Does the assessment **breach EU or Aarhus law**? Can a **movement** be built around the threatened river? Was the **public consulted**, or denied a say? And the deciders are the government and the proponent, with the courts, the European institutions, the scientists, the media, and the public as the pressure points.

Within a season you have moved from "we oppose the dam" to a target list: build the independent science; challenge the assessment under EU and Aarhus law; organise the movement; mobilise the public and demand a say; and make it a national and European story. That is a campaign, not a complaint — and it draws directly on how the Danube Circle won. Notice what the exercise did: it converted a diffuse despair into a short list of concrete tasks — build the science, challenge the assessment under European law, organise the movement, demand a say — each tied to a specific lever and each of which can begin now. That is the difference the five questions make, and in Hungary the first two, what independent science shows and whether the process broke European or Aarhus law, are the ones that most often decide where the fight can be won past a strained domestic system.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on. A court, the European institutions, a journalist, and the public all move on evidence, not indignation — and the Danube Circle's power began with the science of the Hungarian Academy. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Permit, the Assessment, and the Law

Get the documents the decision rests on. Obtain the **permit, the environmental impact assessment, and the consultation record**, and scrutinise them, because a **deficient assessment or inadequate public participation** breaches EU and Aarhus law and is a ground a court — domestic or European — can act on. Establish whether the **EU directives and the Aarhus Convention** were followed. Use Hungary's freedom-of-information and environmental-access rights, backed by Aarhus. The gap between what the paper claims — a sound, lawful, consulted project — and what the science and the law show is where cases are won.

## Layer 2: The Independent Science

Document the reality the paper understates, and put **independent science** at the centre, because in Hungary it carries an authority that anchors everything. Commission or gather **independent expert assessment** — from academics, the Academy's tradition, scientific bodies — of the harm to the **river, the water, the landscape, the ecosystem**, as the Danube Circle marshalled the science of the harm to the Danube. The scientific case should cover not only the direct destruction — the flooded land, the felled forest — but the systemic effects that are easy to overlook and hard to reverse: the changes to groundwater and drinking-water supplies, the disruption of a river’s flow and sediment, the loss of a wetland’s function, the effect on fish and the wider ecosystem. It was precisely the argument that the Danube scheme would alter the physical, chemical, and biological conditions of a two-hundred-kilometre stretch of river and its groundwater that made the scientific case against it so formidable, and a modern campaign should aim for that same systemic depth. Document the threat to **drinking water and shared resources**, which unites the public. Photograph and date the affected place. Record **testimony** from communities and those who know it. The threat to drinking water deserves particular emphasis, because it was central to the Danube case and it unites a public across every political line: a dam or a diversion that endangers the aquifer supplying a city’s water is not an abstract ecological concern but a direct threat to millions of people, and framing the harm in those terms — our water, not only our wildlife — broadens the movement far beyond those who would turn out for nature alone. Document the water threat rigorously, because it is often the argument that turns a local cause into a national one.

## Layer 3: The Harm, the Breach, and the Denied Say

Finally, document the defects. Does **independent science condemn** the project? Does the assessment or process **breach EU or Aarhus law** — a deficient assessment, inadequate participation? Was the **public denied a genuine say** or a referendum? Each is a separate ground — for the European complaint, the court challenge, the demand for participation, and the public campaign — and together they make a case that is hard to wave away. This is the layer that most often decides Hungarian fights, because here a project sold as sound and lawful is revealed to be condemned by independent science, to breach European law, and to have denied the public its say — exactly the case the Danube Circle made against Nagymaros. The strength of assembling all three grounds together — the scientific condemnation, the European-law breach, the denied say — is that they reach different deciders and reinforce one another: the science persuades the public and the courts, the legal breach opens the European route past a strained domestic system, and the denied participation is at once a legal ground and a rallying cry. A community that documents only one of the three leaves levers unused; one that documents all three, as the Danube fight in effect did, arms every front of the campaign from a single body of evidence.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

This is the practical companion to Step 2: the specific records that decide Hungarian cases, and where to obtain them. You will not need every item; you will need the few that establish the harm, the breach of European law, and the denied say.

## The Permit, the Assessment, and the Paper Trail

- **The permit and approvals** — how the project was authorised, and by whom.
- **The environmental impact assessment** — its quality; a deficient assessment breaches EU law and is a ground for challenge.
- **The consultation record** — whether genuine public participation was offered, as EU and Aarhus law require; its absence is a ground.
- **The proponent** — the authority or company, its owners and connections.
- **The EU and Aarhus position** — how the process measures against the directives and the Convention.

## How to Get Them

Permit and assessment records come through the authorities and Hungary's freedom-of-information and environmental-access rights, which the Aarhus Convention reinforces; the consultation record through the same. Where domestic access is obstructed, the Aarhus right of access to environmental information is itself a lever. Lean on **allies**: Hungary's environmental NGOs and public-interest lawyers, the independent scientists and the Academy's tradition, and the European networks that pursue EU-law and Aarhus cases. Move early: challenging the assessment and the participation before construction, and filing complaints while the process is live, carry more weight than the same steps taken after the concrete is poured — and legal and complaint windows are defined. Pay close attention to those windows, because much of the European lever’s power depends on acting in time: a challenge to a permit, a complaint to the Commission, a communication under Aarhus, each has its own timing, and a campaign that lets a deadline pass may forfeit its strongest route while the weaker domestic one remains. Map the deadlines at the outset, alongside the project’s own timeline, so that the campaign moves on the European fronts while they are open rather than discovering too late that a window has closed.

## The Ground Evidence

- **The independent science** — the harm to the river, water, landscape, and ecosystem, from academics and scientific bodies, as the Danube Circle marshalled.
- **The threat to shared resources** — drinking water, a treasured river or landscape, that unites the public.
- **The harm evidence** — what the project would destroy, dated and photographed.
- **The community and identity evidence** — how the place matters to those who live by it and to the nation.
- **Testimony** — from residents, experts, and those who know the place.

## Organise It So It Persuades

Keep a **master timeline** of the permit, the assessment, the consultation, and every legal and complaint step — the spine of any challenge. Keep a **document index** so any fact can be sourced in seconds. And keep an **argument bank** (developed in a later section) that pairs each fact with what it proves, to whom. A well-ordered file is not bureaucracy; it is what lets a resident, a scientist, a lawyer, a journalist, a European official, and a judge all see the same clear picture fast, and it guards against the campaign's own worst enemy, a claim that cannot be sourced when the authority pushes back. In a country where independent science and European law can overcome a strained domestic system, the community with the better-kept file — above all the clearest science and the clearest breach of EU or Aarhus law — is very often the one that wins.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION — HOW TO BUILD THE COALITION AND THE MOVEMENT

Evidence and law do not stop projects on their own. Organised, mobilised people do — and in Hungary, the Danube Circle proved that an environmental movement can move even a powerful state. A Hungarian campaign's strength is the movement it builds: broad, science-anchored, and able to carry civic energy that reaches beyond the single fight.

## Phase 1: The Core and the Cause

Start with a small, committed core — five to fifteen people who will carry the work — and build the campaign around the **river, water, or landscape** at stake, because a threat the nation feels mobilises the public, as the Danube Bend did. Assign roles: science liaison, legal and European liaison, documentation, media, community and mobilisation, and coordination. Agree on the goal — the project stopped, the permit struck, the river saved — and on what you will and will not accept.

## Phase 2: The Broad Coalition

Then widen deliberately, as the Danube Circle did, into a broad coalition. Bring in the **affected communities**; the **independent scientists and the Academy's tradition**, whose authority anchors the case; the **environmental NGOs and public-interest lawyers** who pursue EU and Aarhus cases; the **university and student groups, professional bodies, and civic groups** that joined the Danube fight; and the **wider public**, for whom a treasured river is a national cause across politics. Reach the **European networks and the international press**, whose attention and law reach past a strained domestic system. The Danube Circle drew its power from exactly this breadth — scientists, students, professionals, civic groups, and the wider public as one — which is precisely the spread a winning Hungarian campaign needs. The Danube Circle’s coalition is a model worth copying deliberately: it linked the founding environmentalists with university green groups, a nature-conservation club, youth and peace movements, and professional and scientific bodies, so that the campaign spoke at once with the authority of scientists, the energy of students, and the numbers of the wider public. A campaign that stays a narrow single-issue group is easy to isolate; one that builds such a broad coalition, drawing in every constituency that has reason to care about the river, becomes a movement the state must reckon with.

**A worked coalition example.** Facing a dam on a treasured river, the affected communities and the scientists form the base and the case; the NGOs and lawyers pursue the EU and Aarhus challenge; the students, professionals, and civic groups swell the movement; and the wider public and the European networks carry the fight. No single group could have carried it; together they cover the science, the law, the numbers, and Europe — the model the Danube Circle built.

## Phase 3: Sustaining It

Campaigns die of burnout, not defeat. Meet on a rhythm. Give people concrete, finishable tasks. Mark small wins — a scientific report, a petition milestone, a complaint filed, a wave of coverage, a strong demonstration. Keep the science and the cause at the centre, and the coalition broad and cross-political, since a Hungarian environmental movement's power is its breadth and its capacity to unite people the government would prefer divided. And guard against the pressure and division a proponent or a strained state may apply. Expect attempts to split the coalition — to peel off the moderate from the militant, the local from the national, the scientist wary of politics from the activist — and to pressure the more vulnerable members, and plan against them by keeping the coalition’s shared, concrete goal in front of everyone and by not letting the campaign be baited into the terms the government would prefer. A movement that stays united around a river and a clear demand is far more resilient than one that lets itself be drawn onto contested political ground where it can be divided and dismissed.

## Phase 4: Mobilising the Public

Here is Hungary's distinctive strength. Numbers move the state — the Danube Circle put a hundred and fifty thousand signatures and tens of thousands of demonstrators onto the streets, and the government gave way. Gather **petitions**, demand a **referendum or genuine consultation**, and turn people out for peaceful, well-run demonstrations, as the Danube Circle did. Let the **scientists and respected voices** speak, so the movement carries authority as well as numbers. The point is not spectacle; it is to demonstrate — to the government, the courts, Europe, and the world — that the public stands united for its river, in numbers a state ignores at its peril, as the public stood for the Danube until Nagymaros was halted. And know that, as at Nagymaros, an environmental movement can carry a civic energy that outlasts the single fight.

# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES — EU AND AARHUS LAW, THE COURTS, AND PARTICIPATION

Hungarian domestic law offers real tools, but their reliability is strained; the **European Union and Aarhus frameworks** are the distinctive and often decisive levers, reaching past a domestic system against a domestic government. You do not need a domestic victory to win: an EU or Aarhus challenge, a European complaint, and the demand for participation can strike a permit, reopen a process, and halt a project. Engage public-interest lawyers early — but understand the tools.

## EU Environmental Law

The first lever is **European Union environmental law**. Hungary is bound by the EU's **environmental-assessment, habitats, and birds directives**, and a project approved on a **deficient assessment** or in a **protected habitat** without proper process breaches EU law — a ground a Hungarian court must apply, and a matter the **European Commission** can pursue and the **Court of Justice of the EU** can decide. This is a powerful lever precisely because it binds the domestic government to standards enforced from outside. In practice, the strongest EU-law grounds are usually procedural and evidential: that the environmental impact assessment was inadequate or absent where the directive required it, that a protected habitat was harmed without the assessment and safeguards the habitats rules demand, or that the public was not given the participation the law requires. These are grounds a Hungarian court is obliged to apply as a matter of EU law, and that the Commission can pursue directly, and they are often easier to establish than a broad claim that a project is simply bad — which is why a campaign should frame its case in the specific language of the directives.

## The Aarhus Convention

The second lever, distinctively important where civic space is strained, is the **Aarhus Convention** — the international guarantee of **access to environmental information, public participation, and access to justice**. Where the authorities withhold information, deny genuine participation, or block access to the courts, the Aarhus rights — enforceable through its **compliance mechanism** and reinforced in EU law — are a ground and a shield. Invoke them to force disclosure, participation, and standing.

## The Domestic Courts

The third lever is the **domestic courts**, which can review and annul a permit — but their independence is under strain, so treat a domestic challenge as a real front, useful for delay, disclosure, and the record, while relying, where needed, on the European lever above it. A domestic win is welcome; a domestic loss need not be the end, because the European route remains.

## The Demand for Participation and a Say

Finally, the **demand for a genuine say**. EU and Aarhus law require real public participation, and the demand for a **referendum or proper consultation**, denied at Nagymaros and a rallying cause there, is both a legal ground and a mobilising force. Press it on both fronts. The demand for a genuine say is unusually versatile in Hungary, because it works simultaneously as law and as mobilisation: as law, the denial of proper participation is a breach of the Aarhus Convention and EU rules that a court or the European institutions can act on; as mobilisation, the call for a referendum or a real consultation is a rallying cry that puts the government in the position of visibly refusing the public a voice. The Danube Circle’s petition demanding a referendum did both at once, and a modern campaign should wield the demand for participation on both fronts deliberately.

## Where to Turn and With Whom

EU-law and Aarhus complaints go to the **European Commission** and the **Aarhus compliance mechanism**; permit challenges to the **domestic courts**; participation demands to the authorities and, if denied, to the courts and Europe. Get help early: Hungary's environmental NGOs and public-interest lawyers, and the European networks that specialise in EU-law and Aarhus cases, know the directives, the Convention, and the strongest ground. Help engaged while the process is live and before construction is worth far more than help sought after — and in Hungary, the strongest strategy marries the **European legal lever** to the **mobilised movement** below it.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

You have a file of evidence. Now you must turn it into arguments — because the same fact persuades a court, the European institutions, a journalist, and the public in different ways. Learning to translate your evidence for each audience is what lets a community reach every lever at once.

## One Fact, Many Arguments

Take a single documented fact — say, that independent science shows a dam would drown the Danube Bend and threaten Budapest's water, approved on an assessment that breaches EU law and without a genuine public say. That one fact becomes:

- **An EU-law argument:** the assessment is deficient and the approval breaches the directives — grounds for the Commission and the Court of Justice.
- **An Aarhus argument:** the public was denied information, participation, and a say — a breach of the Convention.
- **A scientific argument:** independent experts condemn the harm to the river and the water — the credible, hard-to-dismiss case.
- **A public argument (the movement):** they would drown our Danube Bend and threaten our water, without asking us — the case that rouses the nation.
- **A national-identity argument:** they would sacrifice a river the nation loves — the cause that unites across politics.

Same fact, five arguments, five audiences. Go through your strongest facts and, for each, ask what it proves to each audience. That table is your campaign's argument bank — and in Hungary the arguments that most often decide things are the **science-and-EU-law** one, because it reaches past a strained domestic system, and the **shared-resource** one, because it builds the movement. Keep the arguments anchored to the same documented facts, so a claim made to a reporter can be backed in the science and a claim made to the Commission can be proven by the record; consistency across audiences is itself persuasive.

## Match the Argument to the Audience

**The domestic courts** want the deficiency framed precisely against the law — the flawed assessment, the breach of the directives — with the documents that prove it.

**The European institutions** respond to a clear breach of the EU directives or the Aarhus Convention, framed against the specific provision, with the record.

**Journalists** need a specific, verified, vivid story with a hook: a named river, a scientific warning, a denied say, and the David-and-Goliath of a movement against the state.

**The public** needs the argument simple and true: they would drown our river and threaten our water without asking us — the case that fills the streets.

**The movement itself** needs to see the fight is winnable and just, so it holds together and grows, as the Danube Circle grew. Sustaining belief in the possibility of victory is itself a strategic task, because a movement that no longer thinks it can win begins to dissolve, and the government knows it. The Danube Circle kept its people’s belief alive through a long fight by marking every advance — a scientific report published, a petition milestone passed, an international ally won — and by reminding its supporters that they were part of something larger than the single dam; a modern campaign should tend that belief as deliberately as it tends its evidence.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY — HOW PRESS COVERAGE SHIFTS OUTCOMES

Media is not vanity; it is a lever. Coverage raises the cost of a project, reaches the European institutions, and builds the movement. The Danube Circle grew from samizdat pamphlets into a mass movement in part by getting its case before the public despite a hostile state, and today independent media and the international press carry a campaign to Europe and the world. The media landscape a Hungarian campaign works in is itself part of the terrain to be navigated: with much of the domestic press consolidated, a campaign cannot rely on national coverage alone and must cultivate the outlets that remain independent, the online and social channels it can control directly, and the international correspondents whose reporting on a strained democracy carries weight in Europe. Building those relationships early, and feeding them accurate, specific, verifiable material, is how a campaign keeps its story alive where the usual channels are narrowed.

## Build the Story

Reporters and the public need specifics: a named place and stake, a scientific warning, and a denied say. "A project threatens the environment" is not a story; "independent scientists warn a dam would drown the Danube Bend and threaten Budapest's water, approved on a flawed assessment and without asking the public" is. Lead with your strongest verified facts — the science, the threatened river, the denied say — and offer a human and expert voice: a scientist, a resident. In Hungary the story of a treasured river and a movement to save it is uniquely powerful, and carries the memory of the Danube Circle.

## Reach the Right Outlets

Work outward in rings. **Local and community voices** first. Then the **national outlets** that remain independent, and, where the domestic media is constrained, the ways around it — independent outlets, online platforms, the movement's own channels. Then, crucially, the **European and international ring** — the international press and the European networks — which reaches the institutions and the law that lie beyond a strained domestic system. Feed each ring what it needs, and let coverage in one pull in the next. The **European and international ring is especially decisive** in Hungary, because the European lever and the world's attention reach where the domestic system may not. There is a specific dynamic worth using here: coverage in the international and European press of a strained democracy tends to draw the attention of the very European institutions that hold the legal levers, so that a well-placed international story does double duty — informing the public and signalling to Brussels and Luxembourg that a case deserves scrutiny. The Danube Circle understood the value of reaching beyond Hungary’s borders, organising an international conference and winning an international prize, and a modern campaign has far more channels to do the same.

## Sustain It, and Keep It True

A single article changes little; a drumbeat changes decisions. Plan a sequence — the science, the flawed assessment, the denied say, the EU breach, the demonstrations — so there is always a next hook, and build your own clear record so the story reaches Europe and endures. Keep messaging disciplined, accurate, and consistent; in a polarised country, one exaggeration handed to the state can cost credibility, especially with the courts and the European institutions. Verified, specific, science-and-river-centred, and relentless — that is the coverage that shifts outcomes here, and that helped a movement grow until it stopped a state.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Written communications are how you object, demand, create a record, and reach the bodies that can act — the courts, the European institutions, and the authorities. Each does double duty: it asks for something and it documents that you asked. Keep copies of everything, and send important items so that receipt is provable. Below are adaptable templates for the situations you will meet most often. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the science, the European law, and the denied say.

## 8A. Request for Environmental Information (Aarhus)

**To:** the authority / ministry
**Subject:** Request for environmental information on [project]

Under the Aarhus Convention and Hungary's environmental-access law, we request the **environmental information** on [project]: the assessment, the permit, the consultation record, and the supporting data. We are entitled to this information, and we ask for it in full and without delay.

## 8B. Objection / Demand for Participation

**To:** the authority deciding [project]
**Subject:** Objection to [project] and demand for genuine participation

We, the community of [place] and [coalition], object to [project]. Independent science shows [the harm to the river, the water, the landscape]; the environmental assessment is **deficient**; and the public has been **denied genuine participation**, contrary to EU law and the Aarhus Convention. We demand a proper assessment, real public participation, and [a referendum / genuine consultation], and that the permit be refused.

## 8C. Complaint to the European Commission (EU Law)

**To:** the European Commission
**Subject:** Complaint — breach of EU environmental law, [project], Hungary

We submit that Hungary has breached EU environmental law in [project]: the **environmental assessment is deficient** / the project harms a **protected habitat** without proper process / **public participation** was inadequate, contrary to [the directives]. We attach the evidence and request that the Commission investigate and, if warranted, pursue infringement proceedings before the Court of Justice.

## 8D. Communication to the Aarhus Compliance Mechanism

**To:** the Aarhus Convention Compliance Committee
**Subject:** Communication — denial of access, participation, or justice, [project]

We submit that Hungary has failed to comply with the Aarhus Convention regarding [project]: [environmental information withheld / public participation denied / access to justice obstructed]. We attach the evidence and request that the Committee examine the matter.

## 8E. Instructing a Lawyer / NGO

**To:** an environmental / public-interest lawyer / NGO
**Subject:** [Project] — the science, EU and Aarhus law, and the courts

We believe [project] is unlawful because [the assessment is deficient / it breaches the directives / participation was denied]. We attach the permit, the assessment, our independent science, and the consultation record, and seek advice on **challenging the permit in court**, **complaining to the European Commission**, invoking the **Aarhus Convention**, and demanding **participation**. Please advise on the strongest ground and the deadlines.

## 8F. Letter to a Scientific Body / the Academy's Tradition

**To:** [a scientific body / academics / experts]
**Subject:** [Project] on [river/place] — a request for independent assessment

We are defending [river/place] from [project], and we ask for your independent scientific assessment of its harm, as the Hungarian Academy of Sciences assessed the Danube dams. Your authority would greatly strengthen the case before the public, the courts, and Europe. We attach what we have and can connect you with the affected communities.

## 8G. Email to a Reporter (National or International)

**To:** [journalist]
**Subject:** Story tip: [specific finding] — [the river / the movement]

I follow your coverage of [beat]. We have something your readers should know, with a clear hook: [independent science and documents] show [quantified finding — e.g. "a dam would drown the Danube Bend and threaten Budapest's water, approved on a flawed assessment and without asking the public"]. It echoes how the Danube Circle stopped Nagymaros. I can share the full file and connect you with a scientist and a resident. Could we talk this week?

## 8H. Petition / Public Statement

**To:** the government and the public
**Subject:** Save [river/place] from [project]

We call on the people of Hungary and the government to save [river/place] from [project], which independent science says would [one-line harm], approved without our say. Sign, share, and stand with us, as the nation stood for the Danube — and we demand [a referendum / genuine consultation] and that the project be stopped.

## Using the Templates Well

Send important communications so that receipt is provable, and log every one in your timeline — the record of an information request, an objection, or a European complaint is itself evidence, and exactly what the courts, the European institutions, and the press will heed. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the science, the European law, and the denied say; attach your evidence; and route them, where you can, through the coalition, the NGOs and public-interest lawyers, the scientists, the European networks, and the press. The reply, or the decision, becomes part of your record, and in Hungary that record feeds the levers that matter most: the science, the European and Aarhus law, and the mobilised movement.

# IF YOU HAVE LITTLE TIME OR FEW RESOURCES: THE RAPID-FIRE VERSION

Not everyone can run a long campaign. With a few hours a week you can still do real damage to a bad project. In rough order of impact for the effort:

**1. Get the independent science.** In Hungary, the authority of independent experts anchors everything. Gather or commission the scientific case for the harm (Step 2, 8F).

**2. Request the information and object.** Use the Aarhus right to the environmental information (8A), and lodge a formal objection demanding genuine participation (8B).

**3. Complain to Europe.** If EU or Aarhus law is breached, complain to the European Commission (8C) and the Aarhus mechanism (8D) — the lever past a strained domestic system.

**4. Build the movement around the river.** Organise around the threatened river, water, or landscape the public feels (Step 3).

**5. Petition and demand a say.** Gather signatures and demand a referendum or genuine consultation (8H), as the Danube Circle did.

**6. Reach a lawyer and the NGOs.** Public-interest lawyers and NGOs (8E) can carry the EU and Aarhus challenge.

**7. Reach the press.** A vivid river-and-movement story (8G), national and international.

Do only the first three and you will have built the science, demanded the information and participation, and complained to Europe — the exact foundation past a strained domestic system. Then come back and read the rest. Even the shortest version has a spine: get the science, use Aarhus and object, complain to Europe.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Hungary's system is tilted in specific ways you must understand, so you neither waste effort nor miss the levers that work.

## Where the Tilt Shows

The tilt here is real and, in recent years, sharper. The **independence of the courts and the media is under strain**, so a domestic challenge or a fair hearing in the national press cannot always be relied on. **Civic space is more constrained** than in the transitional moment of 1989, and pressure can fall on NGOs and campaigners. Projects can be **pushed by a government** with concentrated power and connected interests. Where these tilts bite, a lever aimed only at the domestic system may give little. But — and this is the distinctive Hungarian answer — the country's **membership of the European Union** places levers above the domestic system, and its **tradition of environmental mobilisation** places levers below it.

## What Still Works — And Some Is Distinctive

The decisive point is that even where the domestic system tilts, real and distinctive levers remain — above it and below it.

**European law still binds the government.** EU environmental law and the Aarhus Convention reach past a strained domestic system, enforced from outside by the Commission and the Court of Justice.

**The movement still moves the state.** An environmental movement that mobilises the public, as the Danube Circle did, is a force a government ignores at its peril.

**Independent science still carries authority.** The credibility of independent experts is hard for any government to dismiss.

**The shared river still unites.** A threat to a treasured river or the nation's water rouses the public across politics.

**The world still watches.** International attention, focused on a strained democracy, strengthens every lever.

## The Honest Frame

So the honest frame for Hungary is realistic but far from hopeless: the domestic system is strained, and some fights will turn on the levers above and below it rather than within it — but European law and a mobilised movement give a community real and distinctive power. So diagnose your fight honestly: build the **independent science**; invoke **European and Aarhus law** past the strained domestic system; organise the **movement** and mobilise the public; defend the **shared river**; and where a clean stop is out of reach, pursue the delay, the reopened process, and the record that are the realistic gains. Treat these lesser outcomes as real gains rather than consolation prizes, because in a strained system they often are the win that is available, and they are far from nothing: a project delayed may lose its financing or its political moment, a process reopened may not survive honest scrutiny, and a record built is ammunition for the next fight and the next campaign. The Danube Circle’s own victory grew from years of such incremental pressure before the decisive moment came, and a modern campaign should value the incremental gains that keep a fight alive until its own moment arrives. Do not stake everything on a strained domestic court; reach also to Europe above it and the movement below it. This is the single most important strategic adjustment a modern Hungarian campaign must make relative to an ordinary one: in a system where the domestic institutions cannot be relied on to check the government, the community that behaves as though they can — pinning its hopes on a fair domestic hearing and nothing more — is likely to lose, while the community that builds simultaneously toward the European institutions above and the mobilised public below gives itself two routes the government does not control. Plan from the first week for all three levels, and lean hardest on the two the strained domestic system cannot capture.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO IS CAPTURED

Hungary has real concerns about the concentration of power, the closeness of connected interests to the state, and the use of public resources, so this section matters. When a project advances not on its merits but through such closeness — a permit granted to a connected company, a process bent to a favoured outcome — your strategy shifts toward exposing it and reaching past the capture through Europe.

## How to Tell

Look for the signatures of capture. A project **pushed by the government** or a **connected company**; a permit **granted despite** independent science and public opposition; **public funds or EU funds** directed to favoured interests; a **process bent** — the assessment rushed, the participation denied; and **institutions that will not check** the decision. Trace the project and the money: who profits, who decided, who is connected. In Hungary, a project advanced against the science and the public is often a matter of concentrated power and connected interests rather than an honest weighing.

## Who Can Be Captured — and Who Is Harder

Be clear-eyed about where capture reaches. **Parts of the domestic system — the deciding authorities, and, under strain, the courts and much of the media** — can be captured or constrained. But some forces are far harder to capture, and they are your targets: the **European Commission and the Court of Justice of the EU**, beyond the reach of domestic politics; the **Aarhus compliance mechanism**; the **European Anti-Fraud Office** where EU funds are misused; the **independent scientists**; the **surviving independent media and international press**; and the **mobilised public**. Capture thrives within a domestic system; your task is to lift the matter above it — to Europe, the science, and the world — and below it — to the movement.

## What to Do

Do not rely on a captured domestic system. Instead, **document the project and its irregularities precisely** — who decided, who profits, who is connected, how the process was bent, and whether EU funds were misused — and take it, with the science and the harm, to the forces beyond the capture: the **European Commission and the Court of Justice**; the **anti-fraud office** where EU money is involved; the **Aarhus mechanism**; the **independent media and international press**; and the **public**. Pair the exposure with the scientific case and the European-law breach, so the impropriety and the harm surface together. In Hungary, exposing how concentrated power and connected interests would push a project against the science and the public strips its legitimacy and strengthens every European lever — reaching, as the domestic system may not, past the capture. A particular strength of the European route in cases of capture is that it is largely immune to the local pressures that compromise a domestic institution: an official in Brussels or a judge in Luxembourg does not depend on the domestic government for appointment, budget, or protection, and where European funds are involved, the anti-fraud office has both the mandate and the independence to investigate. A Hungarian campaign that suspects a project is being pushed through capture should therefore document it not for a domestic audience alone but, above all, for these external bodies, which can act where the captured domestic institutions will not.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

The five steps win when they run together. Here is how they fit across the life of a fight — which in Hungary turns on the science, the European lever, and the movement.

## Early (Months 0–8): Science, Information, and the Movement

Pin down the project and its permit (Step 1), and build the **independent science** (Step 2, 8F). Use the **Aarhus right to the information** (8A). Build the **core and the coalition** around the threatened river (Step 3). Move at once on what is decisive: **object and demand participation** (8B); **complain to the European Commission and the Aarhus mechanism** if the law is breached (8C, 8D); and begin to **mobilise the public** and gather petitions (8H). Reach **NGOs, public-interest lawyers, and scientists** early (8E, 8F). This phase lays the foundation, and in Hungary the science, the European complaint, and the movement begun here are what ultimately win.

## Middle (Months 6–36): Escalate on Every Front

Broaden the coalition and the movement (Step 3). Turn your evidence into arguments for each audience (Turning Evidence). Escalate as the facts warrant — the **domestic court challenge**, the **European complaint** advanced, the **Aarhus communication**, and the **mass mobilisation** (Step 4, Step 3). Build the media drumbeat from local to national to European (Step 5). Each front feeds the others. Do the time-critical things first — the objection and the complaints within their windows — because those chances do not return.

## Later (Months 12–48+): The Decision, and Vigilance

Pursue what is achievable — the project **stopped, the permit struck, the process reopened**, the river saved — and, against a strained system, the **delay, the reopened process, and the record** that are the realistic gains. And stay vigilant, because a government may revive a project, and even a stopped project may face a partial defeat like the Danube's, so hold the ground and watch. Judge success honestly. Sometimes it is a project stopped and a river saved, as Nagymaros was stopped. Sometimes it is a permit struck, a process reopened, and a movement grown. Both are victories worth the fight. And there is a further value in the fights that do not achieve a clean stop: each properly documented breach of European law, each Aarhus complaint, each mobilisation builds a record and a capacity that outlast the single project, strengthening the hand of the next campaign and keeping alive, as the Danube Circle kept alive, the public’s memory that it can act and sometimes win. A movement is not built in a day, and even a fight that ends in delay rather than victory can leave the next one better armed.

## The Shape of a Campaign

```
MONTHS 0-8    Build the science | Aarhus information request | Object + demand participation | Complain to Europe | Start the movement
MONTHS 6-36   Broaden the movement | Arguments | Court + European complaint + Aarhus + mass mobilisation | Media
MONTHS 12-48+ Stop it / strike the permit / reopen the process | Guard against revival and partial defeat
```

# FINAL ASSESSMENT

Hungary gives communities a genuinely powerful — and distinctive — set of tools, and the honest measure of this guide is how squarely it faces both their strength and their limits.

The strengths are real and distinctive. **Environmentalism can carry civic resistance** where open dissent is constrained, and grow into a movement that moves the state, as the Danube Circle did. **Independent science** carries an authority hard for any government to dismiss. **Mass petitions and mobilisation** put public will beyond ignoring. **European and Aarhus law** reach past a strained domestic system against a domestic government. And the **shared river** unites the public across politics. These are the levers that let a handful of scientists with samizdat pamphlets grow into a movement that stopped a mega-project a communist state wanted.

The limits are just as real, and this guide names them. The Danube Circle **saved one dam but not the whole river** — Gabčíkovo was built. The 1989 win came in a **unique moment**. And today the **domestic courts and media are strained**, and the European lever is **slow**. No guide should pretend these away; in Hungary, the art is to reach above the strained domestic system to Europe, and below it to the movement.

So the realistic promise is this: **build the independent science; invoke European and Aarhus law; organise the movement and mobilise the public; defend the shared river; and pursue delay and a reopened process where a clean stop is out of reach — running all of it together.** None of these steps requires the collapse of a regime or a unique historical moment; each requires evidence, organisation, endurance, and a willingness to use the law and the streets together — which are within the reach of a determined community in an ordinary year. The Danube Circle won in an extraordinary moment, but the tools it used, and the European law that has since been added to them, are available in ordinary ones, and it is those tools, not that moment, that this guide asks you to take up. Do that, and against a project condemned by science and breaching European law, with a roused public, you can stop it, strike the permit, or reopen the process, as the Danube Circle stopped Nagymaros; against a strained system, you can delay it, expose it, and grow the movement. Do it as the Danube Circle did — a movement that began with pamphlets and science and grew into a force that stopped a state and helped open the door to democracy.

A last word on the movement and the moment. Hungary's lesson is that an environmental cause can be the space where civic resistance survives and grows, and that a movement armed with science, numbers, and now European law can prevail even against a powerful state — and that such a movement can matter far beyond the single river it sets out to save. The powerful count on your resignation, on the strained institutions, on the belief that nothing can be stopped. Their advantage is concentrated power and a constrained domestic system. Yours is independent science they cannot easily dismiss, European law they cannot escape, a river the nation loves, and the proven fact that a Hungarian environmental movement once stopped a mega-project the whole state wanted. Play to that. Keep the science and the record, keep the river and the public's say at the centre, keep the movement broad and growing, invoke Europe above and mobilise the public below, and treat every scientific report, every petition, every European complaint, and every demonstration as a brick in a wall no government can climb.

The tools are here, and Hungary itself proved them: a movement of scientists and ordinary citizens, beginning with underground pamphlets passed hand to hand under a communist regime, stopped a mega-dam the whole state wanted and helped open the door to democracy itself. What decides the outcome, in the end, is method, numbers, and a public that loves its river, and its water, enough to fill the streets for it — and those, together with the European law now added to them, you supply. Begin today, begin with the science and the movement, and build from there. Communities before you have stopped what the whole state wanted — a mega-dam halted despite a treaty and a state, a river's heart saved, and a movement that outgrew its first fight and helped change a whole country. What the Danube Circle won can be won again. So can yours. A movement that began with a duplicating machine and the warnings of a few scientists, under a regime far harder than any a Hungarian campaign faces today, grew until a state that wanted a dam did not dare to build it — and it did so with tools, the science, the numbers, the shared river, that any determined community can take up, now reinforced by the European law the Danube Circle never had. That is the inheritance, and the method behind it, that this guide places in your hands.

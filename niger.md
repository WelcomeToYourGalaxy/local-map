# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN NIGER

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

The harm in Niger is of a particular and dangerous kind, because for fully half a century the desert around two remote northern towns has been mined for uranium, leaving behind not only poverty but radioactivity: contaminated water, poisoned soil, and mountains of radioactive waste left open to the wind. Niger is one of the world's great sources of uranium, the ore that fuels nuclear power, and near the twin mining towns of **Arlit and Akokan**, on the southern edge of the Sahara, French-operated mines drew that uranium from the earth for the better part of fifty years to feed the reactors of France and the rest of Europe. What they have left behind is documented and grave: drinking water with uranium above internationally safe limits, streets with radiation many times the normal background level, radioactive scrap metal sold in local markets and built into people's homes, and, by independent accounts, tens of millions of tonnes of radioactive waste lying exposed, drying and blowing across the open land. One of the two great mines has now closed for good, leaving a contested and unfinished clean-up; the other, and the wealth still buried beneath the desert, have become the subject of a bitter dispute since a recent change of government moved to take control of them. The region is poor and dangerous. But the levers here are real, because the harm can be measured beyond any denial.

In a poor, insecure region, those who would confront Niger's uranium contamination work carefully, through independent science and international accountability — and the tools are real. The distinctive lever is the **independent radiation science**: the contamination — the poisoned water, the irradiated streets, the exposed waste — can be measured by independent scientists, so the harm cannot be denied, an unshakeable foundation. Niger's uranium **fuels the "clean" nuclear power of France and Europe**, so the reactors and their clean-energy claim carry responsibility back to the source — a powerful reputational lever. The company that mined it is a **French state enterprise**, answerable in principle to French law and to its own name. The **clean-up and remediation** the closed mine and the poisoned water demand are a concrete obligation, owed and unfinished. And the **international nuclear, health, and human-rights bodies** reach a harm of this gravity. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Uranium is central to Niger's economy, and the mines are now largely in the state's hands after a bitter dispute and a recent change of government — a political matter that must be handled with strict neutrality, the work kept to the environmental and health harm. The region is poor, remote, and dangerous, in an insecure Sahel that has known rebellion and grave security threats, so the work must be careful and everyone protected absolutely. So the work must favour the independent-science, fuel-chain, home-country, and international channels over any confrontation, protecting individuals absolutely and shielding identities. And the honest prize is the contamination measured and exposed, the clean-up demanded, the fuel chain and the company held to account — carefully, through science and international accountability, not an open stop. These tools all bear on the uranium legacy that defines the country; a community facing any other project draws on the same careful, largely external toolkit — environmental scrutiny where it functions, the international frameworks, and independent documentation. Above all, protect the people.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN BURKINA FASO

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Burkina Faso is a landlocked Sahelian nation — military-led and gripped by insecurity across much of its territory — whose economy has shifted in a generation from the land to gold, and whose defining environmental struggles are the harms of a gold-mining boom and the advance of the desert upon the farms. Mercury and cyanide seep into the water and the land; hundreds of thousands now dig for gold to survive; and against the encroaching desert, Burkina's own farmers have pioneered some of the world's great feats of land restoration — the zaï pit and the stone bund that pushed the desert back — a real and celebrated achievement this guide honours. But the same gold that now feeds the nation also poisons its water and entangles its fragile peace, and its industrial concessions can take farmers' land. Much of the country is dangerous. But a community facing a harmful mine or concession is not without tools.

A community in Burkina Faso facing a harmful project — an industrial gold mine poisoning the water, a concession displacing farmers from their land — works with real, if constrained, tools, pursued through what still functions and never at the cost of anyone's safety. A large mining project is supposed to undergo an **environmental and social impact assessment** — a gate that can be pressed where it is skipped or defective. The **mining code's community provisions** — consultation, compensation, and the share of revenue owed to local development — give a community a legal handle. **Land and customary rights** protect the farmers' ground and require that the displaced be compensated. The **courts and the functioning institutions** can enforce these, where it is safe to reach them. The **foreign mining companies behind the industrial concessions** operate under **international standards and financiers** a case can hold them to. And the **international and regional frameworks, and the community itself**, carry the case where the state is fraught. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The state is military-led and much of the country insecure, with armed groups active, so the work must run through what functions and avoid the conflict areas entirely. The gold is now the economy and entangled with the insecurity. And artisanal mining is a survival livelihood for hundreds of thousands — so the harm to be contested is the pollution, the displacement, and the industrial concession, never the poor who mine to live, who must never be met with mere repression. So the work must protect everyone involved, centre on what is safe, and aim at the harm contested — a mine's assessment enforced, a community's land and compensation defended, the pollution documented and pressed — not an open defeat of the gold economy. Above all, protect the people.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot defeat the gold economy or safely act in the conflict areas, and you must never punish the poor who mine to survive. What you can do is contest the specific harm — the pollution, the displacement, the industrial concession — through what still functions: force the mine through a real assessment, invoke the mining code's community provisions and the land rights, hold the foreign company to its standards, document the harm, and work through the functioning institutions, the international frameworks, and the community, safely, and protecting everyone.

An industrial mine needs an environmental and social impact assessment, a mining permit, and — in law — consultation and compensation for the communities it affects.

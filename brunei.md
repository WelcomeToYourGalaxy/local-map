# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN BRUNEI

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Brunei is one of the very richest nations on earth per person, its wealth drawn almost entirely from the offshore oil and gas beneath its coastal waters — and, unusually, it has kept more than seventy percent of its land under rainforest, protected well over half its territory, joined the tri-national **Heart of Borneo** effort, and lets its forests absorb almost all of its own emissions. This is a real and genuinely rare achievement, especially on an island whose forests have elsewhere been so heavily felled, and this guide honours it plainly. But the same nation is almost wholly dependent on the oil and gas whose burning drives the climate crisis, and now anchors a growing **petrochemical and plastics industry** it calls diversification but that keeps it tied to fossil fuels — even as its forests and coasts face the ordinary pressures of development. And Brunei is an absolute monarchy, among the most politically closed nations anywhere, where open dissent is essentially impossible and criticism of the ruler and the royal family is simply not permitted. No one inside can openly resist.

Those who care about Brunei's environment cannot organise openly, but a green-conscious state that values its conservation record gives real, if narrow, levers — all worked through the state's own aims, never as opposition. The government's **own green commitments and conservation pledges** — the frameworks, the targets, the protected forest — are pledges it can be held to and measured against, on its own terms. The **contradiction between the petrochemical turn and the green image** is an evidence-based argument: doubling down on plastics and fossil fuels sits ill with the conservation brand the state prizes, and true diversification lies elsewhere. The **international frameworks Brunei has joined** — the tri-national Heart of Borneo and the global climate and conservation regimes — carry commitments and reputational stakes a case can invoke. And **science, technical institutions, and the language of stewardship** are channels a closed state permits, where an environmental case can be made safely. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Brunei is an absolute monarchy among the most closed states in the world: open dissent is impossible, criticism of the ruler and royal family is not permitted, and the red lines around the monarchy, religion, and politics are absolute. The oil dependence is real and diversification genuinely hard. There is no domestic space for opposition. And no one can be exposed. So the work must run only through the state's own aims, the international frameworks, and the language of stewardship — never as criticism or dissent, and protecting everyone involved absolutely — and the honest prize is the forest defended and extended, the green commitments upheld, and the worst pressures eased, all on the state's own terms, not an open stop.

You cannot organise openly, and you must never frame anything as opposition, criticism, or dissent.

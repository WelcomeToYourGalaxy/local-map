Burundi is a small, crowded land of green and eroding hills, among the poorest and most densely peopled on earth, its western edge the shore of Lake Tanganyika — one of the world's most ancient, vast, and biodiverse lakes, the lifeline of its millions — and its future shadowed by a great nickel deposit that could bring development or the resource curse. This guide has hidden none of that, nor pretended that the lake is not under severe pressure, or that the nickel can simply be refused, or that the space is open, and there is no landmark victory to promise you. But the lake is the nation's greatest treasure, and there, through the watershed that ties the eroding hills to the failing fishery and through the regional framework the riparian states share, its degradation can genuinely be met; the nickel can be held to the standards and the transparency that determine its worth; and the forests can be restored in ways that feed the hill families as they hold the soil and spare the lake. Those are the levers, and they are real: the watershed and the regional framework that defend the lake; the environmental and governance scrutiny of the nickel; the reforestation that relieves the poverty as it stops the sediment; and the community, technical, regional, and international partners who carry it, as the regional pilots that reduced the sediment and treated the wastewater attest. There is no open confrontation in a semi-closed state, and no landmark victory over poverty and density; this guide has said so throughout, and counselled care, justice, and neutrality on the politics. But the great lake and the crowded hills of Burundi are worth every careful, watershed-grounded, just effort, the levers that run through the lake and the regional framework are real, and the patient, technical, community-based work of defending the lake, scrutinising the nickel, and restoring the land is one that Burundians — the fishers and farmers, the scientists, and their regional and international partners — can genuinely do. So can you.

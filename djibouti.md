# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN DJIBOUTI

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Djibouti is a tiny, strategically pivotal, desperately water-scarce nation whose development is dominated by ports, foreign bases, and debt — and whose gravest environmental question is simply whether its people, above all its rural herders, will have water to live. It has no rivers that run year-round and receives almost no rain; it has among the very least fresh water per person of any nation on earth, far below even the recognised threshold of absolute scarcity; its groundwater is being drawn down faster than it can refill and is turning salty; and its recurring droughts, worsening with the climate, fall hardest on the pastoralist communities of the arid interior, where fewer than half the people have safe water while the capital has far more. Into the teeth of this scarcity the nation builds desalination plants, pipes water in from a neighbour, and pursues ports, free zones, and heavy industry along its coasts and salt flats. The political space is limited, and the geopolitics of chokepoints and bases lies beyond the nation's control. But water is Djibouti's binding constraint, and that gives real ground.

Those who would defend Djibouti's water and land work with real, if constrained, tools — worked carefully and largely through the funders and the technical. The strongest is the **water-sustainability argument**: in one of the world's most water-scarce nations, a project that depletes or salts the water is self-defeating, and showing so is an argument aligned with the nation's own survival, not against it. The **rural herders' fair claim** — to the water and investment they are denied while the capital has far more — is a powerful equity argument. The **natural heritage** — the salt lakes, forests, and coasts — is a unique inheritance worth more whole than industrialised. And the distinctive lever: the **international funders who finance Djibouti's development** carry safeguards, environmental-assessment requirements, and accountability mechanisms that a case can invoke, because donor-financed projects must meet the funders' rules. The nation's own stated **sustainability goals** can be held to alongside them. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. Djibouti genuinely lacks water and genuinely needs development, with few options, and is poor and heavily indebted, so its choices are constrained. Its environment is shaped by a geopolitics of chokepoints and bases beyond its control. It is an authoritarian state with real limits on dissent and criticism, and powerful foreign partners, so the work must run through the international, funder, and technical channels, never open confrontation — protecting the rural and marginalised communities and any advocate whose position could be jeopardised. So the honest prize is a project made sustainable and equitable, the water protected, the rural claim advanced, and the funders' safeguards enforced — not an open defeat of a needed development. Above all, frame everything around the water's sustainability, the nation's own goals, and the funders' rules, and protect the people.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot deny a water-scarce nation its development, nor safely mount open confrontation in an authoritarian state with powerful foreign partners. What you can do is make the case the nation's own survival demands: show that a project depleting or salting the water is self-defeating, advance the rural herders' fair claim, defend the unique heritage, and above all invoke the safeguards and rules the international funders require of every project they finance — all carefully, framed around sustainability and the funders' rules, and protecting the people.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A port, plant, or industrial project advances by state decision and foreign finance, and — where a funder is involved — must meet that funder's environmental and social safeguards and assessment. That safeguard process is the point of entry: a documented case can invoke the funders' rules, show the harm to the scarce water, and advance the rural claim. The strongest entry point is the one that touches the nation's own survival: the water's sustainability.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the finance: which international funders back the project, and what safeguards, assessments, and accountability mechanisms they require. Follow the equity: whether the rural herders are denied the water and investment the capital receives. The leverage lies in the water's sustainability, the funders' safeguards, and the rural claim.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN JERSEY

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

When the derelict Plémont holiday camp on Jersey's north-west coast — eleven and a half acres of coastal headland beside the last puffin colony on the island — was slated for housing, and consent for twenty-eight homes was granted in 2012, islanders did not accept the loss of one of the island's finest stretches of coast. Led by the **National Trust for Jersey**, they had already campaigned for over a decade; now they pressed on, through the States Assembly and the public, until a different path opened. The Trust agreed to buy the site; the States voted to fund half the purchase; the Trust raised the rest; and the headland was bought, the buildings demolished, and the land returned to nature. After fourteen years of campaigning, a place cleared for houses became an open, wild headland for everyone, forever. The lesson is distinctly Jersey: a community here can save a treasured place even after losing the planning battle.

A community in Jersey facing a harmful development works with genuinely strong tools, in a small, close-knit, rule-of-law island democracy. The first line is the **planning system and its objections and public-inquiry process** — a real gate, though, as Plémont shows, one a community can lose. Ecological and coastal value can be defended through **environmental and ecological assessment and designations** — the puffin colony, the scarce coast. The **States Assembly** is an accessible political route that can vote public funds and shift a decision; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. The distinctive Jersey lever is the **National Trust for Jersey and the buyout-and-restoration** it can lead: a respected conservation body that can acquire and hold land can save a site even one with planning consent — buying it, demolishing, and returning it to nature, as it did at Plémont. And a **small island's love of its scarce, treasured coast** mobilises powerfully. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The planning fight can be lost — at Plémont, after years of applications and a public inquiry, consent was granted. The buyout that saved it required raising over eight million pounds, roughly half of it public money voted by the States, which was genuinely contentious. And it took fourteen years. This guide does not pretend the win was quick, cheap, or won on the planning merits. So aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a development refused on planning or ecological grounds where possible, and, where the planning battle is lost, a treasured place bought out and restored — through the planning system, the Assembly, the Trust, and the island's love of its coast, with the patience Plémont demanded.

You win here as Plémont did — by combining a sustained community coalition, the political route through the Assembly, and, above all, the buyout-and-restoration a conservation trust can lead.

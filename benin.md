# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What is harmed?** This is the first question here. What **soil, health, land, water, or labour** is harmed — the degraded fields, the pesticide-sickened farmers, the land taken for the zone, the ill-treated workers.

**5. How can the supply chain be engaged?** How the **global buyers, certification, and standards** can be brought to bear on an export-oriented cotton economy.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on — and here it spans soil and health, land and labour. A cotton institution, a buyer, a certifier, and the farmers themselves all move on evidence — and in Benin, a clear record of the harm and the sustainable alternative is precisely what an effort turns into a real result, so build it well. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Harm, the Driver, and the Alternative

Get the facts the case turns on. Identify the **harm and its driver** — the degraded soil and the pesticide, the taken land and the zone, the ill-treated labour — and, crucially, the **sustainable alternative** that could serve the farmers better. Much of this the farmers know first-hand, joined to the agronomic and health science. Establish exactly **what is harmed, why, and what sustainable path exists**, because the harm-and-its-alternative is the effort's foundation.

## Layer 2: The Soil, the Health, the Land, and the Labour

Document the harm across its dimensions. Establish the **environmental and health harm** — the degraded and eroding soil, the pesticide harm to farmers' health and the water, the land taken for the zone — and the **labour conditions** — the wages, the hours, the treatment of those who work the cotton and the cloth — drawing on **the farmers' testimony, the agronomic and health science, and the labour record**. This evidence across soil, health, land, and labour is what makes the case for reform concrete and undeniable.

## Layer 3: The Harm, the Alternative, the Farmers, and the Supply Chain

Finally, document the reform and the path. Does the **harm** degrade the soil, the health, the land, or the labour? What **sustainable alternative** exists? How can the **farmers** be organised, the **zone** scrutinised, and the **supply chain** engaged? Each is a separate element, and together they make a case for a cotton economy reformed toward sustainability and fairness. This is the layer that most often decides these efforts, because a documented harm, a real alternative, organised farmers, and supply-chain reach are exactly what let a Beninese effort reform the nation's living. Build it well, and the sustainable-cotton case, the alternative, the farmers, and the supply chain become the effort's genuine strength. One practical note on building this layer: because the farmers themselves are both the documenters and the beneficiaries of the case, the documentation in Benin has a double value that repays careful attention. When farmers record the harm they experience — the sicknesses that follow the spraying, the soil that yields less each year, the terms that leave them little — they are not only building an evidentiary record but also coming to understand their own situation collectively, which is the beginning of organisation. The most effective documentation therefore does double duty: it gathers, through the farmers' own participation and testimony joined to agronomic and health science, a rigorous record of the harm and the sustainable alternative; and in the gathering, it builds the farmers' shared awareness and readiness to act. Building this layer well means engaging the farmers as participants in documenting their own reality, so that the resulting file is both credible to buyers, certifiers, and authorities, and owned by the farmers whose reform it will drive — because in Benin the people who document the harm are the same people who must reform it, and their ownership of the evidence is itself a purpose.

Pesticide harm to farmers' health, the exhaustion of the soil, and the fouling of water are not abstract environmental concerns but direct threats to the nation's productive capacity and its people's lives, and framing the demands this way makes them at once humane and economically sensible. Above all, keep the discipline — and the care — that give these efforts their force. Document the harm. Make the sustainable-cotton case. Offer the health-and-organic alternative. Organise the farmers and workers. Scrutinise the zone. And engage the supply chain — because in a country whose living is cotton, an effort that reforms that living toward sustainability and fairness, in the farmers' own interest and with care, is the one that is both effective and just.

Benin's living is cotton, the white gold that is the backbone of its economy and the livelihood of more than two million of its people; but grown with heavy poison it degrades the soil and harms the health of those who farm it, and processed in a flagship zone on terms that favour the few it raises hard questions of land, water, and labour; this guide has hidden none of that, nor pretended that cotton can be opposed when it is the nation's living, or that the state and its president are not deeply invested in it, or that Benin's once-open political space has not narrowed, and there is no landmark victory here to promise you. But the very fact that the harm and the living are one is the key: because the farmers who are harmed are the farmers whose living it is, the way to defend them is not to oppose their cotton but to reform it — to grow it with less poison and more care, to process it on fair terms, and to organise the two million who sustain it so that their economy serves them. There is no easy victory, and no opposing the crop that feeds a nation; this guide has said so throughout, and insisted on care for those who organise in a narrowing space. But the soil and the health and the labour of Benin are worth defending, the levers that run through the farmers' own interest are real, and the patient, constructive work of making a nation's living sustainable and fair is one that its farmers and workers can genuinely do — for themselves, and reform the white gold to serve them. So can you help them.

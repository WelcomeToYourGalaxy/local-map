# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Moldova's environmental life turns above all on the **Dniester**, the great transboundary river the Moldovans call the **Nistru**, on which some **seventy percent of the population depends for its drinking water**, and which is vital too for farming, industry, fisheries, and nature. The river rises in the Carpathian mountains of Ukraine, flows down through Moldova, and returns to Ukraine to reach the Black Sea — and its flows, water quality, and biodiversity are shaped decisively by a large **hydropower complex upstream on the Ukrainian side**, now being further expanded, which Moldova fears will reduce the downstream flow and harm the river it cannot do without. Beyond the river, Moldova is among the very least-forested nations in Europe, its scarce woodlands pressed by illegal logging; its energy, long almost wholly dependent on Russian gas, is being turned toward the European grid and renewables; and its rich but overworked farmland faces soil degradation, erosion, and deepening, climate-driven drought.

A community in Moldova works with a real toolkit — encouragingly real for a democracy bound for Europe, where the levers run through open democracy and European leverage. A project needs an **environmental impact assessment (EIA)** with public consultation. **Working courts** and **free civil society** are genuine, and lawful advocacy, participation, and litigation are the means. The **transboundary river framework** — with Ukraine, and under international water and nature conventions — governs the Dniester the country cannot do without. The **leverage of EU accession** — aligning Moldovan law and practice with **European environmental law** — is real and growing. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Moldova is **poor**, with real development needs and limited means. The gravest pressures on the Dniester come from **upstream in Ukraine — now at war — and from a breakaway region beyond the government's control**, so Moldova's leverage over them is diplomatic and legal, not direct, and must be exercised with care in a wartime and frozen-conflict context. **European accession is a years-long process, conditional and uncertain.** And **no single triumph is to be claimed**. So aim for what these levers can deliver, realistically, through the genuine tools a democracy bound for Europe possesses.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

A big project looks unstoppable, but it's a chain of approvals and commitments, and a chain breaks at its weakest link. A Moldovan fight turns on the **EIA and the open courts**; on the **transboundary river framework** for the Dniester; on the **leverage of EU accession** and European environmental law; on the **defence of the forests**; and on the **clean-energy transition**. Every one of those is a link you can grab — and in a democracy bound for Europe, the accession leverage runs through them all. **Find the one closest to your hand and pull.**

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

To break the chain you have to see it — who decides, how a project moves, and who's watching.

## Who Actually Decides

A project needs an **EIA** with public consultation before permits are granted by the relevant authority. **Working courts** review decisions, and **free civil society** can organise and challenge. The **Dniester** is governed by a **transboundary framework** with Ukraine and under international water and nature conventions. And **EU accession** brings the leverage of aligning with **European environmental law**, watched by the European institutions. Know which of these you can reach, and aim there.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

**The EIA and consultation.** The project prepares its EIA; there's consultation. *Your opening — and it arms every later step:* participate, get the information, and put your objections on the record.

**The courts.** *Your opening:* a flawed EIA or an unlawful permit can be challenged in the working courts.

**The transboundary framework (for the river).** *Your opening:* an upstream threat to the Dniester can be raised in the transboundary framework and under international conventions, with EU support.

**The accession leverage.** *Your opening:* tie your case to European environmental law, which Moldova is committed to align with.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Dig up three things. **Who's behind it and how it's funded** — including any EU or international funds, which carry environmental conditions. **What it threatens** — the Dniester's flow and quality, the forests, the soil — because those are your levers, and the river engages the transboundary framework. **What it still needs** — the EIA not approved, the permit not final, the consultation not done, the accession commitments not met: whatever's missing is the soft spot.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

Each is an ally or an arena. The **environmental authority** runs the EIA. The **courts** review permits. The **transboundary framework** and **international conventions** govern the Dniester. The **EU institutions** watch the accession commitments and European environmental law. And Moldova's **environmental NGOs, free civil society, and press** are the forces that reach furthest — the open-democracy channels that genuinely work.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "the project" is weak. Being specific — this flawed EIA, this threat to the Dniester, this accession commitment — is strong. Answer five questions in writing.

**1. What is it, and who's behind it — and how is it funded?** Name the project, the developer, and the funding, including any EU or international funds with conditions.

**2. What does it threaten?** Map it over the **Dniester (the drinking water of seventy percent of the country)**, the forests, and the soil. **A threat to the Dniester engages the transboundary river framework; a threat to forests or soil engages the law and the accession commitments.**

**3. What stage is it at, and what's the next deadline?** Is the EIA or consultation open? Has the permit issued (opening the courts)? For the river, is an upstream development advancing (to raise in the framework)? These openings are time-bound.

**4. Is the EIA or permit flawed, or does it breach European environmental law?** A defective assessment, an ignored obligation, a breach of a standard Moldova is committed to align with — your openings.

**5. Who's your coalition, and can you tie it to Europe?** Environmental NGOs, free civil society, the press — and the European environmental law and accession commitments the case can be tied to.

**What this looks like in real life.** A project threatens the Dniester or a scarce forest, with a flawed EIA, and can be tied to European environmental standards. The five questions yield: the developer and funding; the threat to the river or forest (the levers); the EIA open; the assessment's flaws or the European-law breach; and a coalition with an accession hook. That's the Moldovan pattern — use the open courts and the accession leverage, and for the river the transboundary framework.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

The difference between a community that can fight and one that can't is proof. Build it in three layers.

**Layer one: the paper trail.** Get the documents the decision rests on — the EIA, the permit application, the funding, and (for the river) the transboundary and upstream data. Then read the EIA for **flaws** — an understated impact, an ignored European-law obligation, a defective process.

**Layer two: the threat, documented.** Document what the project threatens — the **Dniester's flow and quality** (the drinking water of most of the country), the **forests**, the **soil** — with dated, located photographs, expert and scientific evidence, and, for the river, the upstream and flow data the transboundary framework turns on.

**Layer three: the European and framework hooks.** Tie the harm to **European environmental law** (the standards Moldova is committed to align with) and, for the river, to the **transboundary framework and international conventions**. Walking into the EIA, the courts, the European institutions, or the transboundary framework with the flaws, the documented threat, and those hooks is what makes them act.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

- **The EIA, the permit application, the funding, the transboundary/upstream data** — from the authorities and the framework.
- **The documented threat** — to the Dniester's flow and quality, the forests, the soil.
- **The EIA's flaws and any European-law breach** — the legal openings.
- **The European and framework hooks** — the standards and conventions the case ties to.
- **Dated evidence and expert reports** — photographs marked with date and place, and river/forest/soil evidence.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION

Documents give you a case; free civil society and a coalition tied to Europe give you weight in a democracy bound for accession. Build in four moves.

**Root it in the affected community and the NGOs.** Start with those directly affected — the water users, the forest communities — and bring in Moldova's environmental NGOs and free civil society, who know the EIA, the courts, and the accession framework.

**Bring in the experts and lawyers early.** An EIA, European-law, or transboundary-river fight needs credible scientific and legal expertise — on the river's flows, the forests, the soil, and the assessment's flaws. Bring it in from the start.

**Tie the fight to Europe.** The distinctive Moldovan lever: connect your case to **European environmental law and the accession commitments**, which the government is committed to meet and the EU institutions watch — a leverage a democracy bound for Europe genuinely feels.

**Build to last — and, for the river, with care.** Moldovan fights run for months or years through legal and diplomatic channels. Build clear roles, a secure archive, a way to decide together, and — for the transboundary river, in a wartime and frozen-conflict context — a careful, diplomatic approach.

# STEP 4: THE OPEN COURTS, EUROPEAN LEVERAGE & THE RIVER FRAMEWORK

Moldova's sharpest levers are its working courts, the leverage of European accession, and — for the Dniester — the transboundary framework.

## The Open Courts and the EIA

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

A stack of documents wins nothing until you aim it.

**A threat to the river becomes a national emergency.** Not "we oppose the project" but *"this threatens the Dniester, the drinking water of seventy percent of the country — a matter for the transboundary framework and the whole nation."*

**A flawed EIA becomes a court case.** *"The assessment understated the harm and ignored the European standards Moldova is bound to align with — the permit is unlawful."*

**A European-law shortfall becomes accession leverage.** *"This falls short of European environmental law we've committed to meet — and the EU is watching."*

**Illegal logging becomes a defended forest.** *"In one of Europe's least-forested nations, this scarce woodland is being cut unlawfully — and the law can stop it."*

Match it to the venue: the river to the transboundary framework and the nation; the EIA flaw to the courts; the European-law shortfall to the accession process and the EU institutions; the forests to the law and the press.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY

In Moldova, free civil society and press build the public and political weight behind the legal case — and the European dimension reaches the EU institutions watching accession. The Dniester, as the nation's drinking water, is a powerful national cause.

**Lead with the river and the threat, not procedure.** Not "the EIA was deficient" but "the Dniester, the drinking water of most of the country, is threatened — and here is what it would cost." Use your strongest material: the documented threat, dated photographs, expert evidence, and the European hook.

**Work several audiences at once.** Moldovan press and civil society build domestic weight; European attention and the EU institutions reach the accession leverage, where a European-law shortfall bites. Make it easy: captioned photos, accurate summaries, a map, expert voices.

**Two hard rules.** Accuracy above all — one exaggeration can discredit everything. And on the transboundary river, in wartime, be careful and diplomatic — frame it as protecting a shared river, not as blame, and let the framework do the work.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Moldova's tools are real, but constraints press, and you must plan for them.

**When the domestic decision goes against you.** This is a working democracy: use the courts, and tie the case to European environmental law, which the accession process makes it costly to ignore.

**When the threat is upstream and in wartime.** The river's gravest pressures come from Ukraine and a breakaway region beyond the government's control. Work the transboundary framework diplomatically and with EU support — the leverage is legal and diplomatic, not direct, and must be exercised with care.

**When accession is slow.** It is — years, conditional, uncertain. Use the leverage as it grows, and don't rely on it alone; the courts and civil society work now.

**When the nation is poor.** Resources are limited. Lean on the affordable tools — EIA participation, litigation, civil society, and the accession and framework leverage — which don't depend on wealth.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO'S CAPTURED

Moldova, though a functioning democracy, has real, documented concerns about the capture of decisions and funds by connected interests, so this matters.

**How to spot it.** Permits against the evidence; EIAs written for the proponent; funds directed to connected interests; illegal logging tolerated; benefits to the connected while the river, forests, and communities bear the harm.

**Who's supposed to guard against it.** Moldova's anti-corruption and audit bodies examine public money and conduct. The **courts** can quash a tainted permit. The **EU institutions** can act on accession and EU-funds conditions. And Moldova's **free press and civil society** have exposed captured deals — a reform current the accession path strengthens.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — how the process was bent, who benefits, what the law and the European commitments required — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect. Route serious evidence to the anti-corruption bodies, the courts, the EU institutions, and trusted journalists rather than making accusations you can't back. Pair the exposure with the legal and accession leverage.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

The documents build your case; the documented threat and the European hooks aim it; the community, NGOs, and free civil society are the muscle; the courts, the accession leverage, and the transboundary framework press it; the press carries it. Together they multiply. One at a time, they stall — and for the river, move with care.

**Early on (weeks 1–8):** participate in and object to the EIA; document the threat; tie it to European environmental law; build a coalition; for the river, prepare the framework case.

**In the thick of it (months–year+):** challenge a flawed EIA or permit in court; press the accession leverage; raise the river in the transboundary framework; open the media front.

**The long haul (year+):** carry the litigation and the leverage; keep the coalition alive; hold the line for a project stopped or reworked, an assessment reopened, the river's flow defended, forests protected, and cleaner development secured.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN LATVIA

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

*This guide carries Latvia's extraordinary landmark — a river fight that helped set a nation free — but turns for the practical work to the very different, far better tools a free EU Latvia now provides: the environmental-assessment process, the Aarhus Convention, EU environmental law, the courts, and a free press. It names each in plain words the first time.*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In the mid-1980s, under Soviet rule, the authorities were building a huge hydroelectric dam on the **Daugava** — Latvia's greatest river, the "mother river" of its poetry and folk songs — near Daugavpils. Construction had ground on for years; a forest, a cemetery, and whole communities had already been moved. Then, in 1986, a journalist and an engineer published a single article — **"On the Fate of the Daugava"** — that questioned the dam's worth and mourned what it would destroy of Latvia's land, economy, and heritage. It swayed a nation. Hundreds of thousands protested and wrote. The regime tried to censor even the word "Daugava" — down to a brand of waffles — so people **sang about the river** at concerts instead. And in **November 1987 the Soviet state, which almost never yielded, cancelled the dam.** That victory became a spark of the **Latvian Awakening — the Singing Revolution** — and less than four years later, Latvia was free.

The two men whose article "On the Fate of the Daugava" swayed a nation drew on the power of a true story and the nation's own cultural symbols — but the practical tools a free Latvia now provides are far stronger than the Daugava campaigners had. A project needs an **environmental impact assessment (EIA)** with public consultation. Latvia is bound by the **Aarhus Convention** — your rights to environmental information, participation, and access to justice — and by **EU environmental law** (the Habitats and Birds Directives, **Natura 2000**, the Water Framework Directive), enforceable ultimately through the **European Commission** and the EU courts. The Latvian **courts** review permits and assessments, and protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. And a **free press** and a deep civic tradition amplify a true story. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The Daugava was won under a **specific, now-vanished regime** — a Soviet occupation in the brief thaw of reform — where mass public pressure was almost the only channel, and where the fight for the river was also, quietly, a fight for the nation. That double character gave it a force an ordinary planning dispute in today's Latvia will not automatically carry. So take from the Daugava the enduring lessons — the power of a true story, of culture, of peaceful unity — and turn, for the practical work, to the modern tools a free Latvia now provides.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

A big project looks unstoppable, but it's a chain of approvals, and a chain breaks at its weakest link. A modern Latvian project needs its **EIA** and consultation; it runs into **Aarhus rights** and **EU environmental law** (Natura 2000, the Water Framework Directive); it can be challenged in the **courts**; and it can be met with a **true, culturally resonant story** that moves the public. Every one of those is a link you can grab — the modern legal ones for the practical work, the story for the public heart. **Find the one closest to your hand and pull.**

- **If a protected habitat, species, or water body is at stake, EU environmental law is a powerful lever** — Natura 2000 and the Water Framework Directive, ultimately enforceable through the European Commission.
- **If the permit or assessment is flawed, the courts genuinely bite** — this is a functioning EU democracy.
- **If you can tell a true, culturally resonant story, do** — the Daugava proved how far that reaches in Latvia, though an ordinary dispute won't carry a nation the way a fight for freedom did.

That's how a Latvian fight is won today.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

To break the chain you have to see it — who decides, how a project moves, and who's watching.

## Who Actually Decides

A project needs an **environmental impact assessment (EIA)** with public consultation, overseen by the state environmental authority, before permits are granted by the relevant ministry or municipality. **Aarhus** guarantees your rights to information, participation, and access to justice. **EU environmental law** — the Habitats and Birds Directives, **Natura 2000**, the Water Framework Directive — binds Latvia and is ultimately enforceable through the **European Commission** and the EU courts. The Latvian **courts** review the legality of assessments and permits. Know which of these you can engage, and aim there.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

**The EIA and consultation.** The project prepares its EIA; there's public consultation. *Your opening — and it arms every later step:* use your **Aarhus** rights to get the information and participate, and put your objections on the record.

**The permit.** The authority grants or refuses. *Your opening:* a permit on a flawed EIA, or one that breaches EU environmental law, is vulnerable in court.

**EU law and the Commission.** *Your opening:* where Natura 2000 or the Water Framework Directive is breached, complain to the European Commission.

**The public.** *Your opening — the Daugava lever:* a true, culturally resonant story that mobilises the public non-violently.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Dig up three things. **Who's behind it and how it's funded** — including any EU funds, which carry environmental conditions. **What EU-protected value it touches** — a Natura 2000 site, a protected species, a water body — because EU law is your sharpest legal lever. **What it still needs** — the EIA not approved, the permit not final, the consultation not done: whatever's missing is the soft spot.

## Who's Supposed to Be Watching

Each is an ally or an arena. The **state environmental authority** runs the EIA. **Aarhus** guarantees your rights. **EU environmental law** and the **European Commission** protect habitats, species, and water. The **courts** review permits. And Latvia's **environmental NGOs, cultural and civic organisations, and free press** are the forces that reach furthest — and can carry a true story as the Daugava's was carried.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "the project" is weak. Being specific — this EU-protected site, this flawed EIA, this true story — is strong. Answer five questions in writing.

**1. What is it, and who's behind it — and how is it funded?** Name the project, the developer, and the funding, including any EU funds with environmental conditions.

**2. Where is it, and what does it touch?** Map it over habitats, species, water bodies, forests, and the coast — and flag any **Natura 2000 site, protected species, or water body**, because **EU environmental law is your sharpest legal lever**.

**3. What stage is it at, and what's the next deadline?** Is the EIA or consultation open (use Aarhus now)? Has the permit issued (opening the courts)? These steps are time-bound.

**4. Is the EIA or permit flawed, or does it breach EU law?** A defective assessment, an ignored Natura 2000 obligation, a Water Framework breach — your legal openings.

**5. Is there a true, resonant story — and who tells it?** A river, a forest, a heritage the culture holds dear; and the NGOs, cultural groups, and press who can carry it. The Daugava's power was a true story a nation took up.

**What this looks like in real life.** A project threatens a river or forest, touching a Natura 2000 site, with a flawed EIA and EU funding. The five questions yield: the developer and funding; the EU-protected value (the legal lever); the EIA/consultation open; the assessment's flaws; and a true story with groups to carry it. That's the modern Latvian pattern — use Aarhus and EU law and the courts for the practical fight, and a true story for the public heart.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

The difference between a community that can fight and one that can't is proof — legal and cultural both. Build it in three layers.

**Layer one: the paper trail (via Aarhus).** Use your **Aarhus** information rights to get the documents the decision rests on — the EIA, the permit application, the funding, the Natura 2000 assessment. Then read the EIA for **flaws** — an understated impact, an ignored EU obligation, a defective process.

**Layer two: the EU-protected value.** Document the **habitats, species, and water bodies** the project would harm, and their protected status (Natura 2000, the Water Framework Directive) — the heart of an EU-law case — with dated, located photographs and expert evidence.

**Layer three: the true story.** Gather the cultural and human weight — the river, forest, or heritage the nation holds dear, and the community's connection to it — because a true, resonant story is what moves the Latvian public, as the Daugava did. Walking into the EIA, the courts, the Commission, or the press with the legal flaws, the protected value, and a true story is what makes them act.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

- **The EIA, the permit application, the funding, the Natura 2000 assessment** — via your Aarhus information rights.
- **The EU-protected value** — the habitats, species, and water bodies, and their status.
- **The EIA's flaws and any EU-law breach** — the legal openings.
- **The true story** — the cultural and human weight of what's at stake.
- **Dated evidence and expert reports** — photographs marked with date and place, and species/habitat/water evidence.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION

Documents give you a case; a coalition that carries the legal fight and the true story together gives you power — the modern Latvian model, rooted in the Daugava's lesson. Build in four moves.

**Start with the affected community and the NGOs.** Root the fight in the people directly affected, and bring in Latvia's environmental NGOs and cultural and civic organisations, who know the EIA, Aarhus, EU law, and the courts.

**Bring in the experts and lawyers early.** An EU-law and EIA fight needs credible ecological and legal expertise — on the habitats, the species, the water, and the assessment's flaws. Bring it in from the start.

**Tell the true story — and invoke the culture.** The Daugava's enduring lesson: reach past dry objection to something a whole people feels — a river, a forest, a heritage woven into the nation's identity — and carry it through the written word, the song, the peaceful gathering. A true, culturally resonant story is Latvia's distinctive amplifier.

**Build to last, non-violently.** Modern Latvian fights run for months or years through legal channels. Build clear roles, a secure shared archive, an inclusive way to decide together, and a plan for the long haul — and carry it, as Latvia has always carried dissent, peacefully.

# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES & THE EU TOOLKIT

Latvia's sharpest modern levers are the EU environmental framework and the courts — the tools the Daugava campaigners never had.

## Aarhus — Your Rights to Information, Participation, and Justice

Start with **Aarhus**: use your rights to **environmental information** (to get the documents), to **participate** in the EIA and consultation (to put your case on the record), and to **access to justice** (to challenge decisions in court). Aarhus underpins every other legal move.

## EU Environmental Law — Your Sharpest Lever

Where the project touches a **Natura 2000** site, a protected species (the Habitats and Birds Directives), or a **water body** (the Water Framework Directive), you hold a powerful lever: EU environmental law binds Latvia and is ultimately enforceable through the **European Commission** and the EU courts. A breach — an inadequate Natura 2000 assessment, harm to a protected species, deterioration of a water body — is a strong ground. Where domestic remedies fail, **complain to the European Commission**.

## The Courts and the Flawed EIA

The Latvian **courts** review the legality of assessments and permits: a **flawed EIA** or an unlawful permit can be **quashed**. This is a functioning EU democracy, and litigation is a real, effective tool.

## The Story, Carried Non-violently

Behind the legal tools stands the Daugava lever: a **true, culturally resonant story**, carried non-violently through the press, the song, and the peaceful gathering, that moves the public and the deciders. Pair it always with the legal case.

## The Honest Limits

Straight talk: the EU toolkit and the courts are real and effective, but an ordinary planning dispute won't carry a nation the way a fight for freedom did, Commission complaints are slow, and Latvia has its own development and energy pressures. So use the strong tools — Aarhus, EU environmental law, the courts — wrap them in a true story carried peacefully, and count a project stopped or reworked, an assessment reopened, a permit quashed, and delay as wins.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

A stack of documents wins nothing until you aim it.

**A protected site becomes an EU-law case.** Not "we oppose the project" but *"this harms a Natura 2000 site / a protected species / a water body, in breach of EU environmental law the Commission can enforce."*

**A flawed EIA becomes a quashing.** *"The assessment understated the harm and ignored its EU obligations — the permit is unlawful."*

**An Aarhus breach becomes a right asserted.** *"We were denied information / participation the Aarhus Convention guarantees."*

**A river or forest becomes a true story.** *"This is a river / forest woven into who we are — as the Daugava was, when a nation sang for it."*

Match it to the venue: the EU-protected value to the Commission and the courts; the EIA flaw to the courts; the Aarhus breach to the authority and the courts; the true story to the public and the press.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY

In Latvia, a true, culturally resonant story carried by a free press is a distinctive lever — the Daugava proved how far it reaches — and it builds the public and political weight behind the legal case.

**Lead with the true story and the value, not procedure.** Not "the EIA breached the Habitats Directive" but "a river / forest woven into who we are, and the harm a project would do it." Use your strongest material: the cultural and human weight, dated photographs, expert evidence, and any legal action banked.

**Work several audiences at once.** Latvian press and cultural channels build the public and the national feeling; EU-level attention and the European Commission reach the legal enforcement, where an EU-law breach bites. Make it easy: captioned photos, accurate summaries, a map, strong voices.

**Two hard rules.** Accuracy above all — one exaggeration can discredit everything. And carry it non-violently, through the written word, the song, the peaceful gathering — the Latvian way, and the safest.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Make them your own; keep them short, exact and calm; keep a copy of everything.

**8A — Aarhus information request (to the authority).**
*Subject: Environmental information request — [project].*
"Under the Aarhus Convention, we, [group/residents], request the EIA, the permit application, the Natura 2000 assessment, and the funding details for [project] at [location]. [Contact.]"

**8B — Objection in the EIA / consultation.**
*Subject: Objection — EIA for [project].*
"We, [group], object to [project]: [it harms a Natura 2000 site / a protected species / a water body; the EIA is flawed]. We ask that it be refused or reassessed. [Attach evidence.]"

**8C — Court challenge (to a lawyer).**
*Subject: Possible challenge — [project] EIA/permit.*
"Our community faces [project], whose EIA/permit [is flawed / breaches EU environmental law]: [specifics]. We've gathered [the EIA, the evidence]. We ask your advice on challenging it in court, invoking Aarhus. [Contact and evidence summary.]"

**8D — Complaint to the European Commission.**
*Subject: Complaint — breach of EU environmental law, [project], Latvia.*
"We report that [project] breaches [the Habitats/Birds Directive / Natura 2000 / the Water Framework Directive]: [specifics]. We ask the Commission to investigate. [Attach evidence.]"

**8E — To an environmental / cultural NGO.**
*Subject: [River/forest/site] under threat — [project].*
"Our community faces [project] threatening [an EU-protected and culturally cherished site]. We've gathered [the EIA, the value, the story]. We ask for your help with the legal case and to carry the story. [Contact and evidence summary.]"

**8F — To the developer / funder.**
*Subject: Legal and reputational risk — [project].*
"We are the community affected by [project] at [location]. It [breaches EU environmental law / rests on a flawed EIA] and faces challenge and a Commission complaint. This carries real legal and reputational risk, including to any EU funding. [Contact and evidence summary.]"

**8G — Letter to the press.**
*Subject: [Community] moves to protect [river/forest/site] from [project].*
"For generations [this river/forest] has been part of who we are in [place]. Now [project] threatens it. We can provide the story, photographs, expert evidence, and interviews. [Contact.]"

**8H — Rallying the community and the culture.**
*Subject: This is our river / our land — let's stand for it together.*
"[Project] threatens [a place woven into who we are]. Latvia knows how far a true story, carried peacefully, can reach — the Daugava, sung for by a nation, was saved, and helped set us free. With that spirit and the tools of a free EU Latvia, let's defend this too. Please join us. [Contact.]"

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Latvia's tools are strong, but no system is frictionless, and you must plan for it.

**When the domestic decision goes against you.** This is an EU democracy: use the courts, and — where EU environmental law is breached — the European Commission, which reaches above the domestic decision.

**When the story won't carry a nation.** An ordinary planning dispute won't move the country the way a fight for freedom did. Don't rely on the story alone — lean on the legal EU toolkit, and use the story to build what public weight you can.

**When development and energy pressures push.** Latvia has real ones. Meet them with the EIA, Aarhus, EU law, and the courts, and a credible, true story — not with the assumption that the state cannot be moved, which the Daugava disproved.

**When it drags.** Commission complaints and litigation are slow. Keep the coalition, the documentation, and the story alive through the long haul.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO'S CAPTURED

Latvia is a functioning EU democracy, but it has its own concerns about the capture of decisions and funds by connected interests, so this matters.

**How to spot it.** Permits against the evidence; EIAs written for the proponent; EU funds directed to connected interests; benefits to the connected while communities and habitats bear the harm.

**Who's supposed to guard against it.** Latvia's anti-corruption and audit bodies examine public money and conduct. The **courts** can quash a tainted permit. The **European Commission** can act on EU-law and EU-funds breaches. And Latvia's **free press and NGOs** have exposed captured deals.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — how the process was bent, who benefits, what EU and Latvian law required — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect. Route serious evidence to the anti-corruption bodies, the courts, the European Commission, and trusted journalists rather than making accusations you can't back. Pair the exposure with the EU-law challenge.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

Don't run these steps one after another — run them together. Your Aarhus-won documents build the case; the EU-protected value and the EIA flaws aim it; the community, NGOs, and experts are the muscle; the courts and the European Commission press it; and a true story carries the public. Together they multiply. One at a time, they stall.

**Early on (weeks 1–8):** use Aarhus to get the EIA and documents; flag the EU-protected value; object in the consultation; document the value, the flaws, and the story; build a coalition of community, NGOs, and experts.

**In the thick of it (months–year+):** challenge a flawed EIA or permit in court; complain to the European Commission if EU law is breached; carry the true story through the press.

**The long haul (year+):** press the litigation and the Commission complaint; keep the coalition and the story alive; hold the line for a project stopped or reworked, an assessment reopened, a permit quashed, and delay.

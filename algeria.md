And yet the effort is far from futile — indeed In Salah proved it real — because the fracking and the extraction threaten a scarce and irreplaceable water the whole desert depends on, because the desertification can be met by the Green Dam, because the gas footprint invites climate scrutiny and the Sahara offers solar, because the coast and the fossil aquifer can be defended, and because the community showed it can stand for its water; and those levers of the Saharan extraction, the desert, the gas, the coast, and the community are real.

Some honest truths hold across every effort here. **The water is scarce and irreplaceable — the strongest ground of opposition, as In Salah proved.** **The community can stand for its water — a real, if reversible, precedent.** **The technical people and the desert can both be marshalled.** **But the gas is central, the extraction state-led, and the space semi-closed.** Hold both truths at once: real, distinctive levers of water and community, and the honest weight of a gas-central, semi-closed state.

Above all, keep the discipline that gives these efforts their force. Document the harm and the threat to the water. Scrutinise the extraction and the fracking on the water. Hold back the desert through the Green Dam and restoration. Press the gas footprint and advance the solar. Defend the water, the aquifer, and the coast. Use the community, technical, and international channels. And stay careful and neutral on the politics — because in a vast, dry, gas-exporting, semi-closed state, an effort that scrutinises the extraction and defends the water and the desert through the scarce water, the community capacity In Salah proved, the technical rigour, and the international, carried carefully and neutrally on the politics, is the one that is both effective and just.

Algeria is a vast land that is mostly Sahara, its people gathered on the Mediterranean coast, its wealth drawn from the gas and oil beneath the desert, its water among the scarcest on earth and much of it fossil water that will not return, its great desert both a source of riches and a threat that advances on the oasis and the steppe. This guide has hidden none of that, nor pretended that the gas is not central, or that the space is open, or that the In Salah victory was permanent, and there is no lasting landmark victory to promise you. But the water is scarce and irreplaceable, and there, on the ground of the water, even a state-led extraction can be scrutinised and pressed; the desert can be held back through the Green Dam; and the community, as In Salah proved, can stand for its water and be heard. Those are the levers, and they are real: the scarce water, defended as the life it is; the desert, held back by the Green Dam and restoration; the footprint, pressed toward the Sahara's own sun; and the community and technical rigour that at In Salah forced even a state-led fracking to pause. There is no open defiance in a semi-closed state, and no permanent victory over an economy built on the desert's gas; this guide has said so throughout, and counselled care and neutrality on the politics. But the water and the desert and the oases of Algeria are worth every careful, water-grounded effort, the levers that run through the scarce water and the community are real, and the patient, technical, community-based work of documenting the threat, pressing the water case, and holding back the desert is one that Algerians — the desert communities, the technical people, and their international partners — can genuinely do, as In Salah showed, and defend the water that is life in the driest of lands. So can you.

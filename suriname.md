# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN SURINAME

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Suriname's law recognises **no collective land rights at all** — it grants indigenous and tribal peoples only a permission to use their lands "at the discretion of the State," revocable whenever the government pleases. So when Suriname sold **logging and gold-mining concessions to foreign companies on Saramaka territory** without asking, the **Saramaka** — a Maroon people on the Upper Suriname River — had no protection at home. They went over the state's head. They petitioned the **Inter-American Commission on Human Rights**, and then, in **2007, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights** ruled that Suriname had violated their rights to property and to legal personality, ordered the state to **recognise their collective ownership** of their territory, to **share the benefits** of any project, and — decisively — to obtain their **free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC)** before any large project on their land. A people with no rights under their own country's law had won, from an international court, the recognition and the consent their own state had denied them.

The Saramaka, a Maroon people who had no protection under Suriname's own law, drew on a toolkit that distinctively reaches outside the state. The **Inter-American Commission and Court of Human Rights** can be petitioned over the state's head, and did rule for the Saramaka in 2007 — ordering Suriname to recognise their collective ownership, to share the benefits of any project, and to obtain their consent; lawful petition, documentation, and international litigation are the tools. The **FPIC standard** it established means a large project on your land requires your **free, prior, and informed consent** — turning consent into a **veto**. **Collective land rights**, ordered by the Court, protect a people's territory where domestic law grants none. And the **domestic record** and the world's attention keep the pressure on. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The Inter-American ruling is a **binding order the state has been slow to fully implement** — Suriname's domestic law has lagged, and turning a court's order into real, on-the-ground recognition and consent has taken years of pressure. The **domestic ground offers little** — no collective land rights, permission revocable "at the discretion of the State" — so the strength lies outside, in the international system, which is powerful but slow and indirect. And **logging and gold-mining pressure continues** on Maroon and Indigenous lands. So aim for what this lever can deliver — collective ownership recognised, benefits shared, consent established as a veto, a concession halted for want of consent — through the Inter-American system, with the patience its slow, binding reach demands.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "the concession" is weak when home gives you nothing. Being specific — this project without consent, this denied ownership, this Inter-American standard — is strong. Answer five questions in writing.

**1. What is it, and who holds the concession?** Name the project (logging, gold-mining), the foreign company, and the state that sold the concession.

**2. Was your consent sought?** Establish that a **large project on your land** proceeded **without your free, prior, and informed consent** — **FPIC makes your consent a veto, and its absence is your sharpest lever**, as with the Saramaka.

**3. What does your own law give you?** Likely little — **no collective land rights**, permission revocable at the state's discretion — which is why the fight goes **over the state's head** to the Inter-American system.

**4. What has the Inter-American system ordered?** Collective ownership, benefit-sharing, and FPIC, as in the Saramaka ruling — the binding standard you invoke.

**5. Who's your coalition?** The **Maroon or indigenous people, lawyers, human-rights organisations, and international allies** who can carry a petition to the Inter-American Commission and Court.

**What this looks like in real life.** A logging or gold-mining concession is sold on a Maroon people's territory without their consent, their own law giving them no collective rights. The five questions yield: the foreign company and the state; a project without consent (your FPIC lever); no domestic protection (go over the state's head); the Inter-American standard of ownership and FPIC; and a coalition of lawyers and human-rights allies. That's the Saramaka pattern — assert FPIC, and reach over the state's head to the Inter-American Commission and Court.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

The difference between a people who can win over the state's head and one who can't is proof — of ownership and of the absent consent. Build it in three layers.

**Layer one: the collective ownership.** Document your **collective ownership** of the territory — the traditional occupation and use, the boundaries, the community's governance — because the Inter-American Court can order recognition of it, as it did for the Saramaka.

**Layer two: the absent consent.** Document that the **project proceeded without your free, prior, and informed consent** — the concession sold without asking — because FPIC makes your consent a veto, and its absence is the violation.

**Layer three: the domestic dead end and the standard.** Show that your **own law offered no protection** (no collective rights, revocable permission), and marshal the **Inter-American standard** (ownership, benefit-sharing, FPIC). Walking into the Inter-American Commission and Court with documented ownership, absent consent, and the domestic dead end is what wins the binding order.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY

In Suriname, international attention is the distinctive lever, because the fight's strength lies outside a state that offers little — the Inter-American system and the world's conscience. International allies make the media front reach far.

**Lead with the people, the land, and the denied consent, not procedure.** Not "the concession was irregular" but "the state sold our ancestral land to foreign loggers and miners without ever asking us." Use your strongest material: the collective ownership, the absent consent, and the Court's ruling.

**Work several audiences at once.** Domestic attention where it helps; and above all the Inter-American system, human-rights networks, and international press, where a people denied rights at home finds its strength. Make it easy: clear summaries, the ownership claim, the FPIC standard.

**Two hard rules.** Accuracy above all — one exaggeration can weaken a human-rights case. And let the people speak for their own territory and consent, in their own voice.

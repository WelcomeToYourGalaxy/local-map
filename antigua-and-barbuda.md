# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN ANTIGUA AND BARBUDA

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the land of Antigua and Barbuda, and one long fight over a displaced island's future showed that two ordinary residents, armed with the right to go to court and a regional treaty guaranteeing environmental rights, can force a nation's highest court to rein in a government that began building without permission. After a hurricane emptied Barbuda of its people, developers moved in — a private airport begun without a permit, a luxury resort on communal land — while the island's residents were still displaced. Two of them, a marine biologist and a retired teacher, refused to accept it. The government argued they had no standing even to bring a case. But in the end the highest court to which the nation answers ruled that they did have the right to challenge — rebuked the government for bypassing its own planning law, leaned for the first time in the region on the treaty that guarantees a community's environmental rights, and set a precedent that now arms every community in the Caribbean. The airport had largely been built by then; the fight goes on. But the right to fight it — and to fight the next one — was won, and written into law. The details are worth setting down, because they show how such a fight is carried even against long odds. When the hurricane struck, the whole of Barbuda was evacuated to the larger island, and in the months the people were away the bulldozers arrived: a private-jet airport rising on a site cleared without any development permit, its environmental assessment produced late and, its critics argued, unfit for the purpose. A luxury resort was pressed forward on land the community had long held in common. Against all this stood two residents — one a marine biologist who had watched the forest fall, the other a retired teacher and former member of the island’s council — backed by an international legal network willing to carry a small island’s case. The government’s first line of defence was not that the airport was lawful but that these two had no right even to ask the question, and that the case was moot because the runway was nearly finished. The highest court rejected both arguments: it held that residents may indeed challenge such decisions, chided the government for treating a village meeting years earlier as if it satisfied the planning law, and rested part of its reasoning on the regional environmental-rights treaty. The runway was there; but so, now, was the law.

The Antigua and Barbuda story teaches two things about stopping destructive projects here — including its honest limits, which this guide states plainly. The first is the pattern that runs through every guide: not a single silver bullet, but the combination of every lever — the affected community, the documentation, the coalition, the legal challenge, and the international attention — pressed together. The second is distinctly Antiguan and Barbudan, and it changed the law. **Community members have the right to challenge a government's development decisions in court — and a regional treaty guarantees them access to information, participation, and justice — so that even a project already begun can be dragged before a judge.** That power — the right to go to court, the environmental-rights treaty, the defence of communal land, and the precedent that a win sets for all who come after — is Antigua and Barbuda's distinctive lever, and this guide is built around it. What makes this lever unusual, and worth understanding precisely, is that it turns a community’s apparent weakness — that the project is already being built — into the very thing the court will examine. A government that begins without a permit, or on a flawed assessment, has not strengthened its position by building fast; it has created an unlawful act that a court can be asked to review, and, in principle, to order undone. The community’s task is therefore not to physically block the bulldozers, which it cannot, but to establish two things a court will act on: that it has the right to bring the challenge, and that the process was unlawful. The Barbuda residents won on exactly that ground — not by stopping the build, which went on, but by establishing in the highest court that they could challenge it and that the government had broken its own law, a victory that reaches far beyond the single runway.

But the same story carries honest warnings, and this guide will not hide them; they are central to this case. This was a **procedural and precedent victory, not a physical stop** — the right to challenge was won, the government was rebuked, the precedent was set, but the airport had largely been built and the fight continues. The community was **small and outmatched** — two residents against a government and wealthy developers, over many years and at real cost. And the threat came as **disaster capitalism** — development pushed through while the people were displaced and least able to resist. This guide is written with both truths in view: the real, distinctive power of the right to go to court, the environmental-rights treaty, and the precedent — and the ongoing, uphill, not-yet-finished reality that qualifies them. These qualifications are not footnotes; they are the shape of the fight, and a community should enter it expecting them. A legal system moves slowly, and a determined government and a well-funded developer can raise the runway while the pleadings are exchanged, so that by the time a court rules the physical facts on the ground have changed. Winning the right to challenge is a real and precious victory, but it is the beginning of the substantive fight, not its end; the case then returns to the lower courts to decide whether the process was in fact unlawful and what remedy, if any, should follow. A community that expects a single ruling to halt the bulldozers will be disheartened; one that understands it is winning, step by hard-won step, the right to be heard, a rebuke of the government, a precedent for the region, and — perhaps, in time — a remedy, will measure its progress truly and keep its resolve. If your fight, too, is a long legal battle against a government and developers who began before you could object, this is the guide for it.

Most communities do not know HOW to make that power effective. They watch a project begin — often without proper permits — and assume "it is already being built and we have no standing and nothing can be done," and never bring the challenge, never invoke the environmental-rights treaty, never establish that they have the right to be heard. They generate private grievance without a legal claim. The gap between a project challenged and one waved through is rarely apathy — the Barbuda case proved ordinary residents will fight for years. It is method: knowing how to establish your right to challenge, invoke the treaty, and take the government to court.

## The Strategic Framework

Effective campaigns follow the same underlying pattern, whether the goal is to stop an airport, a resort, or another destructive project:

These steps operate **simultaneously, not in sequence**. Documentation feeds the legal case and the public argument at once; the coalition grows; a court hearing or a precedent is a headline and a headline builds the coalition. The community that runs all five together, each reinforcing the others, over the long haul, is the one that wins — a right established, a government rebuked, a precedent set that protects the next community. The Barbuda case did exactly this — it documented the harm, built support at home and abroad, and took the government all the way to the highest court.

## The Levers That Are Distinctly Strong Here

Every country's system has its own pressure points. Antigua and Barbuda's give communities a distinctive set:

- **The right to go to court.** Community members can **challenge a government's development decisions**, and a court can rule the government acted unlawfully, as the Barbuda residents established. This is the lever that most distinguishes an Antiguan or Barbudan fight of this kind, and it rewards close attention. In many places a community that misses the moment of decision — that does not object before the permit is granted — finds the door closed. Here, where a government has acted unlawfully, the door can be opened even late, because the courts can review whether the government kept to its own planning law, and a project built on an unlawful foundation remains vulnerable to challenge. The community’s power lies in establishing, first, that it has standing to bring that challenge, and second, that the process was unlawful — and a court that finds both can, in principle, consider remedies that reach even a completed structure. That is a formidable lever, and it is why establishing the right to go to court is the first and most important move.
- **The environmental-rights treaty.** The **regional treaty** guarantees access to information, participation, and justice in environmental matters, as the highest court recognised in the Barbuda case.
- **Communal land.** Barbuda's tradition of **land held in common** gives its people a distinctive collective stake and standing, at the heart of the fight.
- **The highest court and the precedent.** A ruling by the **final court of appeal** binds and sets a **precedent** that arms every community after, as the Barbuda ruling did.
- **The regional and international attention.** A **precedent-setting case** draws regional and international support, as the Barbuda case drew.

The Barbuda win was a **procedural and precedent victory**, not a halted project; the community was **small and outmatched**; and the fight is **ongoing**. This guide takes those limits seriously — it centres them — and shows you how to use the real, distinctive tools Antigua and Barbuda offers — the right to go to court, the environmental-rights treaty, the defence of communal land, and the precedent — as the Barbuda residents used them, in a long legal fight that won the right to keep fighting.

## The Levels of Decision

An Antiguan or Barbudan development runs through several tracks at once, and each is a place to intervene.

**The government and the planning authority.** The **government and its development-control authority** drive and permit the project — but can be found to have acted **unlawfully** where they bypassed the planning law, as the government was found to have done over the Barbuda airport.

**The courts and the highest court.** The **courts**, up to the **final court of appeal**, can rule on whether the government acted lawfully and whether the community has the **right to challenge** — and can set a **precedent**, as the Barbuda case did. It is worth grasping why this makes the courts the pivot of so many fights of consequence here. A small community has little direct power over a determined government and a well-resourced developer; but the courts sit outside the immediate political contest, and the final court of appeal sits beyond the nation’s borders and its politics altogether. That gives a community a route around a captured or lawless domestic process: by bringing a well-documented challenge, it can compel a court to rule on whether the government acted within its powers, and can win a ruling the government cannot overturn. The other levers — the community, the press, the treaty — matter greatly, but it is the courts, up to the final court of appeal, that most often give an Antiguan or Barbudan community the leverage to force a government to answer for what it did.

**The environmental-rights treaty.** The **regional environmental-rights treaty**, distinctively, guarantees access to information, participation, and justice, and a court can rely on it, as the highest court did in the Barbuda case.

**The communal land and the council.** Barbuda's **communal land** tradition and its **local council** give the community a distinctive collective stake, at the heart of the fight.

**The community and the coalition.** The **community, the residents, and the coalition** are the force that documents the harm, brings the challenge, and carries it to the highest court — the force that changed the law over Barbuda.

## The Journey of a Project

A typical project moves from a government decision, through **permits and the planning process** (sometimes bypassed), toward construction — with the **community, the courts, and the environmental-rights treaty** able to challenge at any point, even after building has begun. **Every one of those stages is a door you can put your foot in**, and the legal challenge — the right to take the government to court — is the distinctive Antiguan and Barbudan door. The distinctive levers are the **right to go to court**, the **environmental-rights treaty**, the **communal land**, and the **precedent**. Find out exactly where the project stands, and, crucially, **whether the government followed its own planning law or bypassed it**, **whether you have the standing to challenge**, and **whether the environmental-rights treaty applies**, because those are often where a project can be challenged.

## Follow the Project, the Permits, and the Law

Behind a project stand a proponent — the government, a private developer — and the permits it needs. Trace who decides, who profits, and — importantly here — **whether the government followed the planning law or began without a proper permit**, **whether the environmental assessment was proper or flawed**, **whether the community was consulted as the law and the treaty require**, and **what the project does to the land, the ecosystem, and the communal ownership**. Two questions unlock most Antiguan and Barbudan fights of this kind: **Did the government follow its own planning law, or bypass it?** and **Do you have the standing — and the treaty rights — to challenge it in court?** The unlawful process and the right to challenge it are, together, the heart of most such campaigns. These two work together in a way particular to the fight here: the unlawful process supplies the substance — the missing permit, the flawed assessment, the absent consultation — and the right to challenge supplies the means, the standing that lets a community put that substance before a judge. A campaign that can prove the process was unlawful but lacks standing has a grievance it cannot bring; one that has standing but cannot show any unlawfulness has a key but no lock to turn it in. Joined together — a demonstrably unlawful process and a community with the recognised right to challenge it — they let a community compel a court to examine what the government did, and, where the law was broken, to say so and consider a remedy. That is the combination the Barbuda residents assembled, and it is why they prevailed at the highest court.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective campaign begins by finding the exact project and process that govern the threat — and the leverage the community holds, which here is often the unlawful process and the right to challenge it. Vague opposition goes nowhere; a fight aimed at "the airport begun without a permit, on a flawed assessment, that we have the standing and the treaty rights to challenge" is a campaign — the kind that changed the law over Barbuda. Answer five questions — and think in years.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. Did the government follow its own planning law?** This is the first Antiguan and Barbudan question of this kind. Did the project **begin with a proper permit and a proper assessment**, or was the planning law **bypassed**, as it was over the Barbuda airport. Assess this honestly and precisely at the outset, because in Antigua and Barbuda the lawfulness of the process is what most determines the strength of a legal challenge. Where a project began with a proper permit and a sound assessment, after genuine consultation, the ground for a court challenge is narrow. But where it began without a permit, or on an assessment produced late or unfit for its purpose, or without the consultation the law requires — as the Barbuda airport did — the government has created an unlawful act a court can review. A community should therefore establish, first and with the documents, exactly how the process departed from what the planning law demanded, for that departure is the foundation on which the whole challenge is built.

**2. Do you have the standing to challenge?** Can you — as a resident, a community member, a council — establish the **right to challenge** the decision in court, as the Barbuda residents established it, even over the government's objection.

**3. Does the environmental-rights treaty apply?** Does the **regional environmental-rights treaty** — guaranteeing information, participation, and justice — apply, as the highest court found it applied in the Barbuda case. This question rewards early and careful attention, because the treaty can transform a challenge. Where the regional environmental-rights treaty binds the state, it supplies a community with rights the government must honour — to be given environmental information, to take part in environmental decisions, and to reach the courts — and a breach of those rights is itself a ground a court can act on, over and above any flaw in the permit. Establishing that the treaty applies, and documenting precisely how the community’s rights under it were denied, therefore adds a powerful and distinctive dimension to the case. The Barbuda ruling’s reliance on the treaty showed its force; a community should determine early whether it can invoke the same rights.

**4. Is communal land or a fragile ecosystem at stake?** Does the project threaten **communal land** or a **fragile ecosystem**, giving the community a collective stake, as at Barbuda.

**5. Who decides, who benefits, and who can be engaged?** Which developer, official, and interest are behind it, who profits; and which courts, legal networks, councils, press, and community can be engaged.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on. A court, the treaty bodies, a government, a journalist, and the community all move on evidence, not indignation — and in Antigua and Barbuda, the unlawful process, the flawed assessment, and the harm to the land are precisely what a campaign turns into a legal case, so document them well and early. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Project, the Permits, and the Process

Get the documents the case turns on. Obtain the **project details, the permits (or their absence), and the environmental assessment** — and scrutinise them: did the project begin **without a proper permit**? Was the **assessment flawed** or unfit? Was the community **consulted** as the law and the treaty require? Establish the unlawfulness of the process precisely, because it is the ground of the challenge, as the bypassed planning law was the ground at Barbuda.

## Layer 2: The Land and the Harm

Document the reality the paper understates. Record the **value** — the ecosystem, the species (the seabirds, the marine life), the communal land, the archaeology, the heritage — as Barbuda's lagoon and its wildlife are internationally significant. Record the **harm** the project would do — the habitat destroyed, the land taken from communal ownership, the water and the wildlife affected. Photograph, map, and date it, and track it over time, because a development often proceeds while the case is fought. Record **testimony** from residents and gather **expert evidence** — a marine biologist's knowledge was central at Barbuda. This is the case that grounds the legal challenge and rouses the public. The value of the place, in particular, deserves to be documented with expert care, because a court weighing whether a development’s harm justifies scrutiny, and a public deciding whether to rally, both respond to a concrete, credible account of what is at stake. Set out, with the science and the images, exactly what the place holds — the wetlands and the lagoon, the seabird colony, the marine life, the archaeological traces, the communal land itself — so that the harm is shown to be not a matter of taste but a loss of things of recognised value. At Barbuda, the knowledge of a resident marine biologist gave the case an authority no outside consultant could match; a community should seek out and record the expert knowledge within it, for that grounded evidence is what makes both the legal claim and the public appeal credible.

## Layer 3: The Unlawful Process, the Standing, and the Treaty

Finally, document the case. Was the **process unlawful** — no permit, a flawed assessment, no consultation? Do you have the **standing** to challenge it? And does the **environmental-rights treaty** — information, participation, justice — apply and strengthen your claim? Each is a separate ground — for the courts, the treaty bodies, and the campaign — and together they make a case that is hard to wave away. This is the layer that most often decides Antiguan and Barbudan fights of this kind, because here a project is shown to have been begun unlawfully, a community is shown to have the right to challenge it, and the treaty is shown to guarantee that right — exactly the case that changed the law over Barbuda, where an unlawful process, a resident's standing, and the environmental-rights treaty together carried the day at the highest court. Assembling all three grounds together — the unlawful process, the standing to challenge, and the treaty rights — is what gives an Antiguan or Barbudan case its force, because each serves a different purpose and they reinforce one another: the unlawful process supplies the wrong to be remedied, the standing supplies the right to bring it before a judge, and the treaty supplies a binding framework of rights that both strengthens the standing and adds grounds of its own. A campaign that establishes only one may find the case turned away on another; one that establishes all three, as the Barbuda residents did, can put the wrong before the court, defeat the objection to its standing, and anchor the whole in a treaty the court will respect — which is how ordinary residents carried a government to the highest court and prevailed.

# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES — THE COURTS, THE TREATY, AND THE STANDING TO SUE

Antigua and Barbuda's courts, the environmental-rights treaty, and — distinctively — the right of a community to establish its standing are a real and powerful toolkit. You do not need to halt the project at once: a right to challenge established, a government rebuked, an unlawful process found, a treaty right recognised, a precedent set — each is a real gain, and together they can change the law. Engage lawyers and the legal networks early — but understand the tools.

## Standing: The Right to Challenge

The first and most distinctive lever is **standing** — the right to bring the challenge at all. A government may argue that a community member has no standing, or that a case is moot because the project is nearly built, as the government argued over the Barbuda airport. But the highest court can rule that residents **do** have the right to challenge a government's development decisions — and winning that right is itself a landmark, because it opens the courtroom door for this fight and the next. Establish your standing; it is the threshold, and winning it is a victory. It is worth dwelling on why the standing question matters so much, because it is easy to underrate. A government defending an unlawful project often prefers not to argue the merits at all; it argues instead that the challenger has no right to be in court — no sufficient personal interest, or no interest a court will recognise — hoping to have the case dismissed before the unlawfulness is ever examined. Defeating that argument is therefore not a technicality but a strategic victory: it forces the government to defend what it actually did, and it establishes, for every community after, that residents may hold their government to account. The Barbuda ruling did precisely this, recognising that a person may raise an environmental wrong even where the harm is to the shared environment rather than to them alone — a principle that opens the courtroom to communities across the region.

## The Environmental-Rights Treaty

The second lever, distinctive and powerful, is the **regional environmental-rights treaty**. It guarantees access to information, public participation in environmental decisions, and access to justice — and a court can **rely on it**, as the highest court did in the Barbuda case, reportedly for the first time in the region. Invoke the treaty: it strengthens your standing, your right to be consulted, and your right to challenge, and it draws the case into a regional framework of environmental rights. The treaty deserves a word of precision, because it is among the most transferable tools this guide describes. It guarantees, across the region that has adopted it, three linked rights: access to environmental information, participation in environmental decisions, and access to justice in environmental matters — together with protection for those who defend the environment. A community denied the information it was owed, or shut out of a decision it had the right to shape, or blocked from the courts, can point to the treaty as a binding standard the government has breached, and a court can rely on it, as the highest court did in the Barbuda case. Because the treaty binds many states in the region, a community anywhere within it can invoke the same rights and the same growing body of decisions — which is why establishing early whether it applies, and how it was breached, is so valuable.

## The Courts and the Unlawful Process

The third lever is the **courts and the unlawful process**. Where a project began **without a proper permit**, on a **flawed assessment**, or without lawful **consultation**, the courts can find the government acted **unlawfully** — and the remedies a court may consider can include, in principle, an order to **restore the land**. Bring the challenge through the courts, up to the **final court of appeal**, whose ruling binds and sets a **precedent** for every community after.

## Where to Turn and With Whom

The standing and the challenge go through the **courts**; the treaty through the **courts and the treaty framework**; the support through the **regional and international legal networks**. Get help early: these networks know the courts, the treaty, and the precedents, and they will support a strong, precedent-setting case, as they supported Barbuda. Help engaged early is worth far more than help sought after, and in Antigua and Barbuda the strongest strategy marries the **standing and the treaty** to the **unlawful process and the communal land**, so that a project begun unlawfully is challenged by a community with the right to be heard, under a treaty that guarantees it, all the way to the highest court — as at Barbuda.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

You have a file of evidence. Now you must turn it into arguments — because the same fact persuades a court, the treaty framework, a government, a journalist, and the community in different ways. Learning to translate your evidence for each audience is what lets a community reach every lever at once.

## One Fact, Many Arguments

Take a single documented fact — say, that a project began without a proper permit, on a flawed assessment, on communal land. That one fact becomes:

- **A legal argument:** the government acted unlawfully, bypassing its own planning law — the case that can halt or undo it.
- **A standing argument:** as an affected resident, you have the right to challenge that unlawful decision — the case that opens the courtroom door.
- **A treaty argument:** your rights to information, participation, and justice were denied — the case that invokes the regional treaty.
- **A communal-land argument:** the collective land of the community was taken or threatened without its consent — the case that grounds the collective stake.
- **A public argument:** a fragile place and a people's land were seized while they were displaced — the case that rouses the nation and the region.

Same fact, five arguments, five audiences. Go through your strongest facts and, for each, ask what it proves to each audience. That table is your campaign's argument bank — and in Antigua and Barbuda the arguments that most often decide things are the **legal** one, because the unlawful process is the ground, and the **standing and treaty** ones, because they win the right to make the case at all. Keep the arguments anchored to the same documented facts, so a claim made to a reporter can be backed in the record and a claim made to a court can be proven; consistency across audiences is itself persuasive. And be honest about the state of the fight: acknowledge that a project may be built while the case proceeds, and argue that the right to challenge, the rebuke, and the precedent are victories that reach beyond the single project, rather than overstating a physical stop that has not come.

## Match the Argument to the Audience

**The courts** want the unlawfulness of the process, the basis for standing, and the treaty rights framed precisely, with the documents that prove them.

**The treaty framework** wants the denial of information, participation, and justice framed against the treaty's guarantees.

**The government** responds to the prospect of an unlawful-process finding, a rebuke, and a precedent it cannot control.

**Journalists** need a specific, vivid story with a hook: a people's land built on while they were displaced, and two residents who took the government to the highest court.

**The community** needs the argument clear and stirring: our communal land, our island, our right to be heard. The public argument, though it moves people most, must stay anchored to the documented facts if it is to serve the other fronts as well. The injustice of a people’s land built on in their absence, and the pride of an island in its communal tradition, rouse a community rightly; but the same case, stated precisely — the missing permit, the flawed assessment, the specific rights denied — is also what a court requires. A campaign that lets the public argument drift into exaggeration hands the government and the developer an easy rebuttal and weakens the case built on the same ground. Keep the public argument vivid but true, so that the story that stirs the community and the evidence that moves a court are one and the same, drawn from a single reliable record built and maintained across the years of the fight.

**The regional environmental-justice world** responds to a precedent-setting case that arms communities across the region. Because this audience thinks in terms of precedent and rights, a campaign gains by framing its case in those terms. The regional environmental-justice community — the lawyers, the networks, the advocates — is moved not only by the harm to a single place but by what a case can establish for every community after: a principle of standing, a reading of the treaty, a remedy that others can seek. A campaign that can show its case raises such a question, and could set such a precedent, speaks directly to what this community exists to advance, and is far more likely to draw the expertise, the support, and the attention that a lone community cannot generate. The Barbuda case drew exactly that support because it promised a precedent for the whole region.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Keep the record and the proof of the unlawful process, keep the community united, assert your standing, invoke the treaty, and treat every hearing won, every right established, every rebuke of the government, and every precedent set as a stone in a wall around your land — and around the land of every community that will invoke your case after you.

The tools are here, and the Barbuda case itself proved them, honestly and incompletely: a people's communal land built on while they were displaced by the strongest hurricane ever seen in the open Atlantic, a private airport begun without a permit on a flawed assessment, and two residents — a marine biologist and a retired teacher — who refused to accept it, who established, against the government's objection and all the way at the highest court, their right to challenge, whose case leaned for the first time in the region on the treaty that guarantees a community's environmental rights, and whose victory set a precedent that now arms every community in the Caribbean; the airport built by then, the fight going on, but the right to fight it won and written into law. What decides the outcome, in the end, is the right to be heard, the environmental-rights treaty, the collective stake in communal land, and the endurance of ordinary people — and those a determined community can summon and sustain. Begin today, begin with the unlawful process and the standing and the treaty, and build from there. Communities before you have won the right to challenge a government and set a precedent for a region — a right established, a government rebuked, a treaty relied on, a wall of law built for all who follow. What the residents of Barbuda won can be won again. So can yours.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN THE REPUBLIC OF CONGO

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Beneath the vast swamp forests of the northern Republic of Congo lies the **Cuvette Centrale**, the single largest tropical peatland found anywhere on earth — and what happens to it matters not only to the Congolese who live there but to the stability of the whole planet's climate. Shared with the neighbouring Democratic Republic of Congo, it holds on the order of **thirty billion tonnes of carbon** — as much as the entire world emits from fossil fuels in roughly three years — locked safely away in waterlogged peat beneath one of the great tropical rainforests, sheltering lowland gorillas, forest elephants, and rare life, and sustaining the fishing and forest communities who have always lived along its rivers. And it is threatened: the government has held and awarded oil exploration blocks that overlap the peatlands, on the basis of an environmental assessment so old that it predated the peatlands' own scientific discovery, while industrial logging presses at the forest's edge. No threat here has been finally beaten. But the peatlands' global value, and the bargain that could keep their carbon in the ground, give real ground.

Those who would protect the Cuvette Centrale work with real, if constrained, tools — worked carefully and largely through the international and the inside the system. The peatlands' **global climate value** — thirty billion tonnes of carbon — is the foundational, evidence-based argument, bringing the world's climate movement, finance, and diplomacy to bear. The **state's own commitments to protect the peatlands** — the pledges it has signed, and its wish to be seen as a steward of the Congo Basin — can be held to, on its own terms. The **climate-finance bargain** — the world paying to keep the carbon in the ground rather than have it drilled — is a genuine lever that meets the nation's fair development need without the oil. The **weak economics and the flawed assessment** make peatland oil a bad and legally weak bet: the reserves are doubtful, and the environmental assessment underpinning the blocks predates the peatlands' own scientific discovery, so it can be challenged. And **pressure on the oil companies and their financiers**, through their climate and environmental standards, reaches the drilling from outside. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The Republic of Congo is a genuinely poor, oil-dependent nation that badly needs revenue, and its government makes a fair argument that it has the same right to develop its resources that industrialised nations used. The political space to oppose is constrained, with real limits on dissent. And no threat here has been finally beaten. So the work must be careful, international-channelled, and framed around the global stakes, the state's own commitments, and the fair finance bargain — never around challenging authority — protecting the forest and Indigenous communities and any advocate whose position could be jeopardised. And the honest path is not to deny a poor nation its development, but to fund a better one — so aim for the drilling scrutinised and slowed, the state held to its pledges, and the finance bargain advanced that keeps the carbon in the ground, not an open defeat of a needed revenue source.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

You cannot justly deny a poor nation its development, nor safely mount open confrontation in a constrained state. What you can do is make the global bargain: press the peatlands' climate value, hold the state to its own commitments, challenge the drilling's weak economics and its assessment that predated the peatlands' discovery, pressure the oil companies and financiers — and, above all, advance the climate finance that pays the nation to keep the carbon in the ground rather than drill it. The honest path is to fund a better development, not to forbid development itself.

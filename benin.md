# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

In Benin the fundamental challenge is unusual, because the source of the harm is at the same time the source of the whole nation's living: **cotton**, the "white gold" that is the backbone of the national economy and the livelihood of more than **two million people**, together with the ambitious industrialisation now built upon it. Benin is one of Africa's leading cotton producers — cotton accounts for something like **two-fifths of the national economy** and the great majority of its export earnings, and over two million Beninese, most of them smallholder farmers working just a couple of hectares each, depend on it directly. That deep dependence on a single crop is the country's great strength and its great vulnerability at once, because the **pesticide-heavy cotton monoculture** degrades the fertile soil and harms the health of those who grow it, and the flagship **industrial zone** now processing the raw cotton into finished cloth brings real jobs and real value but also pressing questions — about the land it occupies, the water it uses and returns, and the conditions of those who labour within it.

A community in Benin works with real but indirect levers, because the aim here is not to oppose cotton — the nation's living — but to make it sustainable and fair. The **case for sustainable and diversified cotton** — less pesticide, more organic, healthier soil — is both an environmental and an economic argument. The **health-and-organic alternative** offers farmers a better path. The **industrial zone** can be scrutinised on its **land, water, and labour**. And the **international supply chain** — the buyers of Benin's cotton and cloth — cares about sustainability and fair labour, which gives outside leverage. Behind all of it stand the two million farmers, whose moral and practical authority to reform the economy that is theirs is the real foundation. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Cotton is the **backbone of the economy and the livelihood of over two million people**, so any effort must protect, not threaten, that living, and offer genuinely sustainable alternatives rather than mere opposition. The government — and a **president who made his own fortune in cotton** — are firmly committed to the cotton-and-textile model as the road to national development. The industrial zone brings **genuine jobs and value** even as its terms are seriously questioned. And Benin, long a democratic model in the region, has seen its **political space narrow**, so any effort must work with real care through farmers' organisations, unions, civil society, and international channels. So aim for what these levers can deliver — carefully, constructively, and in the farmers' own interest.

## The Whole Strategy in One Idea

The harm looks inseparable from the living, but a cotton economy can be reformed link by link, and a chain has a weakest link. A Beninese effort turns on the **sustainable-cotton case**; on the **health-and-organic alternative**; on the **scrutiny of the industrial zone's land, water, and labour**; on the **two million farmers** whose economy it is; and on the **international supply chain**. Every one of those is a link you can grab — and reform in the farmers' own interest runs through them all. **Find the one closest to your hand and pull.**

## Be Honest About Which Fight You're In

Before you spend months on this, work out your real situation:

- **If it's the pesticide and the soil, the sustainable-and-organic case is your sharpest lever** — it protects the farmers' health, soil, and often their income, so it works with the grain of their interest.
- **If it's the industrial zone, scrutinise its land, water, and labour** — the questions are real, and the zone brings genuine value, so press for fair terms, not closure.
- **If the buyers care about sustainability and fair labour, the supply chain is a pressure point** — international buyers fear a cotton dug from degraded soil and unfair work.
- **If the state is deeply invested and the space narrowing, don't oppose the nation's living** — reform it in the farmers' interest, carefully, through their own organisations.

Read the whole guide once before you move. Two habits run through it: **frame everything as reform in the farmers' own interest, not opposition to the nation's living**, and **work carefully through the farmers' organisations, the alternatives, and the supply chain**. That is the one path that can actually succeed in a country whose living is cotton.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "cotton" is self-defeating. Being specific — this pesticide harm, this zone's water use, this labour condition, this fairer model — is right. Answer five questions in writing.

**1. What is the harm, and how does it run through the farmers' own hands?** The pesticide monoculture (the farmers apply it to grow their living), the zone's land/water/labour (the workers need the jobs) — so the effort must reform, not oppose.

**2. What would a fairer, more sustainable model look like?** Less pesticide, more organic, healthier soil, fair zone terms — **the reform to press, in the farmers' own interest.**

**3. What stage is it at, and what's the next opening?** A cotton-season decision, a zone expansion or its terms, a certification or buyer's requirement, a health or soil concern. These are the openings.

**4. Who buys the cotton and cloth?** The **international buyers and certifiers** who care about sustainability and fair labour — the supply-chain lever.

**5. Who's the coalition — and how to work with care?** The **farmers' organisations and cooperatives, the unions, civil society, the certifiers and buyers** — and a plan to work carefully in a narrowing space, protecting the farmers and those who organise.

**What this looks like in real life.** A pesticide-heavy cotton monoculture degrades the soil and harms the growers' health, while the industrial zone raises questions of land, water, and labour. The five questions yield: harm that runs through the farmers' own hands; a fairer, more sustainable model to press; a cotton-season or zone opening; the buyers who want sustainable cotton; and a coalition of cooperatives, unions, and civil society. That's the Beninese pattern — reform in the farmers' interest, through the sustainable case, the alternative, the zone scrutiny, and the supply chain.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION

The difference between an effort that can reform and one that can't is proof — of the harm, and of the better path. Build it in three layers.

**Layer one: the harm to soil and health.** Document the **pesticide harm** — the soil degradation, the health effects on the growers — and the **zone's land, water, and labour** questions, with evidence and testimony. This grounds both the reform and the supply-chain case.

**Layer two: the better model.** Document the **sustainable-and-organic alternative** — the lower-input, healthier-soil, fairer path — and its benefits to the farmers' health and income, because the reform must be shown to serve them.

**Layer three: the supply-chain and coalition map.** Map the **buyers and certifiers** of Benin's cotton and cloth, and the **farmers' organisations, cooperatives, and unions** — so you know whom to reach and how to organise. Walking into the farmers' organisations, the certifiers, the buyers, or the zone authorities with the harm, the better model, and the coalition is what makes reform move.

Benin has real concerns about the concentration of the cotton-and-textile economy and the handling of the industrial zone, so this matters — but tread carefully in a narrowing space.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — how terms favour the connected, who benefits, what a fairer model would look like — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect.

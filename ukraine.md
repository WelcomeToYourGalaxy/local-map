# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN UKRAINE

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action changes project outcomes in Ukraine, and Ukrainian communities recently proved it in a way few countries in this series can match: when wartime measures narrowed environmental assessment and closed public access to the register, Ukrainian environmental organisations did not accept it — they campaigned, took part in the Ministry's redrafting, and brought the matter to the **Aarhus Convention Compliance Committee**, whose recommendations helped produce amendments that restored the scope of assessment, including for reconstruction projects, and created a register open to the public again. Participation rights, lost in an emergency, were argued back — a rare and instructive thing. And Ukrainian communities have a tool most of the countries in this series lack: **everything happens on a public online register**. Under the **Law on Environmental Impact Assessment No. 2059-VIII**, adopted in 2017 to transpose the European Union's assessment directive and replace the Soviet-era system of "state ecological expertise," a developer proposing a planned activity must publish a **notification**, prepare an **EIA report**, and go through **public discussion including mandatory public hearings** before the authority issues an **EIA conclusion** — which must be taken into account before any permit for the activity can be granted, and whose breach can bring the **suspension or termination** of the activity. There is no shortage of hard truths here — a full-scale war above all. But the tools are real, and they have just been defended in the hardest of circumstances.

A community in Ukraine works with genuinely strong legal tools, at war and rebuilding, and they are real. The distinctive lever is the **public Unified Register of Environmental Impact Assessment and the binding EIA process**: under Law No. 2059-VIII, a project's notification, EIA report, mandatory public hearings, the comments received, and the EIA conclusion are all held on a public register; the authority is required to consider the comments; and the conclusion must be taken into account before any permit — everything on the record where a court can see it, with the developer, not the public, bearing the cost of the public discussion. Behind it sits the **Aarhus Convention** on access to information, public participation, and access to justice — and, as the wartime restoration proved, its Compliance Committee can argue rights back. The **courts** can annul an EIA conclusion reached through defective procedure; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful. The reconstruction now opening can be held to the **restored assessment and the international reconstruction partners' safeguards**, so the rebuilding is done right. And the **transboundary assessment** reaches cross-border harms. These tools are general — the public register and binding EIA, the Aarhus rights, the courts, and the reconstruction safeguards reach any planned activity, from a factory to a reconstruction project. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly, for the counterweight is substantial. Ukraine is fighting a full-scale war, and in areas affected by hostilities or occupation none of the ordinary mechanisms operate normally. Martial law has justified derogations, and the balance between transparency and security is unsettled. Reconstruction will bring enormous pressure to build fast, and the temptation to shorten procedure with it. Institutions are stretched, and the pre-war record of enforcement was uneven. And a community whose comments are considered and rejected still needs a court to go further. So winning here rarely means a project's outright abandonment — more often a negative EIA conclusion, or one so conditioned the project changes; commitments written into the conclusion and enforced through permits; or a defective procedure identified and the conclusion annulled by a court. Aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver — a project scrutinised on the public record, its conclusion conditioned or annulled, the reconstruction built right, the rights defended as they were before — through the register, the binding EIA, the Aarhus rights, and the courts.

You win here by working the public record the law creates. # HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

## Who Actually Decides

A Ukrainian project runs through a public, EU-modelled assessment system. The **environmental authority** issues the binding **EIA conclusion** after the public discussion. The **Unified Register** holds the whole record. The **permit-issuing bodies** cannot grant a permit without taking the conclusion into account. The **courts** can annul a conclusion reached through defective procedure. And the **Aarhus Convention and its Compliance Committee** stand behind the participation rights. Map who issues the conclusion and the permit, and check the register, before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

A planned activity moves through notification, the EIA report, the public discussion and mandatory hearings, the EIA conclusion, and then the permit. Your entry point is the public discussion: the moment to put comments into the register that the authority is required to consider, and that a court can later review. Beyond it lie the conditioned or negative conclusion, the permit stage, and — if the procedure was defective — the courts. Enter at the public discussion, on the public record.

## Follow the Money and the Permissions

Follow the register: the notification, EIA report, hearings, comments, and conclusion, all public. Follow the conclusion: whether it is negative, conditioned, or clean, and whether the permit took it into account. Follow the procedure: whether the notification, hearings, and consideration of comments were properly done — a defect can annul the conclusion. Follow the reconstruction: which international partners fund it and what safeguards they bring. The leverage lies in the register, the binding conclusion, the procedure, and the reconstruction safeguards.

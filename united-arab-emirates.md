# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN THE UNITED ARAB EMIRATES

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Concern for the environment in the United Arab Emirates turns, in truth, on a genuine and revealing paradox, and the levers here follow from it: the Emirates is at once one of the world's highest per-capita consumers and a growing oil and gas producer, and a self-styled leader of the green transition, with real investments in renewables, a net-zero pledge written into law, and the hosting of a landmark global climate summit to its name. That paradox is the distinctive ground of environmental effort here, because the strongest lever is to hold a green-ambitious petrostate to its own commitments. The Emirates is a wealthy, hyper-developed federation of seven emirates on the shore of a stressed and shallow Gulf, its water drawn almost entirely from the sea by energy-intensive desalination, its coast reshaped by the great artificial islands it has dredged and built out over the shallow waters, its footprint among the heaviest on earth — and yet a nation that has planted mangroves by the million, invested billions in solar and nuclear power, pledged net zero by mid-century, and presided over a summit that called on the world to transition away from fossil fuels. There is no landmark community victory to point to, and the political space is closed. But the levers, resting on the nation's own commitments and its image-consciousness, are real, and can be used, with care.

A community in the UAE works cautiously, through technical, internal, and international channels, holding a green-ambitious petrostate to its own word — and the tools are real. The **desert and coastal biodiversity** can be conserved through the conservation frameworks. The **water-security argument** is real: in a nation drawing its water almost entirely from energy-intensive desalination of a stressed Gulf, a project that wastes or strains it is self-defeating. And the nation's **image-conscious, globally-engaged posture** gives genuine international, technical, and reputational leverage. These tools are general — the green accountability, the assessment and designations, the water-security argument, and the international frameworks reach the country's harms, from the fossil expansion to a harmful reclamation.

# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN URUGUAY

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action decides project outcomes in Uruguay, and Uruguay offers proof that few countries can match. In 2002 the government signed a letter of intent with the International Monetary Fund committing to privatise the country's drinking-water and sanitation services. Rather than accept it, a coalition of unions, environmental groups, academics, artists, and ordinary citizens formed the **Comisión Nacional en Defensa del Agua y de la Vida (CNDAV)** and turned to a tool Uruguay's constitution puts directly in the people's hands: they gathered the signatures of a tenth of the entire electorate and forced a national vote to amend the Constitution itself. On **31 October 2004, close to two-thirds of Uruguayans voted "yes,"** writing into **Article 47** that access to water and sanitation is a fundamental human right, that these services must be provided **exclusively and directly by the state**, and that the social use of water comes before the commercial. Water privatisation was banned outright, and existing private contracts wound down — the first time any nation had enshrined the human right to water in its constitution by direct democracy, a precedent later echoed around the world. And the win still bites hard, decades later: two decades later, that same Article 47 was the ground on which the courts and public pressure halted and ultimately killed the **Neptuno** project, a scheme that would have let a private consortium treat much of the capital's drinking water. A people rewrote their own constitution and stopped a privatisation outright, because they organised, endured, and used the levers the system placed in their hands. The numbers are worth knowing. The CNDAV formed in 2002 in response to the government's IMF commitment; it ran a four-year grassroots campaign and gathered the signatures of roughly a tenth of the electorate to put a constitutional amendment on the ballot. The vote came on 31 October 2004, alongside the general election, and about 64 per cent said yes — turning an amendment drafted by activists into Article 47 of the Constitution. Existing private water contracts were wound down, and no future ones could be signed; six years later the United Nations itself recognised water as a human right, with Uruguay cited as the pioneer that had done it by popular vote.

The water victory teaches two things about winning in Uruguay. The first is the pattern: not a single silver bullet, but the combination of every lever — direct democracy, the environmental-assessment process and its public hearings, the independent courts and audit bodies, the constitutional shields, mobilisation, and national attention — pressed until a project became impossible. The second is distinctly Uruguayan. This is a small, stable, unusually law-governed democracy in which the people hold **real direct-democracy tools** — the power to **repeal a law by referéndum** and to **amend the Constitution by plebiscito** — backed by genuinely independent courts and one of the region's strongest rule-of-law traditions. Those tools, and that institutional honesty, give Uruguayan communities levers that are rare anywhere.

Most communities, though, do not know HOW to make opposition effective. They attend one hearing and go home. They assume the project "is decided in Montevideo," and so they never make a substantive submission at the audiencia pública, never scrutinise the environmental authorisation, never invoke the constitutional shields, never organise the signatures for a referéndum, never build the mobilisation that changes a decision. They generate a burst of concern without a plan to sustain it. They gather a crowd and lose it to exhaustion. The gap between a community that stops a project and one that watches it get built is rarely courage or numbers. It is method.

**This guide shows what actually works — inside the Uruguayan system, and, when the fight is big enough, above it.**

## The Strategic Framework

Effective opposition campaigns follow the same underlying pattern, whichever project they face:

```
STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION  (find the decision and its weak point)
        |
STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION          (build proof of harm and of every defect)
        |
STEP 3: LOCAL OPPOSITION        (organise a movement that lasts)
        |
STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES        (the EIA, the courts, the constitutional shields, direct democracy)
        |
STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY         (make the fight visible and impossible to ignore)
```

These steps operate **simultaneously, not in sequence**. Documentation feeds the legal case and the media story at once; the movement sustains the pressure that decision-makers and courts respond to; a challenge is a headline and a headline strengthens a challenge. The community that runs all five together, each reinforcing the others, is the one that wins. The CNDAV did exactly this — it documented the harm of privatisation, organised a durable four-year national movement, built the legal and constitutional case, and made water a national cause, all at once, until the vote was won.

## The Levers That Are Distinctly Strong Here

Every country's system has its own pressure points. Uruguay's give communities a distinctive set:

- **Direct democracy.** Uruguayans can **repeal a law** by collecting signatures for a **referéndum**, and **amend the Constitution** by **plebiscito** — the tool that banned water privatisation in 2004. It is demanding, but it is real, and it belongs to the people. No government, and no parliament, has the final word on a question the people are willing to decide themselves — which is a kind of backstop most communities elsewhere can only dream of.
- **Constitutional shields.** **Article 47** protects water as a human right and a public service; the Constitution and Uruguay's rights framework can be invoked against projects that cross those lines.
- **Environmental assessment with public hearings.** Significant projects need an **autorización ambiental previa** from the environment ministry under Law 16.466, based on an impact study and, often, a mandatory **audiencia pública** — a formal entry point for evidence and objection.
- **Independent courts and audit bodies.** Uruguay's judiciary, its administrative-review court, and its **Tribunal de Cuentas** are genuinely independent and have checked projects — as the Neptuno case showed.
- **The Escazú Agreement.** Uruguay has ratified Escazú, strengthening rights to environmental information, participation, justice, and the protection of defenders.
- **A deep civic tradition.** Uruguay has a strong, organised civil society — unions, cooperatives, environmental and neighbourhood groups — with a habit of collective action.

## What This Guide Will Not Pretend

It would be dishonest to promise these levers always prevail. Direct democracy is **powerful but slow and high-threshold** — a referéndum needs the support of a quarter of all registered voters, a constitutional plebiscito the signatures of a tenth, and a national campaign to match; it suits national issues far better than a single local project. Uruguay's countryside is dominated by **agribusiness and forestry** — soy, cattle, and vast eucalyptus plantations feeding giant pulp mills — and those interests, and the foreign investment behind them, carry great weight; the pulp mills went ahead despite years of protest, and monoculture strains the very water the Constitution protects. And a small economy hungry for investment can see the State itself become the proponent. This guide takes those facts seriously, names them plainly, and shows you how to win anyway — and, where an outright stop is not realistic, how to secure the delay, the conditions, the exposure, and the worn-down proponent that, in a hard fight, are often what winning actually looks like.

# HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

You cannot pull a lever you cannot see. Before you spend a single weekend, map who actually decides your project's fate, at which level, and where along that path the decision is weakest.

## The Levels of Decision

A Uruguayan development project runs through several tracks at once, and each is a place to intervene.

**Environmental authorisation.** For most significant projects — mines, pulp mills, large agribusiness, infrastructure, water schemes — the decisive permit is the **autorización ambiental previa (AAP)** from the **Ministerio de Ambiente**, under the environmental-impact law (Law 16.466), based on an impact study and, for high-impact projects, a mandatory **audiencia pública**. This is where most projects live or die administratively.

**The national government and Parliament.** Because Uruguay is small and centralised, big projects are often driven from the national level — a ministry, the executive, sometimes a law passed by Parliament. That makes national politics, and the direct-democracy tools that check it, unusually important.

**The courts and audit bodies.** Administrative acts can be challenged for annulment before the **Tribunal de lo Contencioso Administrativo (TCA)**; rights violations by an **acción de amparo**; and public spending and contracts are reviewed by the **Tribunal de Cuentas** — all genuinely independent.

**The people, directly.** Uniquely, the electorate can act as a decision-maker: repealing a law by **referéndum** or amending the Constitution by **plebiscito**.

## The Journey of a Project

A typical project moves from a proposal, to the **environmental impact study and the AAP** at the Ministerio de Ambiente, through any **audiencia pública**, into the **courts** (the TCA or an amparo) if challenged, with national politics — and, for the biggest questions, direct democracy — running alongside, and only then to construction. **Every one of those stages is a door you can put your foot in.** The earliest doors are the cheapest and widest: a substantive submission at the audiencia pública, a request for the file, evidence that the study is deficient. The later doors — an amparo, an annulment before the TCA, a constitutional argument, a referéndum campaign — are more demanding but real, and Uruguayans walk through them and win. Find out exactly where your project stands on this path today; that tells you which door is open now.

## Follow the Money and the Permit

Behind the project stand a proponent — often a foreign investor (a mining or forestry multinational), a large agribusiness, or the State itself — its financiers and buyers, and the ministry that authorised it. Trace who owns and funds the proponent, who buys its output, and who granted the AAP. Two questions unlock most Uruguayan fights: **Was it lawful — the impact study, the public hearing, the constitutional limits on water — and were the impacts honestly assessed?** and **Who benefits from pretending they were?** Because so many big projects rest on foreign investment and Uruguay's clean international image, the financiers, buyers, and that reputation are often reachable pressure points. Many of Uruguay's largest projects — the pulp mills above all — rest on foreign capital and on the country's marketing of itself as a stable, clean, rule-of-law place to invest; that image is an asset the government and the investors both guard, and a well-documented conflict with the Constitution or a public water source is precisely the kind of thing that tarnishes it. Mapping who funds and buys from a project therefore reveals pressure points that sit far from the ministry that issued the permit.

## The Overseers

Finally, know the bodies that watch the deciders, because they are levers too. The **TCA** reviews the legality of administrative acts; the ordinary courts hear amparos; the **Tribunal de Cuentas** audits contracts and spending — it questioned the Neptuno deal. The **Institución Nacional de Derechos Humanos (INDDHH)** examines rights and, under Escazú, the protection of environmental defenders. And an active press and civil society watch throughout. Each overseer is a separate front, reachable with the same evidence, and a project rarely survives pressure on several at once. You do not have to choose one door: the same well-documented file can go to the Ministerio de Ambiente, the ordinary courts, the TCA, the Tribunal de Cuentas, the INDDHH, the foreign investors, and the press at once, and each front that opens makes the others harder to close. A Uruguayan campaign's power comes not from any single lever but from applying several at the same time — with the constitutional shields, and behind them the people, as the ones that reach furthest.

## What Uruguayan Campaigns Actually Show

A few patterns recur. **Direct democracy can rewrite the rules** — the 2004 vote banned water privatisation for good. It is the rarest and heaviest lever a community can hold: the power not merely to challenge a decision but to change the rules under which every future decision is made. **Constitutional shields endure and keep winning** — Article 47 stopped Neptuno two decades later. **The courts and audit bodies are real checks** — they questioned and halted Neptuno. **Reputation and investors are reachable** — a clean-image, investment-dependent State feels scrutiny. **But direct democracy is slow and national**, agribusiness and pulp dominate the countryside, and the State often backs big investment. The through-line: **submit substantively at the audiencia pública; scrutinise the AAP; invoke the constitutional shields, above all on water; use the courts and the Tribunal de Cuentas; and, for a national-scale threat, organise the direct-democracy campaign — while mobilising the public and reaching the investors — and expect a fight measured in delay, conditions, and exposure as much as an outright stop.**

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Every effective campaign begins by finding the exact decision that governs the project — and its weakest point. Vague opposition to "the mine" goes nowhere; opposition to "the autorización ambiental previa granted on an impact study that understated the threat to a public water source, without a proper audiencia pública, in tension with Article 47" is a case. Answer five questions.

## The Five Core Questions

**1. What exactly is proposed, and under what permit?** Name the project, the proponent, and the specific instrument it needs or holds — the **autorización ambiental previa** from the Ministerio de Ambiente, any concession, contract, or law. Each has conditions and a paper trail, and each is a place a defect hides.

**2. What stage is it at?** Impact study under review? AAP applied for? Audiencia pública held or pending? Authorisation granted? In the courts? Under construction? The stage dictates the door. If the hearing is pending, your submission and evidence are the cheap, powerful route now; if the AAP is granted, an amparo or a TCA annulment is the route.

**3. Does it touch a constitutional shield — above all water?** This is often the decisive Uruguayan question. Does the project threaten a **public water source**, or put water treatment or supply in private hands, engaging **Article 47**? Water is the strongest shield there is, as Neptuno's opponents proved; identify early whether your project crosses it. And be precise about how it crosses: does it hand the extraction, treatment, or supply of water to a private party; does it endanger a public source or aquifer; does it deny the public participation Article 47 requires? Each is a distinct way of crossing the line, and naming the right one sharpens both the legal case and the public argument.

**4. Is the study or process defective, and is the threat national?** Was the **impact study deficient** — understating the water, the forest, the soil, the cumulative effects? Was the **audiencia pública** a formality? And is the threat **national in scale or principle** (like water privatisation) — the kind of issue that could sustain a referéndum or plebiscito — or purely local, where the courts and the AAP are the better route?

**5. Who decides, who benefits, and who is watching?** Which ministry authorised it; who owns, funds, and buys from the proponent (including foreign investors); which communities and organisations are engaged; and which environmental groups, lawyers, press, and international allies can be reached. This maps your levers and your audiences at once.

## A Worked Example

Suppose a foreign company proposes a large mine or water scheme that threatens a public water source, with the impact study under review and the AAP not yet granted.

Working the five questions: the project needs an **AAP** (pending — get the study and act). It is **in environmental review**, so a substantive submission at the audiencia pública is the live route now, with an amparo and a TCA annulment in reserve. The **threat to public water** engages **Article 47** — the strongest shield. The **study** may understate the water harm, and the threat may be **national in principle** (privatising or endangering a public water source), which could sustain a direct-democracy campaign. The ministry decides, so the **constitutional shield, the courts, and the public** carry more weight than quiet lobbying.

Within a weekend you have moved from "we oppose the mine" to a target list: obtain the impact study and make a substantive audiencia submission; build the Article 47 water argument; prepare an amparo and a TCA annulment; take the contract to the Tribunal de Cuentas if apt; assess whether the threat is national enough for a referéndum; identify the foreign investors and the reputational angle; and prepare the water-and-community story for the press. That is a campaign, not a complaint. Notice what the exercise did: it converted a diffuse fear into a short list of concrete, assignable tasks — get the study, submit at the hearing, build the Article 47 case, ready the amparo, weigh a referendum — each tied to a specific lever and each of which can begin this week, several of them before the authorisation is even granted. That is the difference the five questions make.

# STEP 2: DOCUMENTATION — HOW TO BUILD AN UNASSAILABLE CASE

Documentation is the foundation everything else stands on. A judge, a TCA magistrate, an environment official, a journalist, and a project's investors all move on evidence, not indignation. Build your file in three layers.

## Layer 1: The Official Record

Get the documents the decision rests on. Obtain the **environmental impact study and the AAP** from the Ministerio de Ambiente; the **audiencia pública record**; any **concession or contract** (and, for a public-money project, the Tribunal de Cuentas file); and the water and land permits. Much is public, and obtainable through Uruguay's access-to-public-information law. The gap between what the study claims — an adequate assessment, a genuine hearing, no threat to public water — and what the science and the Constitution require is where cases are won. The Neptuno challenge turned on exactly this: the documented conflict between a private-run water scheme and Article 47's demand that water be managed "exclusively and directly" by the State. Read the impact study against the grain, for what it quietly concedes and omits — the aquifer modelled optimistically, the downstream users undercounted, the cumulative effect of surrounding plantations or farms declined, the water demand softened. It is written by consultants paid to secure the authorisation; read against its own annexes and against an independent scientist's eye, it is usually where the strongest objection is hiding in plain sight.

## Layer 2: The Ground Truth

Document the reality the paper hides. Photograph and date the **actual site** — the river, the aquifer, the wetland, the fields, the community, the footprint — and geotag it. Log **incidents**: pollution, a lowered water table, works beyond permit. Capture **before-and-after** with your own photos and with freely available satellite imagery, to show clearing or the true scale. Record **the water and its use** — the public source, the supply, who depends on it — because water is the strongest ground in Uruguay. Add independent technical readings — a hydrologist's, an agronomist's, an ecologist's — where you can get them; Uruguay's environmental movements have a strong tradition of working with committed scientists. Record **testimony** from residents, farmers, and experts.

## Layer 3: The Defects and the Rights

Finally, document the failures of process and the constitutional lines the project crosses. Does it threaten a **public water source** or put water in private hands, in tension with **Article 47**? Was the **impact study deficient** — understating the water, the forest, the soil, the cumulative effects? Was the **audiencia pública** a formality, denying the participation the law and Article 47 require? Was a **contract or concession** unlawful or fiscally unsound? Each defect is a separate ground — for a submission, an amparo, a TCA annulment, an audit complaint, a constitutional argument, or a direct-democracy campaign — and together they make a case that is hard to wave away. This is the layer that most often decides Uruguayan fights, because here a project that looked authorised is revealed to cross the water shield, to rest on a whitewashed study, or to have skipped the participation the Constitution guarantees. Work it methodically against each requirement: does the project threaten a public water source or hand water to private hands, contrary to Article 47; did the impact study honestly assess the water, the forest, the soil, and the cumulative effects; was the audiencia pública genuine and its concerns answered; is the contract lawful and fiscally sound? Where the answer is no, capture the proof — because each 'no' is a separate ground the Ministerio de Ambiente, the courts, the Tribunal de Cuentas, or, on a national question, the people can act on, and the Article 47 water ground is the one that reaches furthest.

# WHAT TO GATHER, AND WHERE TO FIND IT

This is the practical companion to Step 2: the specific records that decide Uruguayan cases, and where to obtain them. You will not need every item; you will need the few that expose your project's particular defect.

## The Permits and Their Paper Trail

- **The environmental impact study and the AAP**, from the Ministerio de Ambiente. Read the study for what it understates — the water source and aquifer, the forest and soil, the community, the cumulative effect of surrounding agribusiness or forestry.
- **The audiencia pública record** — who spoke, what was asked, and whether concerns were answered, or whether the hearing was a formality (or never properly held).
- **The concession, contract, or law** that authorises the project — and, where public money or a public service is involved, the **Tribunal de Cuentas** file.
- **The water permits and the OSE (state water utility) position** — especially where a public water source or supply is at stake.
- **The chain of decisions** — the ministerial acts, technical opinions, and any conditions imposed.

## How to Get Them

Environmental documents are held by the **Ministerio de Ambiente**; contracts and audit records by the contracting body and the **Tribunal de Cuentas**; water matters by **OSE**. Use Uruguay's **access-to-public-information law** to obtain what is not volunteered; a refusal is itself a documentable fact. Move early, because a submission entered while the audiencia pública is open, and evidence gathered before the AAP is granted, carry far more weight than the same point raised later — and because the deadlines for an **amparo** and for annulment before the **TCA** are strict. If the threat is national in scale, note too that a **referéndum** must be launched within a limited window after a law is passed, so timing can be decisive. A practical tip: make information requests specific and dated, keep proof of what you asked and when, and treat a refusal not as a dead end but as a documentable fact — and, in a country with a genuine access-to-information law and independent courts, as something you can compel. An impact study the ministry is slow to release, or a contract a body is reluctant to hand over, is often a sign of exactly where the weakness lies.

## The Ground Evidence

- **Dated, geotagged photographs and video** of the site, the water source, the works.
- **Satellite before-and-after** from freely available imagery, to show clearing, plantation expansion, or the true extent.
- **The water evidence** — the public source, the aquifer, the supply and who depends on it, mapped against the footprint; water is the heart of the strongest Uruguayan cases.
- **Independent technical readings** — a hydrologist's, agronomist's, or ecologist's report, and an independent review of the impact study; Uruguay's movements have long partnered with committed scientists, as the water campaigns show.
- **Maps** overlaying the footprint on the water source, the aquifer, the plantations, and the community — a single map showing a project over a public water supply is worth pages of argument.
- **Testimony** — from residents, farmers, water users, and independent experts.

## Organise It So It Persuades

Keep a **master timeline** of the study, the hearing, the AAP, the contract, and every incident and deadline — the spine of any court case, TCA annulment, or direct-democracy campaign. Keep a **document index** so any fact can be sourced in seconds. And keep an **argument bank** (developed in a later section) that pairs each fact with what it proves, to whom. A well-ordered file is not bureaucracy; it is what lets a volunteer, a lawyer, a journalist, and a judge all see the same clear picture fast — and it guards against the campaign's own worst enemy, a claim that cannot be sourced when the other side challenges it. In a fight that can run for years — the water campaign ran four, and its shield still works two decades on — the community with the better-kept file is very often the one that prevails.

# STEP 3: BUILDING LOCAL OPPOSITION — HOW TO ORGANISE A MOVEMENT THAT LASTS

Evidence and law do not stop projects on their own. Organised people do — sustained, visible, and credible over the months or years a fight takes, and, for a direct-democracy campaign, across the whole country. This is the step the CNDAV did best: a broad coalition that held together for four years and rewrote the Constitution.

## Phase 1: The Core

Start with a small, committed core — five to fifteen people who will carry the work. Assign roles: documentation, legal liaison, technical, media, community liaison, and — if a direct-democracy campaign is in view — signature-gathering and national outreach. Agree on the goal — a refused or conditioned AAP, an annulment, a constitutional shield upheld, a law repealed, a project abandoned — and on what you will and will not accept. Uruguay's strength is its civic density: draw early on the unions, cooperatives, and neighbourhood and environmental groups that already exist near you.

## Phase 2: The Coalition

Then widen deliberately. Bring in **neighbouring communities, farmers and family-farmer unions, the trade unions (the PIT-CNT federation has backed environmental fights), students, cooperatives, and scientists**. Reach the **allies with capacity** — Uruguay's environmental organisations (REDES and others), water and public-service coalitions like the CNDAV tradition, public-interest lawyers, and committed academics; and the campaigners who can reach foreign investors and the country's image. The 2004 water victory drew its power from exactly this breadth — unions, ecologists, academics, artists, and ordinary citizens in one coalition — which is precisely the spread a winning campaign needs, and the only spread that can carry a national referéndum.

**A worked coalition example.** Facing a mine threatening a public water source, a local core joins with a family-farmer union, a national environmental organisation, and the water coalition; a hydrologist documents the threat; public-interest lawyers prepare the amparo and the Article 47 argument; and, because the principle is national, the coalition begins to assess a referéndum while pressing the courts. No single group could have carried it; together they cover the science, the law, the constitutional shield, the local roots, and the national reach — the CNDAV model.

## Phase 3: Sustaining It

Campaigns die of burnout, not defeat. Meet on a rhythm. Give people concrete, finishable tasks. Mark small wins — a strong audiencia submission, a document pried loose, a defect exposed, a batch of signatures gathered. Rotate the exhausting roles. And protect people: Uruguay is far safer for defenders than most of the region, but keep leadership collective and visible, document any pressure, and use Escazú's protections where relevant. A national direct-democracy campaign in particular demands stamina — the CNDAV sustained one for four years — so pace the work and celebrate the milestones. Sustaining a campaign is how these fights are won, and the ones that endure pace themselves. Build in the things that keep people together over a long haul — and a national signature drive is a very long haul: welcome newcomers, share the load, mark the milestones, root the campaign in the unions, cooperatives, and neighbourhood groups that give Uruguayan civil society its density, and make the cause something people are proud to belong to. The water coalition sustained a movement for four years and won a constitutional amendment; the movements that last are the ones that become a community in their own right.

## Phase 4: Showing Strength

Numbers and legitimacy must be seen. Turn people out for the audiencia pública and public events. Gather submissions and, where a referéndum is in view, signatures — the concrete, visible act of collecting a tenth or a quarter of the electorate is itself a demonstration of strength that moves politics. Hold peaceful, well-documented actions that make the fight a national story, as the water movement's marches and its permanent presence in front of the Executive Tower did over Neptuno. The point is not spectacle; it is to demonstrate, to the ministry, the courts, the government, the investors, and the public, that the opposition is broad, rooted, and — if it comes to a national vote — a majority. There is a particular power in the signature drive itself: unlike a petition anyone can ignore, the collection of a tenth or a quarter of the entire electorate is a visible, countable demonstration that the movement can command the numbers to win a national vote, and the mere credible prospect of that has reshaped government decisions before a single ballot was cast.

# STEP 4: LEGAL CHALLENGES — THE EIA, THE COURTS, THE SHIELDS, AND DIRECT DEMOCRACY

Uruguayan law gives communities a real arsenal, and you do not need a final ruling to win with it: a submission, an amparo, a TCA annulment, or a signature drive delays a project, forces disclosure, and creates leverage and headlines. Engage lawyers early — but understand the tools yourself.

## The Audiencia Pública and the Environmental Authorisation

The first lever is inside the permit process. The **environmental impact study** and, for high-impact projects, the mandatory **audiencia pública** let anyone raise concerns the proponent and the Ministerio de Ambiente must address; a substantive, evidenced submission builds the record, can win conditions or a refusal of the **AAP**, and lays the groundwork for a court challenge. Treat the hearing not as a formality but as the first phase of the legal case, and put your strongest science — especially on water — on the record while the window is open. The framework comes from Law 16.466 and its regulations: a project on the list of significant activities cannot proceed without an autorización ambiental previa from the Ministerio de Ambiente (the ministry created in 2020 from the former DINAMA), granted only after an environmental impact study and, for high-impact projects, a public hearing at which the study is placed on view and the community may be heard. A study never genuinely opened to the affected community, or a hearing staged after the decision was effectively taken, is both a ground of objection and a basis to challenge the authorisation later.

## The Courts and the Audit Bodies

Uruguay's independent institutions are real remedies. An **acción de amparo** can act quickly against an act that unlawfully harms a right — including the right to water under Article 47. An **acción de nulidad** before the **Tribunal de lo Contencioso Administrativo (TCA)** can annul an unlawful administrative act, such as a defective AAP or an irregular concession. And the **Tribunal de Cuentas** can question a contract or public spending on legal and fiscal grounds — a route that helped stall the Neptuno project. These can be combined: a project can face an amparo, a TCA annulment, and an audit challenge at once. It is worth knowing what each body is for: the ordinary courts hear the amparo, a fast remedy for an act that clearly and unlawfully harms a right; the Tribunal de lo Contencioso Administrativo is a specialised court whose sole job is to annul unlawful administrative acts, though it is slower; and the Tribunal de Cuentas is the constitutional audit body that must pronounce on the legality and fiscal soundness of public contracts. Because Uruguay's institutions are genuinely independent — the country consistently ranks among the least corrupt and most rule-bound in the Americas — a ruling from any of them carries real weight and is generally obeyed.

## The Constitutional Shields — Especially Water

Uruguay's strongest single lever is often a **constitutional shield**, and the strongest of these is **Article 47** on water. It declares access to water and sanitation a fundamental human right, requires that water and sanitation services be provided **exclusively and directly by the State**, mandates **public participation** in water management, and puts **social use before commercial use**. A project that privatises water treatment or supply, endangers a public source, or bypasses public participation crosses that line — and, as the Neptuno case showed, the courts will enforce it. Neptuno was a public-private scheme to build a new drinking-water plant on the Río de la Plata, with a private consortium handling the extraction, treatment, and transport of water for much of the capital — which opponents argued breached Article 47's demand that water be managed "exclusively and directly" by the State, and its guarantee of public participation. Pressed by the water movement in the wake of the severe 2023 drought that had left Montevideo drinking brackish water, a judge upheld the challenge in 2023, the Tribunal de Cuentas questioned the deal, construction was halted, and the project was ultimately cancelled — what its opponents fairly called a popular veto, and a reminder that the 2004 shield is not a museum piece but a live, winning weapon. Where your project touches water, build the Article 47 case first and hardest; where it touches another constitutionally protected value, invoke that shield in the same way.

## Direct Democracy — the People as the Final Check

Uruguay's most distinctive lever is **direct democracy**. Two tools matter. A **referéndum** can **repeal a law**: within a limited window after a law is passed, citizens gathering the support of about a quarter of registered voters can force a national vote to strike it down. A **plebiscito** can **amend the Constitution**: gathering the signatures of about a tenth of the electorate puts a constitutional reform to a national vote — the mechanism the CNDAV used in 2004 to enshrine the human right to water and ban its privatisation. These tools are demanding — they need a national signature drive and campaign, and they suit issues of national scale or principle far more than a single local project — but they are the ultimate lever: they let the people overrule the government and the Parliament directly. Uruguayans have reached for them before and since: a 1992 referendum blocked the privatisation of the state enterprises, the 2004 vote entrenched public water, and campaigns have repeatedly used the mere threat of a referendum to reshape legislation. The thresholds are deliberately high — gathering the support of about a quarter of all registered voters to force a law-repealing referendum, or the signatures of about a tenth to put a constitutional reform to the vote — and the windows are timed, so a referendum against a new law must be launched quickly. But no other lever lets a community, if its cause is national enough, simply take the final decision out of the government's hands. Reserve them for the fight that is big enough, and, when you use them, build the broad coalition that alone can carry them.

## Where to Turn and With Whom

Submissions go to the **Ministerio de Ambiente**; amparos to the ordinary courts; annulments to the **TCA**; audit challenges to the **Tribunal de Cuentas**; rights and defender issues to the **INDDHH** and under **Escazú**; and a referéndum or plebiscito through the Corte Electoral's signature-and-vote process. Get help early: Uruguay's environmental and public-interest organisations, water coalitions, and lawyers know exactly these procedures and can advise on the strongest ground, the strict deadlines, whether the constitutional shield applies, and whether the threat is national enough for direct democracy. Help engaged while the audiencia is open, and before the amparo and referéndum windows close, is worth far more than help sought after the machinery arrives.

# TURNING YOUR EVIDENCE INTO ARGUMENTS

You have a file of evidence. Now you must turn it into arguments — because the same fact persuades a judge, a TCA magistrate, an environment official, a journalist, an investor, a voter, and the community itself in different ways. Learning to translate your evidence for each audience is what lets a community reach every lever at once.

## One Fact, Many Arguments

Take a single documented fact — say, that a project would put a public water source in private hands, on an impact study that understated the risk, after an audiencia pública that answered nothing. That one fact becomes:

- **A constitutional argument:** the project crosses Article 47 — privatising water and denying public participation — the ground on which Neptuno was beaten.
- **An environmental-law argument:** the AAP rests on a deficient study and an inadequate hearing, and can be annulled before the TCA.
- **A rights argument:** the threat to the community's water is a violation that an amparo can halt.
- **A media argument:** a public water source handed to a private consortium, against a Constitution the people voted for — a clear, resonant story.
- **A democratic argument:** if the threat is national, the people who banned water privatisation once can be asked to defend it again — a referéndum in the making.

Same fact, five arguments, five audiences. Go through your strongest facts and, for each, ask what it proves to each audience. That table is your campaign's argument bank — and in Uruguay the argument that most often decides things is the **constitutional one, especially on water**, because it is the shield the courts and the people both enforce. Keep the arguments anchored to the same documented facts, so a claim made to a reporter can be backed in the court file and a claim made to the court can be spoken plainly on a doorstep during a signature drive; consistency across audiences is itself persuasive. Learning to move fluently between these registers — Article 47 for the court, the deficient study for the ministry, the reputational risk for the investor, the clear principle for the voter, the human story for the reporter — is one of the most valuable skills a campaign develops, because it lets the same underlying evidence work every lever at once without ever straying from the facts.

## Match the Argument to the Audience

**The courts and the TCA** want the illegality framed precisely against the specific rule — Article 47, the environmental law, the contracting rules — with the documents and the timeline that prove it.

**The Ministerio de Ambiente** responds to legal risk and its mandate — the deficient study, the inadequate hearing, the water threat, the exposure to annulment.

**Journalists** need a specific, verified, resonant story with a hook: a named water source, a community, a private consortium, a documented defect, a live decision.

**Investors and buyers** respond to legal and reputational exposure — a project on a constitutionally shaky permit, in a country whose clean image they are buying into.

**Voters** — if it comes to direct democracy — respond to a clear principle they can own: water is ours, and public; do not let them take it.

**The community itself** needs to see that the fight is winnable and shared — the small wins, the movement growing, the case advancing — so it lasts.

# STEP 5: MEDIA STRATEGY — HOW PRESS COVERAGE SHIFTS OUTCOMES

Media is not vanity; it is a lever. Coverage raises the political cost of a project, reaches officials and ministers, reaches the foreign investors your letters cannot, and — uniquely in Uruguay — builds the national awareness a referéndum or plebiscito depends on. The water movement proved it: a four-year public conversation turned an IMF letter of intent into a constitutional principle, and, years later, kept Neptuno in the headlines until it died.

## Build the Story

Reporters need specifics: a named place and stake, a dated and documented defect or harm, and a live decision or deadline. "A project threatens the environment" is not a story; "a public water source would be handed to a private consortium, against the Constitution the people voted for in 2004, on a study that hid the risk" is. Lead with your strongest verified fact, attach the map and the imagery, and offer a human voice — a resident, a water worker, a scientist — and a clear principle, as the water movement's "water is a human right" became.

## Reach the Right Outlets

Work outward in rings. **Local and regional media** first, where the project is felt. Then **national outlets** — Montevideo's papers and broadcasters and Uruguay's strong investigative press — which carry political weight and build the national conversation a direct-democracy campaign needs. Then, for a project resting on foreign investment or touching a globally resonant value like water, **international and financial media**, which reach the investors and the world. Feed each ring what it needs, and let coverage in one pull in the next. In Uruguay the **national ring is uniquely important**, because it is the electorate that can become the final decision-maker — so invest in making the story a national one. It is worth identifying the specific environment and investigative reporters at the Montevideo papers and broadcasters, and the international correspondents who follow water, pulp, and foreign investment; a journalist who already knows the terrain needs less briefing and carries more weight, and a single well-sourced investigation into a public water source handed to a private consortium can move a minister or an investor more than a month of statements. Offer your strongest documented finding as an exclusive and you gain an ally, not just a mention.

## Sustain It, Make It Safe and True

A single article changes little; a drumbeat changes decisions — and, for a referéndum, builds a majority. Plan a sequence — the study, the hearing, the AAP, the court case, the audit ruling, the signature drive, the action — so there is always a next hook, and build your own record (a simple site, a social feed, a growing album of dated photographs and video) so the story exists in a form journalists can pick up and that survives if posts are removed. Keep messaging disciplined, accurate, and consistent; one exaggeration handed to the other side can cost you credibility you cannot rebuild. Verified, specific, principled, and relentless — that is the coverage that shifts outcomes, and in Uruguay it is also how a movement talks its way to a national majority.

# EMAILS & LETTERS

Written communications are how you submit, object, create a record, and reach the bodies that can act. Each does double duty: it asks for something and it documents that you asked. Keep copies of everything, and send important items so that receipt is provable. Below are adaptable templates for the situations you will meet most often. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the law and the evidence.

## 8A. Submission at the Audiencia Pública

**To:** the Ministerio de Ambiente
**Subject:** Observaciones — audiencia pública, [project], autorización ambiental previa

As a person/community affected by [project] at [location], I submit the following. My grounds, with evidence: (1) the impact study [understates/omits] [the threat to the water source, the forest, the soil, the cumulative effects]; (2) [the project endangers or privatises a public water source, in tension with Article 47 of the Constitution]; (3) [the audiencia/process was inadequate on [point]]. I request that the AAP be **refused**, or granted only on strict conditions, and that these observations be formally answered. [Attachments: technical report, maps, imagery.]

## 8B. Access-to-Information Request

**To:** [the Ministerio de Ambiente / the contracting body / OSE]
**Subject:** Solicitud de acceso a la información pública — [project]

Under the access-to-public-information law, I request: the environmental impact study and the AAP; the audiencia pública record; the concession or contract for [project]; the water permits and OSE's position; and [correspondence on [issue]]. Please respond within the legal period. I note the public interest in environmental and water information.

## 8C. Instructing Counsel — Amparo / TCA Annulment

**To:** a public-interest or environmental lawyer
**Subject:** [Project] — amparo and/or nulidad before the TCA

We believe the [AAP / concession] for [project], of [date], is unlawful because [it threatens or privatises a public water source contrary to Article 47 / the impact study is deficient / the audiencia was inadequate]. We attach the study, the AAP, the hearing record, and our timeline, and seek your advice on an **acción de amparo** and/or an **acción de nulidad before the TCA**, the strict deadlines, and the strongest ground — above all the Article 47 water argument.

## 8D. Complaint to the Tribunal de Cuentas

**To:** the Tribunal de Cuentas
**Subject:** [Project] — legality and fiscal soundness of the contract

We bring to the Tribunal's attention the contract for [project], which [involves public money / a public service / a public water source]. We are concerned that it [is legally irregular / commits public resources unsoundly / conflicts with Article 47]. We request that the Tribunal examine the contract's legality and fiscal soundness, and we attach the relevant documents.

## 8E. Letter to an Investor / Buyer / Financier

**To:** [the project's investor / buyer / financier]
**Subject:** [Project] — legal and reputational risk

We write regarding your [investment in / purchasing from / financing of] [company] in connection with [project] at [location]. The project [one-line stake — e.g. "would privatise a public water source against Uruguay's Constitution, rests on a legally vulnerable authorisation, and is under legal challenge"], and our documented concerns include [the Article 47 conflict, the deficient study, the pending amparo]. We ask you to weigh the legal, reputational, and financial risk — in a country whose clean, rule-of-law image is part of what you are investing in — and whether your own [environmental and human-rights commitments] are consistent with it. We are glad to share our evidence.

## 8F. Letter to a Legislator / Minister

**To:** [a legislator / the relevant Minister]
**Subject:** [Project] — concerns and request

We are residents of [place] writing about [project]. Our concern, briefly: [one or two specific grounds — the threat to a public water source, a deficient study, a conflict with Article 47]. Within your responsibility, we ask you to [refuse or condition the authorisation / uphold Article 47 and the public management of water / scrutinise the contract]. [If true:] [#] communities and organisations share this concern, and — should it come to that — the public that voted for Article 47 in 2004 will defend it. We welcome a meeting and can share our full evidence.

## 8G. Email to a Reporter (National or International)

**To:** [journalist]
**Subject:** Story tip: [specific finding] — [the water source / the community]

I follow your coverage of [beat]. We have something your readers should know, with a clear news hook: [documents] show [quantified finding — e.g. "a public water source would be handed to a private consortium, against the Constitution Uruguayans voted for in 2004, on a study that hid the risk"], with [a decision/hearing] due [date]. It is specific, verified, and it echoes the water referendum and the Neptuno fight. I can share the full file and connect you with residents and a scientist. Could we talk this week?

## 8H. Public Call for a Referéndum / Plebiscito (national-scale threats)

**To:** allied organisations, unions, and the public
**Subject:** Defending [water / the affected value] — a call to the people

[Project / law] threatens [the national principle — e.g. the public, human right to water enshrined in Article 47]. Where the government and Parliament will not stop it, the Constitution gives the decision to us. We call on organisations and citizens to join a campaign to [gather signatures for a referéndum to repeal the law / a plebiscito to strengthen the constitutional shield], as the CNDAV did in 2004. We will need the signatures of [a quarter / a tenth] of the electorate; here is how to help, and where to sign.

## Using the Templates Well

Send important communications so that receipt is provable, and log every one in your timeline — above all the audiencia, the amparo and TCA deadlines, and, for a law, the short referéndum window. Keep them factual, specific, and grounded in the law and the evidence; attach your documentation and technical reports; and route them, where you can, through your coalition, your lawyers, and your union and international allies. The reply — or the silence — becomes part of your record, and in Uruguay that record feeds the levers that matter most: the audiencia and the AAP, the courts and the Tribunal de Cuentas, the constitutional shields, and — for the fight big enough — the people themselves.

# IF YOU HAVE LITTLE TIME OR FEW RESOURCES: THE RAPID-FIRE VERSION

Not everyone can run a long campaign. With a few hours a week you can still do real damage to a bad project. In rough order of impact for the effort:

**1. Check for the water shield — and any constitutional line — first.** Does the project threaten a public water source or put water in private hands, engaging **Article 47**? That is Uruguay's strongest ground, the one that beat Neptuno. Identify it before anything else.

**2. Make a substantive submission at the audiencia pública.** One evidenced submission to the Ministerio de Ambiente (8A) puts your case on the record, can win conditions or a refusal, and lays the ground for a court challenge — but the window is limited, so act while it is open.

**3. Get the documents.** The impact study, the AAP, the hearing record, and any contract are obtainable through the access-to-information law (8B). The gap between the paper and the reality is usually the case.

**4. Use the courts and the audit body.** An amparo, a TCA annulment, or a complaint to the Tribunal de Cuentas (8C, 8D) can suspend or unravel an unlawful authorisation or contract — as they did to Neptuno.

**5. Reach the investors and the reputation.** A letter to a foreign investor (8E), and national coverage, reach a project resting on investment and Uruguay's clean image.

**6. Ask whether the threat is national.** If the principle is national — like water privatisation — assess a **referéndum or plebiscito** (8H); it is the ultimate lever, though it demands a national campaign.

**7. Tell the story.** A public water source and a clear principle is a powerful story (8G), and in Uruguay national awareness is itself a lever.

Do only the first three and you will have identified your strongest shield, put your case on the record while it counts, and gathered the file a bigger fight needs. Then come back and read the rest. Even the shortest version has a spine: check the water shield, submit at the hearing, get the documents. Everything else in this guide amplifies those three moves. A community that does only them has already done more than most projects' opponents ever manage — it has found its strongest constitutional ground, placed its objection on the record where it counts, and assembled the file that a court challenge, an audit complaint, or a referendum campaign will all draw on.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

Uruguay's system is tilted in specific ways you must understand, so you neither waste effort nor miss the levers that still work.

## Where the Tilt Shows

Uruguay is one of the most law-governed, least corrupt states in the region — but the tilt is real and mostly economic. The countryside is dominated by **agribusiness and forestry**: soy, cattle, rice, and vast **eucalyptus plantations** feeding giant **pulp mills**, and those interests carry great political weight. The pulp mills — built on large foreign investment, sometimes under special agreements with the State — **went ahead despite years of protest**, and monoculture expansion strains the very water the Constitution protects. The pulp sector is the sharpest example: successive governments courted enormous foreign investments in eucalyptus plantations and riverside pulp mills — the first sparked a years-long diplomatic conflict with Argentina over the Uruguay River, and later mills were built under bespoke State agreements — and they went ahead despite sustained environmental protest, because the investment and the jobs were treated as national priorities. It is a candid warning: where the State itself has staked its development strategy on a project, the administrative levers alone rarely stop it, and the fight must lean on the constitutional shields, the courts, the reputation, and — if the principle is national — the people. A **small economy hungry for foreign investment** can find the **State itself becoming the proponent**, championing a project as national development. And the strongest lever, **direct democracy, is slow and high-threshold**: it needs a national signature drive and a majority vote, which suits national principles far better than a single local project, and cannot be summoned quickly. Where this tilt bites, the process gives less than it should — but real levers still reach past it.

## What Still Works — And Some Is Distinctive

The decisive point is that even where the system tilts, real and distinctive levers reach past it.

**The constitutional shields hold.** Article 47's protection of public water is written into the Constitution by the people themselves, and the courts enforce it — as Neptuno's defeat showed, decades after the vote.

**The courts and audit bodies are genuinely independent.** An amparo, a TCA annulment, and a Tribunal de Cuentas review are real remedies that have checked projects, whatever the government wanted.

**Direct democracy is the ultimate backstop.** However slow, the power to repeal a law or amend the Constitution means no government has the last word on a national question — the people do.

**Escazú and the record endure.** Ratified Escazú protections for information, participation, justice, and defenders, and a carefully kept record, power the case over time. Uruguay's ratification of Escazú is not a formality: it strengthens the community's right to the environmental information a project rests on, to genuine participation in the decision, to access the courts to challenge it, and to protection if defending it brings threats — and it gives an extra, treaty-backed argument every time an authority tries to withhold a document or stage a hearing.

**Reputation is a real pressure point.** A State that trades on a clean, rule-of-law image, and the foreign investors who buy into it, both feel scrutiny a local objection cannot reach.

## The Honest Frame

So the honest frame for Uruguay is genuinely favourable by regional standards, but not unlimited. Direct democracy, the constitutional shields, independent courts and audit bodies, Escazú, and a deep civic tradition give a community real — in places unique — power, as the water victory proved. But agribusiness, forestry, and pulp dominate the land and water, the State often backs big investment, and the strongest lever is slow and national in scale. So diagnose the tilt honestly: lean on the water and constitutional shields, the courts, and — for a national threat — direct democracy, not on the goodwill of an investment-hungry State; document every defect; reach the investors and the reputation; and think in terms of delay, conditions, exposure, and a proponent worn down as much as an outright, permanent stop, while remembering that on a national principle the people can still simply overrule the government. Against a clean, investment-backed project that is often what winning looks like — and, where a constitutional shield is engaged, the win can be complete.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO IS CAPTURED

Uruguay ranks among the least corrupt countries anywhere, and outright bribery is rare. But undue influence takes subtler forms — a project waved through for a favoured investor, a study massaged, a contract that quietly serves an interest — and where it appears, your strategy shifts: you stop trying to persuade a captured decision-maker and start exposing and going around them.

## How to Tell

Look for the signatures of undue influence. An **AAP granted against the evidence**, over a plainly deficient study; an **audiencia pública staged** as a formality; a **contract or special agreement** unusually favourable to a foreign investor; a project **fast-tracked as national development** despite a clear conflict with Article 47 or the environmental law; **conditions never enforced**; and technical opinions **overridden without explanation** — each is worth documenting. In a country that keeps good records, the paper trail that reveals a massaged study or an irregular contract is usually obtainable. One anomaly may be a judgment call; a pattern is capture.

## Who Can Be Captured — and Who Is Harder

Be clear-eyed about where undue influence reaches. A **ministry decision, an environmental authorisation, or a national development priority** can be bent toward a powerful investor or agribusiness interest. But Uruguay's system has bodies harder to reach that a captured executive cannot control: the **independent judiciary** and the **TCA**; the **Tribunal de Cuentas**, which questioned Neptuno; the **INDDHH**; the **Corte Electoral**, which administers the direct-democracy tools; a free and vigorous press; and, ultimately, the **electorate itself**, which can overrule any government. Undue influence thrives on staying quiet; your task is to lift the matter into the light — and, on a national question, all the way to the people.

## What to Do

Do not confront and tip off the captured decision-maker. Instead, **document the anomaly precisely** — the massaged study, the staged hearing, the sweetheart contract, the overridden opinion — and take it, with evidence, to the bodies beyond its reach: the courts through an amparo or a TCA annulment, the Tribunal de Cuentas, the INDDHH, and the press. Pair the referral with the constitutional-shield argument and, if the principle is national, the threat of a referéndum, so the wrongdoing and the harm are exposed together. Exposure has its own momentum: a court's or the audit court's attention creates a record other bodies must reckon with, and a massaged study or an irregular contract, once proven, can unravel the authorisation — as the challenges to Neptuno showed. And protect yourself: keep the work collective and documented, use Escazú's defender protections, and never carry it alone.

# INTEGRATION & TIMELINE

The five steps win when they run together. Here is how they fit across the life of a fight.

## Early (Weeks 0–8): Identify, Document, and Submit

Pin down the project, the permit, and the stage (Step 1), and check first for the **water or constitutional shield** (Step 4). Begin the file — get the impact study, the AAP, the hearing record, and any contract through the access-to-information law (Step 2 and What to Gather). And move fast on what will not wait: **make a substantive submission at the audiencia pública**, and, if an unlawful act has occurred, prepare an **amparo** or a **TCA annulment** within its deadline. Draw on Uruguay's dense civil society — the unions, cooperatives, and environmental groups — from the start. This early phase is cheap, and it sets up everything else.

## Middle (Months 2–24): Escalate on Every Front

Grow the movement and coalition (Step 3). Turn your evidence into arguments for each audience (Turning Evidence). Escalate as the facts warrant — the **amparo**, the **TCA annulment**, the **Tribunal de Cuentas** complaint, and the **constitutional-shield** argument, above all on water (Step 4). Refer any capture to the courts, the audit court, and the press. Press the foreign investors and the reputation (8E). Build the media drumbeat from local to national to international (Step 5). And, if the threat is national in scale, begin assessing and organising a **referéndum or plebiscito**. Each front feeds the others. Do the cheap, time-sensitive things first, because a lapsed hearing, amparo, or referéndum window cannot be recovered. And sequence the moves with your lawyers and coalition so they land when they bite hardest — the audiencia submission while the window is open, the amparo or annulment inside its deadline, the Article 47 argument pressed wherever water is at stake, and, if the fight is national, the referendum launched within its timed window after a law is passed — because in Uruguay the difference between a strong case and a lost one is often nothing more than whether the move was made before the door closed.

## Later (Months 6–48+): Sustain, Win the Line, and — if National — Take It to the People

Sustain the movement against burnout; keep the record and the coverage alive. Pursue the durable prizes — a **refused or conditioned AAP**, an **annulled authorisation or contract**, a **constitutional shield upheld**, a **project abandoned** — and, for a national principle, the ultimate prize: a **referéndum won** or a **shield written into the Constitution**, as in 2004. And judge success honestly. Sometimes it is an outright stop — a project killed, as Neptuno was. More often, against investment-backed agribusiness, it is a project **delayed, shrunk, conditioned, or so exposed and legally besieged that it is abandoned**. That is not a lesser victory; it is what winning usually looks like — and where a constitutional shield or the people's vote is engaged, the victory can be total and lasting.

# FINAL ASSESSMENT

Uruguay gives communities a genuinely powerful — and, in places, unique — set of tools, and the honest measure of this guide is how squarely it faces both their strength and their limits.

The strengths are real and distinctive, and by regional standards exceptional. Uruguayans hold true **direct-democracy** tools — the power to **repeal a law** by referéndum and to **amend the Constitution** by plebiscito — the very tools that in 2004 made water a human right and banned its privatisation. The **constitutional shields**, above all **Article 47** on water, are enforced by **genuinely independent courts** and audit bodies, as the defeat of the Neptuno project showed decades later. The **environmental authorisation** and its **audiencia pública** offer a formal objection point; **Escazú** protects information, participation, and defenders; and a **dense civic tradition** of unions, cooperatives, and environmental groups can carry even a national campaign.

The limits are just as real. Agribusiness, forestry, and pulp dominate the land and the water; the pulp mills went ahead despite protest; a small, investment-hungry economy can put the State on the proponent's side; and the strongest lever, direct democracy, is slow and national in scale. No guide should pretend otherwise. The water victory took a four-year national campaign to win; that scale of effort is the price of the strongest tool, not an exception to it.

So the realistic promise is this: **check the water and constitutional shields first, submit substantively at the audiencia pública, use the independent courts and the Tribunal de Cuentas, invoke Article 47 where water is at stake, reach the investors and the reputation, and — for a threat national in scale — organise the referéndum or plebiscito that puts the decision in the people's hands, mobilising the public throughout.** Do that, and even against investment-backed agribusiness you can force delay, conditions, exposure, an annulled authorisation, and a proponent worn down — and, where a shield or the people's vote is engaged, an outright and lasting stop. Do it as the water movement did, building a coalition broad and patient enough to rewrite the Constitution itself, and you can win a victory that protects not only your own water but the whole country's, for good.

A last word on endurance. These fights are won in months and years, and the greatest of them in a national campaign, and the proponent is counting on your exhaustion. Its advantage is money, investment, and patience; yours is legitimacy, a Constitution the people themselves wrote to protect their water, courts that enforce it, and — on a national question — the power to overrule the government by vote. That last advantage is one almost no proponent anywhere has to reckon with, and it changes the whole balance of a fight: a company can outspend and outlast a lawsuit, but it cannot outvote a mobilised people. Play to that. Keep the file, keep the coalition, keep the record and the coverage alive, and treat every delay, every annulment, every audit ruling, every investor that hesitates, and every headline as a brick in a wall that grows too high and too costly for the project to climb.

The tools are here, and Uruguay's own history proves they work: a coalition of ordinary citizens rewrote their Constitution to make water a public human right and ban its privatisation — and that vote, two decades on, still kills the projects that would cross it. What decides the outcome, in the end, is method, endurance, and collective courage — and those you supply. Begin today, begin cheaply, and build from there. Communities before you have stopped what looked unstoppable — a privatisation reversed at the ballot box, a public water source kept in public hands two decades later, a Constitution rewritten by the very people who live under it. The tools are here, and the strongest of them — the constitutional shield on water and the people's power to overrule the government by vote — have already been made to work by ordinary Uruguayans with no more than organisation and endurance; what has ever been missing in the fights that were lost was the will and the coalition to use them, not the law itself. So can yours.

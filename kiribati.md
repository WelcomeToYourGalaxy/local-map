# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN KIRIBATI

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

This guide must begin honestly, because Kiribati is a poor, remote, low-lying atoll nation where the hardest destructive decisions are driven not by greed but by genuine need. Kiribati is a scattered republic of atolls in the central Pacific, home to around 120,000 people, with an ocean territory larger than India and more tuna caught in its waters, year after year, than in those of any other nation on earth — and with fishing licences supplying more than seventy percent of the government's revenue. In that context, its most consequential recent decision was to reverse one of its own greatest conservation achievements: in late 2021 the Cabinet decided to reopen the **Phoenix Islands Protected Area** — some four hundred thousand square kilometres of ocean, an area the size of California, a UNESCO World Heritage site and once one of the largest marine reserves on earth — to commercial fishing, because the fund meant to replace the lost revenue had raised only a fraction of what was promised, and a poor nation, in the end, could not afford to keep so much of its ocean closed to the fishing that sustains it. The historical devastation of **Banaba**, whose phosphate was strip-mined until the island was left barely habitable and its people resettled far away, is a warning of what extraction can do. This guide will not pretend that opposing such decisions is simple, or that the community has prevailed; it has not.

A community in Kiribati defending its ocean and reefs works with real tools, but the honest lesson of the Phoenix Islands is that conservation survives only if it is made to serve the people. The distinctive lever is the **maneaba** — the ancient meeting-house of collective decision-making at the heart of I-Kiribati governance, a community's first and most legitimate forum, giving the whole community a voice. The **elected Maneaba ni Maungatabu**, the national Assembly, must approve major measures and is answerable to the people and the islands; protest, petition, and democratic participation are lawful. **UNESCO's World Heritage status and the international conservation partners** can reward, fund, and — crucially — make protection pay, so a poor nation need not choose between its ocean and its revenue. The **Pacific regional and fisheries bodies** give a small nation collective strength, and major harmful projects must reckon with **environmental assessment**. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The obstacle here is often genuine poverty and genuine sovereignty, not villainy: the government reopened the reserve because a low-income nation depends on fishing revenue and the conservation funding fell short — a real dilemma, not a scandal — and it rightly resists having conservation imposed from outside as a neo-colonial demand. Banaba warns what extraction can do, but also reminds that a poor island's resources are often all it has. So the honest path is never to lecture a struggling nation but to help make protection pay and serve the people — through the maneaba, the democracy, and the partners who can fund and reward the defence of the ocean — and the honest prize is the ocean and reefs defended in a way the people can afford, not a conservation imposed that will not last.

A Kiribati decision runs through custom, democracy, and international partnership. The **maneaba**, the traditional meeting-house, is the community's own forum of collective decision. The **Cabinet and the elected Maneaba ni Maungatabu** decide the licences, the reserves, and the major measures, answerable to the people. The **World Heritage system and the international conservation partners** can fund and reward protection. And the **Pacific regional and fisheries bodies** give a small nation collective strength. Map who decides — and who can make protection pay — before spending a week on it.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

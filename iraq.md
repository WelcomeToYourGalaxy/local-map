# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Here the loss is ancient and vast: the wetlands that cradled the world's first cities are dying, and with them a culture five thousand years old. Iraq is the ancient land between two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates, whose waters formed the **Mesopotamian Marshes** of the south — the largest wetland ecosystem in the Middle East, a vast floodplain of reeds and open water where the **Marsh Arabs** have fished, herded their water buffalo, harvested the reeds, and built their famed arched reed houses since the time of Sumer. Once drained almost to nothing by Saddam Hussein in a deliberate campaign to punish and displace their people, the marshes partly returned after 2003 and were named a **UNESCO World Heritage Site**. But now they are dying again — starved by the **upstream dams in Turkey and Iran** that hold back the two rivers, parched by years of drought, poisoned by rising salt and pollution, and pressed on their edges by **oil exploration** — while in the oil-soaked south the **gas flares** of the oil fields burn day and night over the towns, fouling the air and the health of whole cities and their children.

A community in Iraq works with real, if indirect, levers — and their distinctive truth is that they reach far beyond a fragile state, through the world that has claimed and helped restore this heritage. The **World Heritage status** of the marshes, and the bodies that guard it (UNESCO), can be invoked against harm. The **international oil companies** that draw the oil operate under **their own commitments and reputations**. The **shared rivers** are governed by the **norms of international waters** and by diplomacy with Turkey and Iran. And the **international attention and funders** behind the marshes' protection can be petitioned and held to their commitments. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Iraq **lives on oil**, so the state is bound to the industry that flares the gas and presses the marshes. Much of the **water loss comes from beyond its borders** — the dams in Turkey and Iran that Iraq cannot control. And the state is **fragile and at times dangerous**: though it holds elections and has a real civil society, it is corruption-ridden and marked by armed groups, by the deadly repression of protest, and by the threatening of activists — so the safety of your people comes first. So aim for what these levers can deliver, carefully, and with your people's safety first.

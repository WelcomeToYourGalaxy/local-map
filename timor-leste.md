# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Here the fight has an unusual character, because the great destructive project is bound up with **national pride and the nation's very survival**, and the deepest question is not only whether a project harms a community but whether it is a **wise use of a young nation's dwindling petroleum wealth**. Timor-Leste is Asia's youngest country, a poor but genuine and hard-won democracy born from a long and costly struggle for independence, whose economy rests almost entirely on offshore oil and gas — and whose main producing field **ceased output in 2024**, leaving the sovereign **Petroleum Fund** to be drawn down while the government pursues a vast petroleum-infrastructure scheme along the south coast. That scheme, known as **Tasi Mane**, would build a supply base, an oil refinery, an LNG plant, a highway, and airports across the length of the southern shore, to bring the country's own offshore gas **onshore** for processing on Timorese soil rather than piping it, as before, across the sea to Australia. It promises jobs and industry and national dignity; but it would take **well over a thousand hectares of community farmland, fishing grounds, and salt farms**, has already displaced families in anticipation, and is judged by international economists and the country's own respected independent analysts to be **unlikely ever to recover its costs** — an expensive and doubtful bet on petroleum by a nation that economists have long urged to diversify.

A community in Timor-Leste works with real, strong levers, because it is a genuine democracy — and the distinctive path is not opposition to the nation but the nation's own prudent conscience. The **resource-governance debate** — whether the finite petroleum wealth is wisely spent — is a powerful, patriotic argument, and the country's own respected independent analysts judge the Tasi Mane scheme unlikely ever to recover its costs. **Civil society** is capable and respected. The **institutions** — parliament, the courts — genuinely work, through lawful, open, unifying advocacy. The **church** carries deep moral authority. And **land rights** protect the farmland, fishing grounds, and salt farms the project would take. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. The project is **wrapped in national pride and the promise of prosperity**, the country is **poor and desperate for revenue**, and **opposition can be cast as unpatriotic** — bringing the nation's gas onshore is seen by many as a matter of sovereignty and dignity, so to oppose the megaproject can be painted as opposing the nation's advancement, even as its people are displaced and its wealth spent on works that may never pay. And there is **no landmark victory** to promise. So aim for what these levers can deliver — development made wiser and juster, the finite wealth spent prudently, the land and the displaced protected, a doubtful bet questioned in the open — by claiming the national interest for wise stewardship, not by setting community against nation.

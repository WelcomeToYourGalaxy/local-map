# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN GUINEA-BISSAU

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Guinea-Bissau is a small West African republic of mangrove estuaries, tidal flats, low-lying coast, and the offshore **Bijagós Archipelago** — a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve of some eighty-eight islands where saltwater hippos wade the tidal channels at dusk, where more green turtles nest than almost anywhere else in Africa, and where West African manatees, Atlantic humpback dolphins, and vast flocks of migratory shorebirds gather. And, distinctively, the indigenous **Bijagó people** have, through their sacred forests, their customary law, and their taboos, conserved this treasure for generations — a proven, living community stewardship of a kind found almost nowhere else in this series. Yet the archipelago is pressed hard by foreign fishing, the cashew economy, and the deep poverty of a nation whose economy leans almost entirely on the cashew harvest and the sale of its offshore fishing access, and whose politics have seen more coups than almost any nation since independence. A proven custodianship and genuine conservation partnerships give real ground.

Those who would defend Guinea-Bissau's archipelago work with real tools, resting on that proven custodianship. The distinctive lever is the **Bijagós Biosphere Reserve and the Bijagó custodianship** — the sacred forests, customary law, and community stewardship that have conserved the islands for generations, backed by the international conservation designation. The **offshore fishing that depletes the waters** can be pressed through the reform and transparency of the fishing-access agreements and the anti-illegal-fishing frameworks. The **logging** can be addressed through forest governance and law. The **mangroves and the coast** can be defended through conservation and the traditional rice-farming system that sustains them. Major projects must reckon with **environmental assessment**. And the **conservation, community, and international partners and frameworks** offer real channels a fragile state affords. But these tools have grave limits, and this guide states them plainly. The pressures — the overfishing, the cashew monoculture, the logging — are driven by deep poverty and a fragile economy, so they cannot be met by enforcement alone, and the poor who fish and farm to survive must never be punished. The state itself sells the fishing access that depletes the waters. The governance is chronically unstable and strained by drug-trafficking, which must be kept clear of absolutely and handled with strict neutrality and care. So the work must be careful, custodianship- and conservation-channelled, keeping clear of the trafficking and the political turmoil, protecting everyone involved — and the honest prize is the archipelago defended, the fishing pressed toward reform, the forests and mangroves protected, resting on the proven Bijagó stewardship, not a single triumphant stop.

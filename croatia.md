# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN CROATIA

*Complete Community Opposition How-To*

*This guide is built around the fight for the Ombla spring above Dubrovnik — a hidden cave world and a city's drinking water, saved from the state power company by demanding a proper assessment under Europe's nature rules — and it names the tools you'll meet (the Natura 2000 protections, the environmental-impact assessment, the financier's exit, the citizens' initiative and referendum) in plain words.*

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

At the source of the **Ombla** spring — the very water Dubrovnik drinks — the national utility, **HEP**, planned an underground hydropower plant that would have flooded the **Vilina Cave–Ombla Spring** karst system, home to **sixty-eight cave species, fourteen of them found nowhere else on Earth**, and strained the city's drinking supply. The project rested on a **decade-old environmental study**, and its backers rushed to approve it before harder questions could be asked. Croatians asked them anyway. Environmental groups — **Zelena akcija**, the Croatian Friends of the Earth — together with a bank-watch network and the local **Srđ je naš** citizens' initiative, challenged the outdated assessment and demanded a proper one under the European rules Croatia was joining. When that assessment showed the lasting damage to a **Natura 2000** protected site, the project's foundations gave way: in 2013 its main financier, a European development bank, **cancelled its €123 million loan**, and in 2015 Croatia's own **environment ministry refused the impact assessment**, ruling that the harm to the protected network could not be excluded even with mitigation. The plant was abandoned. Sixty-eight cave species, and a city's water, were saved.

Zelena akcija and the Srđ je naš initiative drew on tools available to communities across Croatia, sharpened by the European rules it joined in 2013. The **Natura 2000 network** protects Europe's most important habitats, and harm to it can sink a project, as it sank the Ombla plant. A proper **environmental-impact assessment**, done under EU rules, forces a project's true harm into the open — demanding a real one, not a decade-old study, is a decisive move. The **financier's exit** is a sharp lever: a European development bank cancelled its €123 million Ombla loan when the harm became clear. The **local referendum** carries it — Srđ je naš won 84 percent against a golf-and-villa resort on the plateau above Dubrovnik; protest, petition, and litigation are lawful and safe. And EU-standard **participation, access-to-information, and appeal rights** back it all. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. EU rules are **powerful but slow and technical**, so you need expertise and endurance. Developers **rush to approve projects on outdated studies** before the harder questions land. And the pressure to build energy, tourism, and coastal real estate is **strong on Croatia's beautiful and contested coast**. So aim for what these levers can deliver by forcing the truth into the open and using every European and democratic tool.

A hydropower plant or a coastal resort looks unstoppable when a state utility or big money is behind it, but it depends on approvals, assessments, and financing with weak points — and in the EU, a proper assessment and a Natura 2000 protection are chains that hold. # HOW THE SYSTEM WORKS

To break the chain you have to see it — who decides, how a project moves, and where its weak points are.

## Who Actually Decides

Big projects involve a **state utility** like HEP or private developers, and the **government** and its **environment ministry**, which grants or refuses the impact assessment. But Croatia is in the **European Union**, so the **Natura 2000 network** protects key habitats, a **proper environmental-impact assessment** under EU rules is required, and EU-standard **participation and appeal** rights apply. **Financiers** — often European development banks — can be made to withdraw. And **municipalities**, **citizens' initiatives**, and **local referendums** hold real power. Know which of these lie between the project and the ground.

## How a Project Moves — and Where You Jump In

**The assessment.** A project needs an approved environmental-impact assessment. *Your opening — the decisive one:* demand a proper, honest one under EU rules, not an outdated study.

**The Natura 2000 test.** *Your opening:* the harm to a protected site, which can sink a project.

**The financing.** *Your opening:* a European financier that can be made to withdraw, as one withdrew from Ombla.

**The local decision.** *Your opening:* a citizens' initiative and a referendum, as at Srđ.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

Being against "the project" is weak. Being specific — this assessment, this Natura 2000 site, this financier — is strong. Answer five questions in writing.

**1. What is it, and what does it need to proceed?** Name the project (a hydropower plant, a coastal resort), the utility or developer, and what it needs: an approved impact assessment, permits, financing — each a stage you can contest.

**2. What would it harm — and is it Natura 2000?** The **water, the karst, the habitat, the coast** — and, crucially, whether it harms a **Natura 2000** site, which can sink it, as the Vilina Cave harm sank Ombla.

**3. What does its assessment rest on?** Whether it rests on an **outdated or dishonest study** — as Ombla rested on a decade-old one — because **demanding a proper EIA under EU rules is your decisive move**.

**4. Who finances it?** Whether a **European development bank or financier** backs it, which can be made to withdraw, as one withdrew €123 million from Ombla.

**5. Is there a local vote to win?** Whether a **citizens' initiative and referendum** can decide it, as Srđ je naš won 84 percent against the resort.

**What this looks like in real life.** A state utility plans a plant that would flood a protected cave system and strain a city's water, on a decade-old study. The five questions yield: the plant and its need for an assessment, permits, and financing (the stages to contest); the harm to a Natura 2000 site (the sinking point); the outdated study (demand a real one); the European financier (make it withdraw); and, for a coastal scheme, a referendum to win. That's the Ombla pattern — force a proper assessment, prove the Natura 2000 harm, and the foundations give way.

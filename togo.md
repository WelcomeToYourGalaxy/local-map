# HOW COMMUNITIES CAN STOP DESTRUCTIVE DEVELOPMENT PROJECTS IN TOGO

# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Community action shapes what happens to the coast, land, and water of Togo — and here the threat has an unusual character, because some of the most destructive harms are not single projects but slow disasters set in motion by infrastructure elsewhere. Togo is a narrow West African coastal state, ruled by one family since 1967, where the sea is devouring the shore at five to ten metres a year: the former capital, **Aného**, dozens of fishing villages, and the coastal road are projected to vanish by 2038 if nothing changes; the coast road has already been moved inland three separate times; and six fishing villages have simply disappeared beneath the waves for good. This erosion is driven not by nature alone but by human works — the great dam far upstream in Ghana that traps the sand the coast needs, and the ports of Lomé and Cotonou whose long structures block the drift of sediment along the shore that the coast depends on to renew itself. Alongside this, a state-run phosphate plant on the coast at **Kpémé** discharges its industrial waste directly into the sea, and poverty pushes fishermen to mine the very sand that protects them. Togo is a hard place to oppose the powerful, protest has been repressed, and no community has won a landmark victory. But the levers, though constrained, are real.

In a semi-authoritarian state whose harms are often transboundary and infrastructural, a community has more than one arena, and the tools, though constrained, are real. The distinctive lever is the **regional ECOWAS Court of Justice**, which citizens can petition directly and which has ruled against the Togolese government — a regional legal arena the government cannot control. At home, a **constrained but real domestic space of civil society, press, and courts** offers genuine, if limited, room to organise, document, and litigate. Because the erosion's cause lies partly in works in another country and in regional ports, the **transboundary and regional frameworks** reach across borders to the drivers of the harm. The **international and regional coastal programmes** — the World Bank, UN, and regional coastal and climate funds — pay for the protection and bring standards with them. And the phosphate plant's pollution and any coastal project must reckon with **environmental assessment and the pollution and marine law**. These tools are general — the regional court, the domestic space, the transboundary frameworks, the coastal funders, and the assessment reach the country's various harms, from the eroding coast to the polluting plant. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Togo is ruled by a long-entrenched family, and protest and opposition have been met with repression, so open confrontation carries risk and the work must be careful. The coastal erosion is a slow disaster, driven partly by works in another country and by decades of development, and cannot simply be switched off. The phosphate that pollutes is a mainstay of the economy, so the state is slow to curb it. And no community has won a landmark victory. So favour the funded, legal, regional, and careful arenas over confrontation — they both work and carry less risk — and aim for what these tools can genuinely deliver: the coastal protection funded and the standards applied, the transboundary cause pressed, the phosphate pollution assessed and challenged, a case carried to the ECOWAS Court — protecting those who organise and speak, especially inside the country.

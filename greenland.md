# INTRODUCTION & FRAMEWORK

Just eight kilometres from **Narsaq** — a close-knit community of about a thousand people who live by fishing, sheep farming, and the harvest of the land and sea — lay **Kuannersuit**, known in Danish as **Kvanefjeld**: one of the world's greatest deposits of rare-earth metals, the elements that drive electric motors, wind turbines, and weapons. But the ore was laced with **uranium and thorium**, and to mine it meant risking radioactive dust and waste in a fragile Arctic where nothing washes clean. The Australian company that held it, backed by a Chinese partner, had sunk more than a hundred million dollars into it and was, under the government of the day, on track for final approval. The people of Narsaq had feared it for years — "we rely on the land and the sea," as one put it, and "uranium dust would destroy everything we depend on." So they made their fear the country's decision. When Greenland went to the polls in **April 2021**, the mine was the question on the ballot in all but name, and the party that promised to stop it, **Inuit Ataqatigiit**, won. That **November, the parliament passed a law banning uranium mining** — and because the law barred mining any deposit above a hundred parts per million, while Kvanefjeld's ore ran at roughly three hundred, it did not merely pause the project; it **closed it**. A town of a thousand, using a nation's democracy, had stopped a mine the whole world wanted.

The people of Narsaq made their fear the country's decision — and the distinctive lever they used is self-government at an intimate scale. Greenland's own **parliament (the Inatsisartut)** and **government (the Naalakkersuisut)** control its mineral resources, and can be shaped by an election a single project can decide, as the April 2021 vote decided Kvanefjeld. The **consultation and environmental process** governs a project. The **courts** are open. The **land-and-sea livelihoods** that bind Greenlanders to their environment are a powerful, unifying stake. And a community's voice, at this intimate scale — a town of a thousand shaping a national election — can move a nation; protest, petition, and democratic mobilisation are the tools. But these tools have real limits, and this guide states them plainly. Greenland depends heavily on an annual **block grant from Denmark**, and many Greenlanders see mining revenue as the path to the **economic independence** they long for — so there is a powerful, standing pressure *for* large projects, and the anti-uranium victory ran against that current, not with it (only eight years earlier, the same parliament had lifted a decades-old ban on mining radioactive materials to court that revenue). The company did not simply walk away: it pursued **compensation through international arbitration**, a claim reported in the billions. And Greenland's rare earths sit at the centre of a **great-power contest** — China, the United States, and Europe all covet them — so a local decision can draw pressures far larger than the fjord it concerns. So aim for what this lever can deliver, reckoning honestly with the money, the independence dream, and the geopolitics it must face.

# STEP 1: TARGET IDENTIFICATION

**1. What is it, and who's behind it?** Name the project, the foreign company and its partners, and whether an investor-state claim could loom.

**2. What does it risk?** The **radioactive dust, the pollution, the fragile Arctic, and the land-and-sea livelihoods** — **the livelihood stake is your sharpest, most unifying lever**, as at Narsaq.

**3. Can it be made the country's decision?** Whether, at Greenland's intimate scale, the project can become **the ballot question**, so a government that would stop it wins — the decisive Kvanefjeld move.

**4. What stage is it at, and what's the opening?** Is the consultation or environmental process open? Is an election coming (make it the question)? Can the Inatsisartut change the law? These are the openings.

**5. Who's your coalition, and what current runs for it?** The **affected community, the wider public, and international allies** — and an honest read of the **economic-independence and great-power current** you may be going against.

**What this looks like in real life.** A world-coveted mine, laced with radioactive material, threatens a small town's land and sea, on track for approval. **Layer two: the process and the paper trail.** Get the **consultation record, the environmental assessment, and the approvals**, and read them for the flaws and the understated risks.

**Layer three: the political map.** Map the **electoral and legislative opening** — whether the project can be made the ballot question and the law changed — and the **current** that runs for it (independence, money, geopolitics). Walking into the consultation, the election, the Inatsisartut, or the courts with the documented risk and that political map is what makes them act.

# WHEN THE SYSTEM IS TILTED

**When the independence-and-money current runs for the project.** Expect it — mining revenue is seen as the path to freedom from the Danish grant. **When the company pursues arbitration.** The company may pursue a billion-dollar investor-state claim against the government. That falls on the government, not the community; don't let the threat deter the democratic fight, and support the government in resisting it.

# WHEN IT'S ACTUAL CORRUPTION — AND WHO'S CAPTURED

Greenland is a functioning democracy, but the closeness of foreign mining money to a small political system, and the pull of the independence dream, create real risks of capture, so this matters.

**How to spot it.** Approvals against the environmental evidence; a process shaped for a favoured investor; the independence dream used to wave through harm; benefits to the connected while communities' land and sea bear the cost.

**Who's supposed to guard against it.** Greenland's audit and oversight bodies examine public money and conduct. The **Inatsisartut** and the **courts** can change the law and quash a flawed process. The **electorate** can turn out a captured government, as in 2021. And Greenland's **communities, press, and international allies** can expose a bad deal.

**How to fight it safely.** Document precisely and factually — how a process was shaped, who benefits, what the environmental evidence shows — and keep clear the line between what you can prove and what you suspect. Route serious evidence to the audit bodies, the courts, and trusted journalists rather than making accusations you can't back, and, above all, take it to the electorate. Pair the exposure with the democratic and legislative fight.
